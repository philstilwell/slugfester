From debate to scorecard

The standard one-on-one workflow · September 2026. Earlier scorecards may use different controls. Select a panel to enlarge it.

Download PDF (3.6 MB) Skip to the rubric quality check
Prefer text? Read the complete guide

Controlled inputs, not perfect objectivity. Separate artificial-intelligence (AI) reviews reduce avoidable influence, but shared model biases and mistaken judgments can remain. Selection is curated, not a representative sample of all debates.

  1. Define the debate

    Check for duplicates and identify the recording, speakers, event year, and a neutral central question. Record what each side must establish: its burden. A critic need not prove an alternative unless that burden is adopted. A duplicate recording is not silently reassessed.

  2. Secure the complete source

    Check transcript coverage, material gaps, and speaker changes against the full substantive debate. Use complete captions; transcribe if needed. A complete automatic caption track may be preferable to an incomplete human-written one. Preserve timestamps, acquisition information, and digital fingerprints that reveal later changes. Freeze the evidence, assessment rules, model settings, permitted repairs, and cost limits before judgment.

  3. Map the arguments, then lock the evidence

    Identify important claims, replies, concessions, and unanswered objections. Tie each selected move—a substantive argumentative contribution—to an exact source passage, speaker, topic, and relevant burden or reply. Section weights total 100%; move importance is 1, 2, or 3. Freeze both before scoring. Explain unequal coverage; never invent symmetry or drop arguments merely to fit the display.

  4. Two isolated AI reviews

    Both review the same locked evidence and rate every selected move, with reasons. Neither sees the other’s judgments, previous scorecards, published commentary, winner labels, or AI-added arguments. “Independent” means separate AI contexts with controlled inputs, not unrelated model families or infallible human experts.

  5. Resolve differences

    Check audio whenever speaker attribution is below high confidence. Fixed rules flag dimension differences greater than 8 points, move-score differences greater than 4 points, adjustment differences greater than 2 points, and relevant categorical differences. A fresh adjudicator reviews the disputed evidence and anonymous options, without reviewers’ identities or calculated overall totals. Use the selected option for disputed ratings and rounded means for undisputed numerical ratings. Preserve both original reviews, the resolutions, and audio evidence, then freeze the resolved record.

  6. Calculate the scores once

    Six fixed dimensions determine each move’s score:

    • 25% logical coherence: Does the conclusion follow from the premises?
    • 20% evidence and warrant: Are the evidence and its connection to the conclusion adequate?
    • 20% responsiveness: Does the move answer the actual live challenge?
    • 15% relevance and burden: Does it advance or challenge what must be established?
    • 10% precision and clarity: Are terms, scope, and qualifications clear?
    • 10% calibration and charity: Is confidence proportionate, and is the opposing argument represented fairly?

    Move: round the weighted sum of the six ratings. Section, for each side: multiply each move score by its previously locked importance, add these results, divide by the total importance, and round. Overall, for each side: combine section scores using their prelocked percentage weights, add the burden-completion adjustment, then round and keep within 0–100.

    The default final adjustment is zero. An integer from −5 to +5 is allowed only for consequential full-debate burden completion not already counted in the moves. It cannot reward style or penalize the same defect twice. Models supply judgments; software calculates totals. No person hand-adjusts the result.

    Two separate worked examples—not actual debate results: moves scoring 80 and 70 with importance 2 and 1 produce (80 × 2 + 70 × 1) ÷ 3 = 76.67, rounded to 77. Sections scoring 80 and 70 with weights 60% and 40%, plus a +2 adjustment, produce 48 + 28 + 2 = 78. The first example’s 77 is not an input to the second.

    Stability checks: compare the final result with both original passes. Mean absolute distance is limited to 4 points, maximum distance from either pass to 8, and excursion beyond their range to 3. If both initial integer-rounded results favor the same side, the final result may favor that side or tie, but cannot reverse the winner. A failed check blocks acceptance; it is not permission to tune scores.

  7. Explain the locked result

    Publish every selected move exactly once. Quotations must match the eligible source wording and timestamps must lead to the relevant passage. Each critique explains the strongest feature, principal limitation, remaining burden, and locked score. Overall commentary identifies concrete strengths and material blunders for both sides. Explanations cannot change the selected moves, resolved judgments, or calculated totals.

  8. Review fallacies and cognitive biases separately

    After scores and critiques are locked, two fresh blind reviews examine every selected move against the local LogFall and CogBias definitions. Neither sees existing labels or the other review. A fresh adjudicator decides every candidate raised by either reviewer, including candidates raised by only one.

    A label must identify a material defect supported by the complete source and already reflected in the critique. A contested premise is not automatically circular reasoning; a weak analogy is not automatically equivocation; citing an expert is not automatically an appeal-to-authority fallacy. Distinct labels require distinct failures. When uncertain, omit the label.

    Labels never add another numerical penalty. Finding no applicable labels is valid. Comparisons with earlier debates check consistency, not a target number of tags. Accepted labels link to local definitions and debate-specific explanations, with external LogFall or CogBias links for further reading.

  9. Keep AI contributions separate

    Clearly label strengthened arguments and genuinely new ideas for both sides as AI contributions. They are not transcript statements, are not attributed to the speakers, and never affect participant scores. Check their source-specific reasoning, added value, and repetitive wording. Biographical research also stays outside judgment packets and score calculations.

  10. Verify before publication

    Check source fingerprints, the score calculation chain, exact quotations, move coverage, label decisions, and the audit record. Compare explanation depth and specificity with 25 earlier debates without targeting their scores, argument counts, or tag rates. Test desktop and mobile pages, keyboard access, expandable sections, overflow, and links. Preserve rejected outputs and allow only bounded repairs that cannot rewrite locked scoring substance. Required checks must pass before publication, and the published result is then verified.

Team debates and scope limits

The ordinary workflow assesses one substantive advocate per side. A narrowly defined exception permits incidental host advocacy only when the recording is organized around two primary opponents, non-primary advocacy occupies at most 5% of the assessed window and adds no unique load-bearing argument, and excluded interventions and directly dependent replies are frozen and disclosed before judgment. Neither primary side receives credit or penalty for excluded material.

Panels and roundtables are not silently forced into that route. Authorized, stable two-team debates use a separate, disclosed approximation method. The team is the scoring unit: no individual score is inferred. Moves and labels retain their actual speaker attribution. Team and panel scores are excluded from individual rankings, profile averages, score distributions, and opponent records. Team checks include audio verification and tests of whether the leading side changes when contributions are rebalanced or one teammate is removed.

What scores mean: an estimate of argumentative performance in this debate, not the truth of a worldview, a speaker’s overall expertise, or a person’s worth. Fame, applause, accent, and speaking polish earn no automatic bonus. A true claim can be defended badly, and a false claim argued carefully. These safeguards improve consistency and traceability, not perfect objectivity.

What this snapshot measures

Positions, not personal identities

Supporting and challenging refer to the religious claim at issue in each debate. They are assigned from the published positions, not from the stored PRO/CON columns or a speaker’s personal beliefs. Challenging a claim need not assert that no God exists. A creator-only argument does not establish a personal, intervening God.

Descriptive argument families

Families use explicit word and phrase rules applied to the assessed move and its identifier, with the section title as a fallback. A move can appear in several families; percentages therefore need not add to 100. These are browsing labels, not a fresh philosophical assessment. “Other / general” retains unmatched material.

Each debate gets equal weight

Score charts first average the unique assessed moves within each debate, position, family, and selected role. The score-range chart shows the median and middle half of those debate averages, not confidence intervals. The dimension grid averages each debate’s share below the selected score threshold (70 by default). Repeated speakers can still influence the sample; these figures do not establish statistical significance.

Saved assessments, limited sample

The snapshot includes 226 of 300 published debates available when it was built. It describes selected assessed moves, not every utterance or all public debates. Unclear position pairs, team approximations, and legacy panels without compatible argument-level records are excluded. Small samples are marked below five debates. Scores assess the reasoning presented, not whether God exists.

Classifications and exclusions

No cases awaiting review. All 74 exclusions have recorded decisions.

Constructive and reply groups can contain different debates. Their comparison does not measure an argument’s change after rebuttal. Concessions remain in the other charts but are omitted from the case/reply comparison.

Position assignments use the corrected September 1 research taxonomy where available, plus explicit reviews of previously omitted panels and later debates. Legacy grouped scores without compatible argument-level records remain excluded. Scope follows the main debate topic, with explicit editorial corrections. Individual arguments can address related questions within that debate.

Calibration concerns whether the strength of a claim matches its support; charity concerns fair representation of an opponent. Relevance and burden concerns contact with the question a side needs to establish.
