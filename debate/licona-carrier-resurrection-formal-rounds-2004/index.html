Overall assessment

Mike Licona — Historical resurrection defense: 82/100

Published strengths

  • The crucifixion case combines attestation, ordinary lethality, medical judgment, and survivor implausibility into the debate’s clearest and least controversial historical premise.
  • Licona anchors bodily continuity in direct Pauline grammar, Romans, and Philippians rather than relying only on later gospel physicality.
  • His synthesis connects death, tomb, appearances, adverse witnesses, and movement formation within one explanatory proposal that directly answers the motion.

Published weaknesses

  • The best-explanation claim declares natural theories failures before comparably measuring their probabilities, independence assumptions, and combined explanatory reach.
  • Religious significance risks circularly increasing the miracle prior unless divine intention is independently established rather than inferred from the disputed event.
  • Closing replies overstate Carrier’s account as synchronized identical hallucination and underaddress source dependence, selective disclosure, and ordinary literary development.

Richard Carrier — Naturalistic historical challenge: 81/100

Published strengths

  • Carrier presses the earliest-source question through Paul’s omissions, embodiment contrasts, vocabulary, grammar, and relevant Jewish conceptual parallels here.
  • His empty-tomb critique distinguishes source quantity from independence, quality, historical necessity, and evidential demands appropriate to a remarkable claim.
  • The naturalistic synthesis links visionary mechanisms, chronological elaboration, and selective disclosure into a comparative challenge rather than merely denying resurrection.

Published weaknesses

  • The opening narrates a detailed sectarian trajectory without direct evidence securing each transition from grief experiences to canonical physical stories.
  • Explanations for Paul invoke guilt, attraction, stress, and fatigue speculatively, making the mechanism adaptable where individual evidence is weakest.
  • Literary symbolism and later detail are treated too readily as invention, although theological shaping can coexist with remembered historical claims.

Historical explananda and resurrection inference

Mike Licona — section score 83/100

Strongest feature: Licona constructs a cumulative case: Christian and non-Christian attestation, Josephus’s lethal survival example, medical judgment, Strauss’s survivor objection, Crossan’s confidence, and Carrier’s own better-than-98-percent death estimate converge on the indispensable premise that crucifixion killed Jesus. Principal limitation: Although he names sources and authorities, he does not establish their dating, independence, or case-specific basis, so convergence remains summarized rather than demonstrated. Live burden: Because Carrier concedes that Jesus probably died, evaluators should distinguish this premise’s strong dialectical success from the separate question whether it materially discriminates resurrection from Carrier’s vision-and-body-exchange account. Locked score: The fixed 90 appropriately rewards a clear, multiply supported, opponent-conceded foundation while withholding exceptional credit because several attestational and medical warrants are asserted at survey level.

Paul and the nature of the resurrection body

Empty tomb, sources, and literary development

13:45–1:20:17

Mike Licona — section score 78/100

Licona argues that Jerusalem proclamation, hostile theft explanations, and bodily-resurrection language independently converge on the conclusion that the women probably discovered Jesus’s tomb empty after his burial.

Strongest feature: Licona offers a cumulative inference: proclamation where authorities could expose a body, hostile theft explanations presupposing a missing body, and bodily-resurrection language point toward the same conclusion. Principal limitation: Treating those considerations as independent remains contestable: Carrier argues that the theft report derives from Matthew and that resurrection could mean a replacement body, while Licona answers with tradition and Pauline continuity. Live burden: The burden turns on whether hostile polemic is independent, Jerusalem opponents could realistically and publicly identify and produce the body, and Pauline language entails continuity with the buried corpse. Locked score: The fixed 79 rewards a load-bearing three-part case while withholding stronger credit because its convergence depends on contested independence and bodily continuity.

Richard Carrier — section score 81/100

Carrier argues that late, interested, anonymous empty-tomb reports lack the independent evidence available for Caesar’s Rubicon crossing, so historians should not regard the tomb as established.

Strongest feature: Carrier systematically compares historical necessity, physical traces, hostile corroboration, critical source identification, and eyewitness authorship, making his evidential-insufficiency conclusion cumulative and responsive to Licona’s earlier empty-tomb case. Principal limitation: The inference treats evidence expected for a public military invasion as a benchmark for a burial claim, while Licona later answers that expectations differ by event and challenges the comparative sources’ quality and chronology. Live burden: Evaluators must decide whether Carrier’s five criteria appropriately scale to the event alleged, and whether Licona’s appeals to early, multiple, adverse, and embarrassing testimony supply independent support. Locked score: The fixed score of 85 rewards a source-specific comparative framework and strong engagement with a central premise, while reflecting the unresolved fit between the Rubicon benchmark and the empty-tomb claim.
