Overall assessment

Richard Swinburne — Christianity adequately explains suffering: 78/100

Published strengths

  • Distinguished logical compatibility from physical limitation and built a cumulative account around consequential responsibility, character formation, sanctity, and subject-centered value.
  • Preserved significant qualifications: victim benefit conditions divine permission, usefulness is neither necessarily overwhelming nor his sole reason, and redemption promises no immediate relief.
  • Answered the heaven objection with a temporally ordered account of formative earthly freedom followed by settled heavenly character, while endorsing practical relief.

Published weaknesses

  • Relied on speculative victim preference and underdefended analogies to move from valuable responsibility to permission of nonconsenting victims’ extreme suffering.
  • Repeatedly identified possible goods without demonstrating their necessity, proportionality, or sufficient benefit for victims in famine, disaster, and atrocity.

Bart Ehrman — Christian explanations of suffering are inadequate: 85/100

Published strengths

  • Tested Swinburne’s own benefit condition against natural evil and nonconsenting victims while conceding the genuine relevance of free will to human-caused suffering.
  • Used heaven as an internal counterexample and sharpened biblical diversity into a specific conflict over causal responsibility and unfulfilled imminent expectations.
  • Closed constructively by distinguishing explanatory uncertainty from people’s continuing ability and obligation to reduce concrete suffering.

Published weaknesses

  • Sometimes moved too quickly from persistent suffering to broad claims that prayer does not matter or that intervention is absent, without comparative evidence.
  • Compressed several historical and authorial claims into professional judgment, leaving the textual basis less developed than the philosophical victim-centered objections.

Coherence and responsible agency

25:05–36:53

Richard Swinburne — section score 81/100

Swinburne limits omnipotence to logically possible acts, contrasts logical with physical constraints, and argues that certain valuable states require allowing suffering.

Strongest feature: Swinburne precisely narrows omnipotence to logically possible action, uses the surgeon comparison to distinguish logical from physical limitation, and thereby answers Ehrman’s initial appearance of outright contradiction. Principal limitation: The compatibility result depends on the further, only introduced proposition that relevant goods cannot exist without permitted suffering, so it does not yet establish that actual suffering is necessary, proportionate, or explanatorily adequate. Live burden: His side must identify those goods, defend their logical dependence on suffering, and connect possibility-level reconciliation to the victim-centered moral conditions developed later. Locked score: 83/100 reflects unusually clear modal reasoning and direct engagement, moderated by an evidentiary bridge that remains asserted rather than demonstrated at this stage.

Bart Ehrman — section score 86/100

Ehrman frames the tension among divine power, divine love, and suffering, requiring either a rejected premise or an adequate extenuating circumstance.

Strongest feature: Ehrman states the three propositions cleanly, distinguishes denying one from supplying an extenuating circumstance, and makes explanatory adequacy rather than mere doctrinal repetition the debate’s central burden. Principal limitation: The opening establishes a prima facie tension rather than a contradiction under every defensible account of omnipotence or love, and it does not yet compare particular Christian explanations. Live burden: His side must show that Swinburne’s proposed circumstances fail on their own stated conditions without treating the initial triad as a completed deductive disproof. Locked score: 85/100 rewards the motion-level framing, conceptual clarity, and calibrated appearance language, while reserving judgment on whether the subsequent victim, natural-evil, and scriptural challenges defeat the offered reconciliation across the full range of suffering presented in the exchange.

Natural evil and victim benefit

36:52–51:06

Richard Swinburne — section score 72/100

Swinburne argues natural suffering creates otherwise unavailable choices for sufferers and observers, whose repeated responses form character and advance possible holiness.

Strongest feature: Swinburne supplies an intelligible mechanism from natural suffering through serious response choices to character formation, extending his agency account beyond harms directly caused by human wrongdoing. Principal limitation: Showing that suffering creates opportunities does not establish that suffering at the observed severity is needed, proportionate, or beneficial to each victim under his earlier authorization condition. Live burden: He must connect character-forming opportunity to the justification of particular sufferers’ costs, explain why less destructive circumstances would not suffice, and avoid treating observers’ development as automatically compensating victims. Locked score: 76/100 credits a relevant, coherent natural-evil proposal and clear causal sequence, while substantially discounting its asserted counterfactual necessity and unresolved victim-level moral adequacy.

Bart Ehrman — section score 87/100

Ehrman invokes Christian belief in freely inhabited heaven without suffering to challenge any universal logical dependence between freedom and suffering.

Strongest feature: Ehrman offers an internal Christian counterexample with sharply defined modal force: freedom and an absence of suffering are reportedly co-instantiated in heaven, challenging a universal necessity claim. Principal limitation: The argument does not establish that Swinburne accepts heavenly freedom as developmentally independent of earthly formation, leaving open the later distinction between formative choice and settled good character. Live burden: The counterexample tests the bare claim that freedom requires suffering; Swinburne bears the burden of showing why prior earthly formation is necessary, and Ehrman’s lack of a turn after that closing answer incurs no penalty. Locked score: 86/100 rewards the clear, responsive, and powerful counterexample, tempered because its ultimate force depends on the relevant conception of heavenly freedom and the merits of Swinburne’s temporally ordered reply.

Biblical diversity and conflict

53:22–1:03:55

Richard Swinburne — section score 80/100

Swinburne accepts biblical diversity but assigns different explanations to different evils, adding character formation in Hebrews and disclosed goodness in John.

Strongest feature: Swinburne concedes the textual plurality, offers a coherent complementary-purpose interpretation, and supplies specific Hebrews and John examples instead of denying the diversity Ehrman documents. Principal limitation: Different explanations can coexist, but allocating them to different evils does not resolve cases where authors appear to assign opposed causal responsibility to suffering, and the textual interpretations remain compressed. Live burden: He must demonstrate that the accounts concern compatible levels, events, or purposes rather than merely asserting multiple good reasons under a later church synthesis. Locked score: 81/100 rewards direct engagement, concrete scriptural examples, and charitable acceptance of plurality, moderated because compatibility is clearer than authorial agreement or independent support for the proposed harmonization.

Bart Ehrman — section score 85/100

Ehrman surveys punishment, wrongdoing, redemption, hostile powers, mystery, and Ecclesiastes, arguing scripture offers diverse rather than straightforwardly unified explanations.

Strongest feature: Ehrman presents a wide, differentiated taxonomy of biblical responses, identifies their contrasting causes and purposes, and carefully reserves the stronger inconsistency claim for a more specific later comparison. Principal limitation: The survey compresses textual detail and representativeness, while diversity alone does not show that several explanations cannot be complementary or apply to different kinds of suffering. Live burden: He must identify genuine conflicts rather than rely on plurality, distinguish his personal affinity with Ecclesiastes from proof, and answer Swinburne’s different-evils interpretation. Locked score: 85/100 reflects strong evidence within the debate, clear organization, high relevance, and good calibration, with a reservation for abbreviated exegesis and the limited inference available from taxonomy by itself without closer analysis of the authors’ historical settings and intended claims.

Incarnation, intervention, and kingdom

58:23–1:11:19

Richard Swinburne — section score 78/100

Swinburne separates incarnation as sharing permitted danger from redemption as reparation through Christ’s well-lived life and consequent death, without promising immediate relief.

Strongest feature: Swinburne gives a distinctly Christian account with careful internal distinctions: God shares exposed suffering through incarnation, while Christ’s life and consequent death provide reparation rather than an immediate termination of suffering. Principal limitation: The war and vandalism analogies illustrate solidarity and payment but do not independently justify divine permission, representative reparation, or the connection between redemption from sin and observed suffering. Live burden: He must defend those theological premises and explain how delayed redemption contributes to adequate explanation rather than merely restating doctrine, while preserving his explicit no-immediate-end qualification. Locked score: 79/100 credits substantial development, precision, and calibrated scope, moderated by analogical compression and an incomplete bridge from solidarity and reparation to present suffering.

Bart Ehrman — section score 82/100

Ehrman contrasts incarnation’s claimed solidarity and salvation with continued disaster, disease, genocide, and apparently ineffective prayer, asking for evidence of present divine intervention.

Strongest feature: Ehrman distinguishes shared suffering from effective intervention and presses a relevant evidential question about what observable difference incarnation and salvation make amid continuing disaster, disease, and genocide. Principal limitation: Continued suffering does not establish that no intervention occurs, and his broad statement that prayer does not matter outruns the comparative evidence offered in the exchange. Live burden: He must frame persistence as counterevidence rather than categorical disproof, while engaging Swinburne’s claim that redemption has delayed and nonpreventive purposes. Locked score: 80/100 recognizes a clear, motion-relevant challenge and concrete examples, but discounts causal overreach, limited evidence about prayer, and the gap between apparent nonintervention and a demonstrated absence of divine action across the very different cases that he groups together.

Practical response, heaven, and final choice

1:13:28–1:18:46

Richard Swinburne — section score 79/100

Swinburne argues earthly consequential freedom forms character suited for heaven, where settled goodness can later coexist with no serious possibility of evil.

Strongest feature: Swinburne directly answers the heaven counterexample by distinguishing two temporally ordered goods, formative earthly choice and subsequently settled good character, rather than denying heavenly freedom. Principal limitation: The sequence relies on theological assumptions about entry and character formation and does not show that severe earthly suffering is necessary or proportionate to producing the relevant fitness. Live burden: His side must connect actual harms to this developmental mechanism and defend why less destructive formation would be inadequate, while no penalty can attach to Ehrman’s lack of a later reply opportunity. Locked score: 84/100 reflects a coherent, precise, highly responsive closing answer, moderated by limited independent warrant and the continuing proportionality problem for children, disaster victims, and others who seemingly receive no formative opportunity.

Bart Ehrman — section score 83/100

Ehrman separates explanatory uncertainty from practical agency, urging concrete action against homelessness, disease, poverty, and starvation rather than passive speculation.

Strongest feature: Ehrman clearly distinguishes lacking a perfectly satisfying explanation from the capacity and obligation to respond, grounding his conclusion in concrete forms of relief rather than resignation or passivity. Principal limitation: Practical action completes a subsidiary burden but does not itself prove that Christian explanations are inadequate, and Job and Ecclesiastes receive only brief support for the stronger epistemic conclusion. Live burden: His side must keep humanitarian response separate from the motion-level explanatory verdict while preserving the corrected claim that people can respond and avoiding any invented contradiction. Locked score: 83/100 rewards clarity, calibration, and constructive practical relevance, tempered because the action recommendation bears indirectly on the central adequacy dispute and cannot independently settle which account of evil is intellectually satisfying.
