Overall assessment

Blake Giunta — Jesus rose from the dead: 77/100

Published strengths

  • Narrowed the historical foundation to early proclamation rather than depending on complete Gospel reliability.
  • Used comparative Bayesian reasoning without claiming that evidential fit deductively establishes resurrection.
  • Conceded that one genuinely plausible naturalistic rival would materially undercut his explanatory case.
  • Distinguished scientific heuristics from broader historical and philosophical standards of rational assessment.

Published weaknesses

  • Moved too quickly from early proclamation and sincerity to the truth of a bodily resurrection.
  • Relied on contested narrative details when dismissing alternatives after narrowing the source base.
  • Left the theological prior dependent on many compressed and independently unsupported premises.

Matt Dillahunty — Resurrection is unproven and probably legendary: 84/100

Published strengths

  • Repeatedly separated the existence of reports, sincere belief, actual experience, and supernatural cause.
  • Applied evidential discipline even to skeptical alternatives by rejecting the unsupported drug explanation.
  • Qualified the legend conclusion and preserved uncertainty rather than assuming resurrection was impossible.
  • Explained why narrative agreement and ordinary accuracy cannot automatically verify an extraordinary cause.

Published weaknesses

  • Sometimes treated ideal historical source criteria as stronger necessities than his argument justified.
  • Underestimated epistemic probability by leaning too heavily on the absence of repeat resurrection cases.
  • Compressed complex atonement theology and attributed compensatory motives beyond the closing evidence.

Early testimony and historical access

3:46–2:01:27

Blake Giunta — section score 83/100

Giunta dates the Corinthians creed within years of Jesus’s death and connects it to Jerusalem, arguing that apostolic appearance proclamation began too early for leisurely legendary invention.

Strongest feature: Giunta identifies a genuinely early evidential datum and explains why proximity to Jerusalem constrains theories that require resurrection proclamation to emerge only after prolonged, geographically remote embellishment. Principal limitation: The dating and provenance establish that named appearances were proclaimed, but neither the creed’s formula nor its circulation independently discloses what Peter, the Twelve, or other recipients experienced, so early attestation cannot bear the whole distance from report to event. Live burden: Giunta must connect the early formula to sufficiently reliable access, sincere experiential belief, and a causal comparison that favors bodily resurrection over mistake, vision, social transmission, or compressed tradition. Locked score: 82 reflects a strong and relevant historical foundation whose inferential reach remains materially narrower than the motion’s supernatural conclusion.

Matt Dillahunty — section score 86/100

Dillahunty argues that interested, indirect, later, and mutually divergent accounts lack the contemporaneity and independence desirable for establishing an extraordinary resurrection event.

Strongest feature: Dillahunty supplies claim-sensitive criteria and concrete narrative disagreements, properly emphasizing that theological interest, temporal distance, uncertain authorship, and dependence can limit historical access to extraordinary events. Principal limitation: He sometimes presents ideal source conditions as though their absence substantially settles reliability, without differentiating the early creed and Acts material from later Gospel narratives or calibrating how historians responsibly use imperfect evidence. Live burden: Dillahunty must engage how much the particular early sources support resurrection, granting their genuine value for tracing proclamation while explaining why they remain insufficient for the claimed experience and divine cause. Locked score: 75 credits a relevant evidential caution and numerous specifics, while marking the argument’s occasional overbreadth and incomplete engagement with the affirmative’s strongest restricted source base.

Sincerity, legend, and alternative explanations

20:10–1:58:40

Blake Giunta — section score 76/100

Giunta argues that swoon, imposture, and shared hallucination each conflict with important features of the reports, making resurrection comparatively better than familiar naturalistic rivals.

Strongest feature: Giunta does not rest solely on ignorance; he identifies theory-specific pressures, including a badly injured survivor’s condition, close associates recognizing an impostor, and the difficulty of literally shared hallucinations. Principal limitation: Several rebuttals depend on contested narrative details and a too-narrow menu of rivals, while heterogeneous visions, memory development, social reinforcement, and legendary aggregation need not resemble one simultaneous group hallucination. Live burden: Giunta must compare resurrection against the strongest composite naturalistic account using evidence independently secured by his early sources, not against simplified alternatives whose failure leaves many other causal mixtures untouched. Locked score: 67 reflects useful comparative engagement but substantial incompleteness, because defeating three familiar proposals does not yet make a bodily miracle the best supported explanation.

Matt Dillahunty — section score 85/100

Dillahunty reads the movement from Paul’s sparse appearance list to increasingly physical and detailed Gospel stories as evidence of legendary elaboration and unresolved narrative disagreement.

Strongest feature: The argument offers a recognizable developmental pattern rather than mere disbelief, connecting later specificity, physicality, and divergent scenes to known processes of narrative expansion over time. Principal limitation: Chronological increase in surviving detail does not alone prove that the detail was newly invented, since genre, authorial purpose, source selection, and independent earlier traditions could also explain why Paul’s formula is sparse. Live burden: Dillahunty must establish that the observed sequence is more expected under legendary development than under selective reporting of preexisting beliefs, while keeping his conclusion probabilistic and distinct from denying every early appearance claim. Locked score: 82 credits a coherent positive alternative with substantial explanatory relevance, reduced because the temporal pattern remains suggestive rather than discriminating without stronger dependence and provenance analysis.

Probability, method, and supernatural inference

34:26–2:34:23

Blake Giunta — section score 80/100

Giunta argues that sincere apostolic belief is more expected if God raised Jesus than under proposed hallucination accounts, so the evidence comparatively supports resurrection without entailing it.

Strongest feature: Giunta correctly frames likelihood evidence as comparative rather than deductive, explaining how a datum may favor one hypothesis even when it neither proves that hypothesis nor exhausts the alternative space. Principal limitation: High fit conditional on resurrection is inexpensive unless the prior probability of the relevant God’s existence and intention, the specificity of the evidence, and the likelihoods under stronger composite rivals are independently constrained. Live burden: Giunta must supply defensible priors and compare the same secured evidence across serious alternatives, avoiding a resurrection hypothesis tailored to predict precisely the tradition under examination. Locked score: 83 rewards sound Bayesian structure and a direct response to the charge of ignorance, while recognizing that the numerical and theological inputs remain asserted more than demonstrated.

Matt Dillahunty — section score 82/100

Dillahunty argues that a resurrection conflicts with ordinary experience, so rational acceptance requires unusually strong evidence and may remain suspended when the available record is insufficient.

Strongest feature: The principle sensibly makes evidential thresholds sensitive to background knowledge and distinguishes mundane biographical claims from a divine reversal of death, while preserving “I do not know” as a legitimate conclusion. Principal limitation: “Extraordinary” can become rhetorical unless translated into comparative probabilities, and rare events are not automatically poorly evidenced merely because ordinary experience supplies few analogues. Live burden: Dillahunty must specify which evidential defects keep the posterior below acceptance and engage Giunta’s claim that divine background knowledge can alter the prior, rather than letting the label extraordinary settle the outcome. Locked score: 87 reflects a powerful, motion-level standard joined to appropriate epistemic restraint, short of decisive because its calibration remains qualitative and contested.

Theological fit and divine hiddenness

22:38–2:30:33

Blake Giunta — section score 62/100

Giunta argues that if God exists, free moral agency, redemption from evil, and Jesus’s role in that plan can make one unique resurrection antecedently fitting.

Strongest feature: Giunta recognizes that resurrection probability cannot be assessed in isolation and attempts to explain why a good God might uniquely act in Jesus rather than habitually raising the dead. Principal limitation: The compressed free-will and atonement narrative contains many controversial theological premises, and showing internal compatibility does not establish that God exists, adopts this redemptive strategy, or would signal it through resurrection. Live burden: Giunta must independently support the divine attributes, moral-agent framework, need for past-sin resolution, and Jesus’s unique role before these claims can substantially raise the prior. Locked score: 62 reflects a relevant attempt to address background probability, but the argument remains too abbreviated and assumption-heavy to provide strong antecedent support for the historical conclusion.

Matt Dillahunty — section score 78/100

Dillahunty argues that if divine ability can fit physical or spiritual appearances and selective perception alike, the explanation accommodates every outcome and predicts too little.

Strongest feature: The objection targets explanatory discrimination: allowing God to make companions hear but not see, or to choose physical and spiritual modes as needed, can insulate the hypothesis from contrary configurations. Principal limitation: Flexibility alone does not destroy explanation if an independent theology constrains divine purposes, and Dillahunty does not examine whether Giunta’s redemptive account supplies any such restrictions. Live burden: Dillahunty must show that the proposed divine intentions are insufficiently specific to predict the actual evidence, while Giunta retains responsibility for stating risky expectations rather than retrospective accommodations. Locked score: 85 reflects a sharp and broadly applicable challenge to ad hoc theistic explanation, moderated because a sufficiently developed purposive model could answer it in principle.
