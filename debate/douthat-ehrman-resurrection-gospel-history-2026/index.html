Overall assessment

Ross Douthat — Historical-resurrection challenge: 84/100

Published strengths

  • Douthat isolated the methodological effect of miraculous content through a controlled comparison instead of merely accusing historical skepticism of prejudice.
  • He correctly distinguished fallible, mediated testimony from inerrancy and showed that contradiction does not logically entail total historical worthlessness.
  • His local-knowledge and Acts arguments supplied concrete documentary considerations supporting continuity and earlier proximity rather than relying only on general trust.
  • He pressed the visionary account to explain bodily claims, persistent doubt, missionary activity, and movement formation as a connected causal package.
  • His closing appeal to narrative strangeness directly engaged the literary-development explanation across several awkward resurrection details.

Published weaknesses

  • The hypothetical attestation threshold was never connected rigorously to the actual number, independence, provenance, or skeptical disposition of the resurrection sources.
  • Accurate names and geography were asked to carry broader historical credit without establishing that sayings and narrated events were preserved by the same mechanisms.
  • The argument from Acts’ ending insufficiently compared early composition with thematic or literary reasons for ending while Paul preached in Rome.
  • Narrative oddity and psychological realism remained compatible with layered literary development, so neither became strong positive evidence for occurrence.

Bart Ehrman — Skeptical historian: 86/100

Published strengths

  • Ehrman combined dependence, anonymity, dating, linguistic distance, oral transmission, and contradiction while still allowing that the Gospels preserve credible history.
  • He used concrete incompatible Gospel details to defeat simultaneous inerrancy without inflating that conclusion into wholesale historical skepticism.
  • His probabilistic account of historical establishment directly answered both the miracle-content challenge and the proposed power of abundant attestation.
  • He calibrated the visionary reconstruction carefully, acknowledging uncertainty about the number and grouping of experiences rather than asserting uniform hallucination.
  • He supplemented visions with messianic reinterpretation and polemical literary development, producing a more complete ordinary causal explanation.

Published weaknesses

  • The initial source-transmission case compressed the inference from conditions permitting change to evidence that the particular resurrection traditions materially changed.
  • The claim that unprecedented miracles cannot be historically established sounded stronger than the comparative probabilistic method itself warranted.
  • The empty-tomb response did not contact the earlier local-knowledge argument selected as its decisive target and therefore functioned as relevant contrary material rather than an answer.
  • The natural reconstruction still left persistent doubt, collective diffusion, missionary scale, and the precise development of bodily content only partly explained.

Documentary Base, Dating, and Historical Proximity

23:46–67:20

Ross Douthat — section score 83/100

Acts ends with Paul alive in Rome and omits subsequent upheavals, supporting an early-sixties composition that would place Luke-Acts nearer its narrated events.

Strongest feature: The argument identifies conspicuously omitted persecution, warfare, and temple destruction, then develops a chronologically intelligible inference from Acts concluding while Paul remains alive in Rome. Principal limitation: It privileges authorial unawareness without comparatively testing thematic closure, selective narrative scope, or deliberate literary purpose, so omission supports an earlier date without securing it. Live burden: The pro case must demonstrate that this ending is appreciably more expected under early composition than later literary design, while separately addressing anonymity, documentary dependence, contradiction, and transmission. Locked score: The strong band reflects substantial central relevance, concrete textual evidence, and direct engagement with the skeptical dating component, moderated by slightly overstated calibration and incomplete coverage of the wider source-critical challenge.

Principal limitation: The cited comparison establishes substantial literary difference more securely than it establishes the precise direction, causes, or historical consequences of every relationship, while the move does not engage a developed continuity interpretation. Transmission, Variation, and Gospel Contradictions

31:54–59:04

Ross Douthat — section score 87/100

Errors, compression, and divergent interpretation can occur in sincere eyewitness-derived mediation, so Gospel discrepancies defeat strict inerrancy without making the accounts historically worthless.

Strongest feature: The reply distinguishes inerrancy from historical usefulness and advances compatibility rather than proof, conceding ordinary error while resisting the inference from discrepancy to worthless testimony. Principal limitation: Its memoir analogy is anecdotal and cannot establish that the Gospel contradictions emerged under comparable conditions, from memories, or through transmission reliably preserving events. Live burden: The pro route must examine variations, distinguish ordinary testimonial divergence from theological alteration, and provide positive evidence that witness-derived continuity explains these documents better than layered literary development. Locked score: The very strong band reflects careful calibration, major responsiveness, central relevance, and charitable acknowledgment of error, tempered because general compatibility with sincere testimony cannot verify the provenance or reliability of these particular accounts.

Bart Ehrman — section score 89/100

The Gospels retain credible history but resist straightforward reading because anonymous, later non-eyewitness authors transmitted changing oral stories in another language and produced contradictions.

Strongest feature: The move combines anonymity, third-person narration, linguistic distance, later composition, oral transmission, and contradiction into a basis for bounded confidence while preserving the possibility of credible history. Principal limitation: Its transition from conditions permitting change to demonstrated alteration remains compressed, because it offers limited evidence about how frequently, directionally, or materially the traditions changed before composition. Live burden: The skeptical case must specify which resurrection claims are weakened by dependence or transmission and explain why preserved local knowledge or mediated eyewitness material cannot support those claims. Locked score: The very strong band reflects central-burden contact, qualification, acknowledgment of possible preservation, and a documentary foundation, with restraint for the underdeveloped bridge between transmission risk and actual corruption.

Historical Method and Extraordinary Claims

35:33–41:11

Ross Douthat — section score 88/100

When two stories share the same transmission history but only one contains a miracle, historians must explain whether miraculous content itself warrants additional distrust.

Strongest feature: The controlled hypothetical isolates miraculous content while holding lateness, oral transmission, and linguistic change constant, compelling the historian to explain whether rarity adds distrust beyond source defects. Principal limitation: This diagnostic question provides neither empirical base rates nor a comparison establishing that resurrection testimony equals an ordinary report in independence, quality, or transmission history. Live burden: The pro route must advance from identifying methodological asymmetry to showing how resurrection testimony defeats dependence, witness error, embellishment, invention, and other concrete alternatives. Locked score: The very strong band reflects exceptional clarity, logical focus, central historical-method relevance, and charitable isolation of the disputed variable, while recognizing that a methodological challenge does not independently constitute positive evidence.

Bart Ehrman — section score 89/100

Historians compare unprecedented miracle reports with historically familiar invention or alteration and may find the latter more probable without declaring the reported miracle impossible.

Strongest feature: The reply directly answers the matched-story challenge by distinguishing metaphysical possibility from historical establishment and requiring comparison between a miracle report and familiar mechanisms such as alteration, mistake, or invention. Principal limitation: Its suggestion that an unprecedented miracle cannot be historically established is stronger than the general comparative argument supplied, because sufficiently powerful evidence could conceptually overcome a low prior without abandoning ordinary historical reasoning. Live burden: The skeptical case must evaluate the actual strength, independence, and provenance of the resurrection reports instead of allowing unprecedented content to function as a fixed evidentiary ceiling. Locked score: The very strong band reflects high responsiveness, central relevance, explicit qualification, and a coherent probabilistic framework, moderated because its categorical-sounding methodological boundary slightly outruns the warrant developed in this exchange.

Experiences and the Origins of Resurrection Belief

70:04–77:03

Ross Douthat — section score 81/100

Common bereavement visions inadequately explain the early combination of radical bodily claims, continuing doubt, missionary activity, and the sudden formation of a new religion.

Strongest feature: The objection identifies multiple explananda beyond an initial experience, including radical bodily claims, persistent doubt, missionary action, and movement formation, thereby exposing the limits of a simple bereavement-vision analogy. Principal limitation: It moves from dissimilarity toward explanatory insufficiency without comparing stronger natural models involving a few visionaries, prior messianic commitment, persuasion, social amplification, and later theological development. Live burden: The pro route must specify a rival causal account and establish the earliest form and interdependence of its listed phenomena, rather than treating the religion’s eventual scale as direct evidence for resurrection. Locked score: The strong band reflects important central-burden contact, substantial responsiveness, and a concrete explanatory challenge, moderated by implicit qualifications, partly unstable causal scope, limited documentation, and incomplete engagement with a multistage alternative.

Bart Ehrman — section score 84/100

Some followers likely experienced post-crucifixion visions, came to believe Jesus was alive, and persuaded others, although the evidence does not establish bodily resurrection.

Strongest feature: The reconstruction is explicitly calibrated about the unknown number and grouping of experiences, separates followers’ conviction from historical establishment, and supplies a familiar initial mechanism for the rise of resurrection belief. Principal limitation: Visions explain the first convictions more readily than the complete doctrinal content, bodily emphasis, persistent doubt, collective spread, and durable missionary consequences, leaving several important causal links compressed. Live burden: The skeptical case must develop a multistage account showing how limited experiences, persuasion, prior commitments, and social transmission generated the movement’s distinctive claims without assuming that every follower shared an identical vision. Locked score: The very strong band reflects coherent central-burden contact, candid uncertainty, proportionate force, and a plausible ordinary starting point, moderated by incomplete explanatory coverage and limited direct testing of the bodily-resurrection alternative.
