Overall assessment

Than Christopoulos — Resurrection historicity: 79/100

Published strengths

  • Christopoulos clearly framed the dispute through priors and comparative likelihoods, directly connecting evidential strength to the probability required by the motion.
  • His likelihood-ratio formulation kept the affirmative case comparative rather than treating resurrection as established merely because some supporting reports exist.
  • He assembled a cumulative historical case from source indicators, known burial, an empty tomb, reported appearances, conversion, and costly witness conduct.
  • His incarnation argument was appropriately conditional and modest, claiming only that contested theistic background assumptions could prevent a prohibitively low prior.

Published weaknesses

  • The stable-natural-order argument established compatibility between regularity and miracles, but it did not adequately answer why pervasive mortality should not still lower resurrection's prior probability.
  • The source and empty-tomb cases bundled several disputed considerations without sufficiently establishing document-specific independence, confirmed witnesses, or the necessary burial sequence.
  • Appeals to sincerity, persecution, and willingness to die supported committed belief more readily than the truth or accurate transmission of the underlying resurrection claim.

Matt Dillahunty — Skeptical naturalism: 88/100

Published strengths

  • Dillahunty sharply distinguished the probability of receiving resurrection reports if resurrection occurred from the probability that resurrection occurred given those reports.
  • He repeatedly separated sincerity and costly commitment from truth, directly answering the affirmative reliance on transformed and suffering witnesses.
  • He identified death, burial, and prior occupancy of a tomb as necessary evidential foundations that require support before an empty tomb can carry explanatory weight.
  • He preserved rational uncertainty as a legitimate conclusion when neither resurrection nor any particular natural alternative receives sufficiently grounded probability assignments.
  • His alternatives argument correctly maintained that weaknesses in individual natural explanations do not themselves supply affirmative evidence for resurrection.

Published weaknesses

  • The demand for empirical evidence, confirmed primary witnesses, contemporary corroboration, and independent investigation was forceful but sometimes stated too categorically for an ancient historical inquiry.
  • The divine-action critique challenged incarnation and sacrificial suffering, but it did less to address the affirmative's narrower conditional claim that theistic background beliefs could alter the prior.
  • The account-disparity objection identified a real reliability issue, yet it did not specify which differences were irreconcilable or how strongly each one should reduce the cumulative case.

Source Reliability, Independence, and Transmission

6:44–49:34

Than Christopoulos — section score 80/100

Testimony can provide evidence for a miracle because testimonial knowledge is generally indispensable and a sufficiently reliable report can increase a hypothesis’s probability.

Strongest feature: The move establishes a modest epistemic threshold: testimony is not automatically disqualified from evidential consideration because its event is miraculous, and reliable reporting can increase a hypothesis's probability. Principal limitation: The inference from testimony's indispensability in ordinary knowledge to its usefulness for extraordinary claims leaves reliability conditions implicit: independence, transmission integrity, incentive, corroboration, genre, and the comparative probability of error. Live burden: The constructive must identify which resurrection reports satisfy those conditions and show that their testimonial profile favors resurrection over mistaken, legendary, coordinated, or naturalistic explanations. Locked score: The strong/competent 79 fits the move's coherent possibility claim, relevance, and stable proposition, while recognizing general testimonial admissibility, partly unstable scope, implicit qualification, and limited source-specific warrant.

Matt Dillahunty — section score 79/100

Unresolved differences among the resurrection narratives reduce their reliability, particularly when those accounts are presented as divinely inspired evidence for a uniquely important event.

Strongest feature: The reply contests the affirmative’s source-reliability foundation by identifying narrative variations and explaining why unresolved divergence becomes consequential when accounts are presented as divinely inspired testimony about an important historical occurrence. Principal limitation: It aggregates disparities without demonstrating which examples are contradictions, centrally significant, or resistant to harmonization, and insufficiently distinguishes standards for inspiration claims from ordinary historical reliability. Live burden: The negative must analyze particular discrepancies, establish their seriousness, and show that peripheral perspectival variation cannot explain them before inferring substantial unreliability from their cumulative presence. Death, Burial, and Empty-Tomb Foundations

22:04–52:28

Than Christopoulos — section score 78/100

A known burial, failure to produce Jesus’s body, multiple attestations, hostile acknowledgment, and discovery by women collectively support an authentic empty tomb.

Strongest feature: The argument connects identifiable burial circumstances, nonproduction of the body, multiple testimony, adversarial acknowledgment, and culturally disadvantageous female discoverers, making the empty-tomb inference directly relevant and cumulatively substantial. Principal limitation: Those categories remain insufficiently differentiated, because narrative multiplicity does not establish independence, silence about producing a body is not independent confirmation, and women witnesses require historically specific qualification. Live burden: The proponent must demonstrate that the burial location was public, attestations derive from meaningfully separate evidential streams, and early-tradition development cannot explain the surviving narratives without an independently verified empty tomb. Locked score: The strong/competent 78 appropriately recognizes coherent, load-bearing cumulative support while discounting implicit qualifications, overstated force, incomplete alternative-testing, and comparatively weaker evidential warrant.

Matt Dillahunty — section score 87/100

Before resurrection can be inferred, Jesus’s actual death must be historically established through evidence adequate to exclude mistaken determination or premature burial.

Strongest feature: The reply isolates an indispensable prerequisite for resurrection—actual death—and challenges whether the affirmative’s burial-and-empty-tomb sequence establishes that prerequisite enough to exclude mistaken determination or premature burial, making its burden contact precise and consequential. Principal limitation: Calling the narrative propaganda discounts converging contextual and medical indicators without examining them, while the demand for confirmation risks suggesting forensic verification where ancient-history reasoning depends upon testimony and inference. Live burden: The negative must explain what degree of independent confirmation historical inquiry requires, then demonstrate why the narrative and contextual considerations remain inadequate under that standard. Locked score: The very strong 85 reflects necessity, formulation, coherence, and responsiveness, with evidentiary development and representational charity remaining the constraints.

Principal limitation: Rejecting a uniform hallucination does not address heterogeneous experiences, misperception, reconstructive memory, legendary development, testimonial dependence, or transmission processes, while independence and firsthand access remain undocumented. Live burden: The proponent must establish which reports are independent, how sensory particulars were preserved, and why the evidence discriminates resurrection from interacting natural explanations rather than merely defeating an unusually narrow rival. Locked score: The mixed 72 reflects excellent central relevance and responsiveness but weaker coherence, evidential verification, qualification, scope control, and charitable engagement with the strongest alternative family.

Matt Dillahunty — section score 93/100

Witness sincerity cannot ensure factual accuracy because honest people may be mistaken, and later transmission can distort even an initially sincere statement.

Strongest feature: The reply sharply separates honesty from factual accuracy, answering costly conduct, miracle testimony, and varied appearance reports while preserving the explicit possibility that sincere witnesses were mistaken or their statements were distorted. Principal limitation: Its evidential basis establishes general routes to error, not that any witness erred, possessed secondhand information, or underwent transmission corruption, so the critique limits corroborative force without independently disproving the reported experiences. Live burden: The affirmative must establish firsthand status, memory, accurate preservation, and evidential support beyond sincerity, even when costly behavior is charitably treated as evidence against deliberate deception. Locked score: At 91, this very strong reply is coherent, centrally relevant, carefully qualified, and highly responsive, with its principal constraint arising from case-specific evidence rather than logic.
