Overall assessment

Michael Licona — Historians can establish Jesus's resurrection as the best explanation: 78/100

Published strengths

  • Licona established a clear probabilistic threshold and consistently organized his affirmative around explanatory scope, power, and avoidance of unsupported additions.
  • He insulated the core case from sweeping Gospel objections by relying on narrower appearance beliefs that Ehrman himself substantially conceded.
  • His reply to Apollonius identified concrete temporal and attestation differences, while his natural-versus-divine probability distinction directly challenged Ehrman’s categorical barrier.

Published weaknesses

  • Licona repeatedly asserted that resurrection explained the facts without strain, but never supplied a comprehensive, consistently weighted comparison against developed natural alternatives.
  • His strongest probability reply depended on God’s existence and intention without providing independent historical warrant for either antecedent within the debate.
  • He sometimes expanded modestly conceded appearance beliefs into bodily, transformed-corpse and tomb-related content that the locked factual bedrock did not independently contain.

Bart Ehrman — Historical methods cannot prove a resurrection: 79/100

Published strengths

  • Ehrman’s late, anonymous, non-eyewitness source critique supplied concrete reasons to resist treating the Gospel narratives as immediate testimony.
  • He sharply distinguished crucifixion and hostile conversion from positive evidence, preventing background facts and sincere conviction from carrying unwarranted miraculous force.
  • His public-evidence requirement directly confronted the motion’s disciplinary boundary and exposed the theological assumptions embedded in Licona’s conditional divine-causation reply.

Published weaknesses

  • Ehrman defined miracles as least probable and then used that definition to bar historical proof, without separating prior rarity from posterior probability.
  • His children’s telephone analogy oversimplified ancient communal transmission and supplied little evidence about how the actual resurrection traditions developed.
  • The Apollonius challenge initially assumed evidential parity without comparing dates, independence, source quantity, or content, leaving Licona a strong and specific rebuttal.

Historical Method and Public Standards

13:32–95:19

Michael Licona — section score 77/100

Licona defines historical proof as reasonable or adequate certainty rather than doubt’s elimination, because historians ordinarily express conclusions probabilistically, much like courtroom judgments.

Strongest feature: Licona establishes a calibrated threshold for the entire affirmative, coherently explaining that historical proof concerns reasonable probability rather than impossible certainty and thereby preventing residual doubt alone from defeating his conclusion. Principal limitation: The courtroom comparison illustrates ordinary usage but does not independently justify transferring a legal threshold into historical inquiry, whose evidence, error costs, and adjudicative procedures differ substantially. Live burden: Licona must still specify how explanatory comparison produces adequate certainty here, demonstrate that resurrection clears that threshold, and answer whether extraordinary causal claims require stronger warrant than ordinary reconstructions. Locked score: The frozen 84 appropriately reflects excellent relevance, clarity, responsiveness, and coherence, alongside comparatively weaker evidentiary support for the chosen threshold and its application to this uniquely disputed conclusion.

Bart Ehrman — section score 80/100

Ehrman says historians prefer independent sources that agree because contradictions weaken confidence about which account deserves reliance, establishing his standard for evaluating Gospel testimony.

Strongest feature: Ehrman articulates a plausible and readily applicable source-critical principle, explaining why agreement among genuinely independent witnesses can increase confidence and why contradiction creates a concrete selection problem. Principal limitation: The formulation leaves contradiction undefined and risks treating discrepancies of unequal importance alike, while consistency alone cannot establish independence, accuracy, or freedom from shared tradition. Live burden: Ehrman must distinguish material conflicts from ordinary variation, show that the relevant Gospel differences concern resurrection claims rather than peripheral details, and explain what reliable core survives disagreement. Locked score: The frozen 79 appropriately rewards coherent reasoning, clarity, methodological relevance, and constructive responsiveness, while the absent materiality threshold, limited independent defense, and somewhat overbroad suggestion that contradiction leaves no usable source prevent a higher assessment.

Source Quality and Reliability

35:31–90:09

Michael Licona — section score 81/100

Licona narrows his case to facts widely granted by historians, arguing that biblical inerrancy, traditional authorship, particular dating, and comprehensive Gospel trustworthiness are unnecessary premises.

Strongest feature: Licona directly limits the dependency of his argument, correctly observing that broad objections to Gospel inerrancy or traditional authorship do not automatically defeat a case built from narrower concessions. Principal limitation: Calling the selected facts virtually undisputed does not independently establish them, and source distortion can still affect precisely what appearance beliefs, experiences, or bodily interpretations the narrowed facts contain. Live burden: Licona must warrant each selected fact from available sources, define the conceded content conservatively, and show that Ehrman’s transmission objections do not undermine the evidentiary detail required by resurrection. Locked score: The frozen 80 fittingly rewards a coherent, precise, responsive source-control strategy with substantial burden relevance, while incomplete fact-level documentation and overstated consensus prevent the narrowing maneuver from resolving the underlying reliability dispute.

Bart Ehrman — section score 79/100

Ehrman argues that the Gospels’ thirty-five-to-sixty-five-year gap, non-eyewitness character, and internally unclaimed traditional authorship weaken their evidential value as direct resurrection testimony.

Strongest feature: Ehrman provides concrete source characteristics, including a specified chronological gap and the distinction between internal anonymity and later traditional titles, which directly weakens claims of immediate eyewitness testimony. Principal limitation: The passage does not document its dating and authorship judgments or establish how much those features reduce reliability, since later non-eyewitness works can preserve earlier material. Live burden: Ehrman must trace the resurrection traditions through their available earlier sources, separate dependence from independent attestation, and demonstrate that transmission problems materially undermine the limited bedrock Licona actually uses. Locked score: The frozen 84 fits the move’s strong coherence, specificity, responsiveness, relevance, and evidentiary substance, with appropriate deductions because categorical non-eyewitness phrasing and an incomplete account of preserved tradition exceed what this excerpt independently establishes.

Historical Bedrock and Evidential Relevance

21:10–88:32

Michael Licona — section score 83/100

Licona designates Jesus’s crucifixion and the disciples’ conviction that the risen Jesus appeared as widely accepted bedrock facts requiring explanation from competing hypotheses.

Strongest feature: Licona identifies two intelligible and highly relevant inputs for comparative explanation, separating the historically secure crucifixion from the disciples’ reported interpretation rather than directly treating resurrection itself as established evidence. Principal limitation: His appeal to virtual scholarly unanimity is undocumented and compresses important disputes about what the disciples experienced, how many reported experiences, and whether bodily content belongs within the conceded fact. Live burden: Licona must substantiate the claimed consensus, preserve distinctions between belief and occurrence, and demonstrate why crucifixion plus appearance conviction comparatively favors bodily resurrection rather than merely requiring some psychological or social explanation. Locked score: The frozen 79 matches substantial burden relevance and understandable structure, but reduced evidence warrant, overstated consensus, and the gap between these facts and resurrection properly prevent a stronger result.

Bart Ehrman — section score 89/100

Ehrman distinguishes a necessary precondition from supporting evidence, noting that thousands were crucified although their deaths provide no positive reason to infer resurrection.

Strongest feature: Ehrman precisely separates background eligibility from discriminating evidence, showing that crucifixion is necessary for a resurrection claim but, given thousands of crucifixions, contributes almost nothing distinctive toward its truth. Principal limitation: The objection can overstate Licona’s use, because crucifixion may belong within the event sequence an explanation must cover even when it supplies little independent likelihood favoring resurrection. Live burden: Ehrman must preserve the distinction between explanatory scope and positive discrimination, then show that removing crucifixion’s rhetorical weight materially weakens the aggregate comparative inference rather than merely relabeling one input. Locked score: The frozen 89 fits the move’s exceptional coherence, warrant, responsiveness, relevance, precision, and fair calibration, with only a modest reservation about whether Licona presented crucifixion as standalone confirmation rather than necessary explanandum.

Appearances and Rival Explanations

23:25–82:31

Michael Licona — section score 72/100

Licona contends scriptural reinterpretation alone would not generate bodily-resurrection belief after an unrescued execution unless experiences had already persuaded the disciples that Jesus appeared.

Strongest feature: Licona targets a concrete causal weakness in a scripture-only alternative, reasoning that an executed and buried messiah would not straightforwardly yield bodily-resurrection conviction without some experience demanding reinterpretation. Principal limitation: The passage moves from the likelihood of experiences to their bodily significance while providing little support against creative exegesis, grief, community reinforcement, visionary phenomena, or combinations of those mechanisms. Live burden: Licona must demonstrate not merely that experiences occurred, but that their content and aftermath discriminate bodily resurrection from natural experiences subsequently interpreted through scripture and collective expectation. Locked score: The frozen 71 fits a relevant and understandable challenge to one rival, yet limited psychological evidence, restricted alternative coverage, and excessive confidence in the transition from experience to bodily appearance substantially reduce warrant and calibration.

Bart Ehrman — section score 78/100

Ehrman argues that postmortem appearance testimony cannot prove Jesus’s resurrection unless materially comparable claims about Apollonius receive equivalent treatment, testing whether Licona’s evidence overgenerates.

Strongest feature: Ehrman deploys a useful parity test, asking whether the affirmative’s treatment of postmortem appearance claims would generate resurrection conclusions for other ancient figures and thereby exposing potential discriminatory weakness. Principal limitation: The passage supplies no comparative dates, source independence, textual proximity, witness access, or detailed claim content, while reducing Licona’s cumulative case to somebody reporting a later appearance. Live burden: Ehrman must establish genuine evidential symmetry between Apollonius and Jesus, answer differences in source quality, and demonstrate that the same explanatory criteria yield inconsistent outcomes when applied evenhandedly. Locked score: The frozen 75 appropriately credits direct responsiveness, relevance, clarity, and a coherent challenge, but limited supporting comparison and an insufficiently charitable reconstruction of the affirmative case materially weaken warrant and force.

Miracle Probability and the Motion

27:22–76:54

Michael Licona — section score 77/100

Licona concludes that resurrection’s substantial superiority over rival explanations makes it quite to very probable, satisfying his reasonable-certainty standard and answering the motion affirmatively.

Strongest feature: Licona unmistakably completes his argumentative route, connecting the best-explanation claim to a probabilistic historical conclusion and directly answering whether historians can prove that Jesus rose. Principal limitation: The conclusion assumes that comparative superiority guarantees high absolute probability, although the passage neither supplies priors nor demonstrates comprehensive victory over developed alternatives by the promised criteria. Live burden: Licona must justify the transition from best among considered explanations to quite or very probable, quantify the alleged margin meaningfully, and establish that no omitted competitor changes the comparison. Locked score: The frozen 75 reflects exceptional relevance and strong responsiveness to the exact motion, but mediocre warrant, incomplete logical bridging, imprecise probability language, and confidence exceeding the displayed comparison appropriately constrain the final constructive payoff.

Bart Ehrman — section score 69/100

Ehrman argues historians establish probable past events, whereas miracles are spectacularly unusual and therefore definitionally least probable, placing resurrection beyond historical demonstration.

Strongest feature: Ehrman identifies the motion’s central methodological tension with admirable directness, contrasting history’s probabilistic conclusions with an event defined as an extraordinary departure from ordinary natural regularity. Principal limitation: The inference conflates low natural frequency with lowest probability after case-specific evidence and alternative causal assumptions, so rarity alone does not establish that resurrection must remain posteriorly inferior. Live burden: Ehrman must defend the admissible probability framework, explain why purported divine agency cannot alter the comparison, and show that methodological restraint follows without simply defining the disputed conclusion outside history. Locked score: The frozen 69 accurately recognizes excellent relevance and responsiveness plus a clear intuitive concern, while serious coherence, warrant, precision, and charity weaknesses follow from treating unusual, naturally irregular, and least probable as equivalent.
