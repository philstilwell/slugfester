Overall assessment

John Dominic Crossan — Metaphorical resurrection: 82/100

Published strengths

  • Distinguished source facts from theological interpretation and consistently emphasized the limits of historical recovery.
  • Used concrete Gospel redaction and Synoptic dependence to challenge uncomplicated eyewitness and independence claims.
  • Presented a clear positive account of Jesus's present kingdom, communal participation, and nonviolent mission.
  • Developed metaphorical resurrection as consequential communal vindication rather than empty symbolism.

Published weaknesses

  • The resurrection case often established ethical or theological significance without directly answering Pauline bodily transformation and early physicality evidence.
  • The Lazarus reading treated spiritual culmination as stronger evidence against literal narrative content than the passage demonstrated.

Michael Licona — Bodily resurrection: 84/100

Published strengths

  • Established a careful distinction among the real Jesus, the historical reconstruction, and the Christ of faith.
  • Presented concrete historical criteria and specific proposed eyewitness pathways rather than relying solely on confessional assertion.
  • Directly answered the nonliteral raising interpretation while conceding that investigability is not proof.
  • Used Pauline transformation language and early-source convergence to contact the central bodily-resurrection burden.

Published weaknesses

  • Several cumulative arguments depended on source independence that was asserted more confidently than it was demonstrated, especially regarding John.
  • The eschatological-judge argument relied on broad scholarly agreement and uncertain textual recollection without adequately presenting the underlying evidence.

Historical Method and Access to Jesus

12:14–31:22

John Dominic Crossan — section score 77/100

Historical facts remain distinguishable from interpretation but never interpretation-free, paralleling the dialectical relationships between faith and history and between metaphor and literal statement.

Strongest feature: The move usefully warns that historical facts arrive within interpretive frameworks and connects that insight coherently to the debate's recurring contrasts between faith and history, metaphor and literal statement. Principal limitation: The expansive language of inseparability and dialectical partnership remains underspecified, risking a collapse of important differences between evidentially constrained interpretation and comparatively discretionary theological construction. Live burden: The metaphorical-resurrection route must provide operational criteria showing how interpretations are compared, constrained, and sometimes rejected rather than merely observing that interpretation accompanies every historical judgment. Locked score: The strong and competent band recognizes a relevant, conceptually fertile methodological orientation with adequate coherence, while reflecting its partly unstable terminology, broad necessity claim, limited qualification, and absence of sustained engagement with a realist alternative.

Michael Licona — section score 84/100

Historical inquiry recovers only a partial historical Jesus, distinct from both the complete real person and the New Testament's Christ of faith.

Strongest feature: The distinction among the real Jesus, the necessarily incomplete historical reconstruction, and the Christ of faith is exceptionally intelligible, appropriately qualified, and methodologically productive for the debate's central evidentiary inquiry. Principal limitation: The passage illustrates partial recoverability effectively but supplies comparatively little independent justification for deciding which particular features of the confessional portrait survive historical examination. Live burden: The bodily-resurrection route must therefore apply this careful framework consistently, demonstrating rather than presuming that its later source claims cross the boundary from theological testimony into historically warranted conclusions. Locked score: The very strong band reflects excellent conceptual coherence, direct central relevance, and disciplined qualification, moderated by thinner evidentiary development and limited engagement with a competing methodological interpretation.

Gospel Witness, Redaction, and Independence

36:59–128:23

John Dominic Crossan — section score 89/100

Matthew and Luke reshape Mark, while divergent sayings from the cross reveal theological composition that limits confident recovery of eyewitness recollection.

Strongest feature: The reply directly contacts both eyewitness-lineage and historical-core arguments by using concrete redactional variation to distinguish possible source ancestry from confidence in recovering uninterpreted eyewitness recollection. Principal limitation: Although the examples demonstrate theological composition, they do not comprehensively rebut the specific Petrine and Johannine transmission claims or show that redaction prevents recovery of particular historical elements. Live burden: The metaphorical-resurrection route must move from demonstrated compositional freedom to discriminating judgments about which identity and resurrection traditions become historically uncertain, while acknowledging that interpretation need not erase underlying testimony. Locked score: The very strong band reflects exceptional responsiveness, precise limited scope, sound calibration, and substantial central relevance, with remaining restraint attributable to incomplete engagement with the opponent's concrete lineage evidence.

Michael Licona — section score 81/100

Mark and John plausibly preserve eyewitness-rooted material through Petrine tradition behind Mark and disciple-derived testimony used by John's author.

Strongest feature: The move offers concrete transmission pathways for two major Gospel traditions and carefully distinguishes direct Johannine authorship from an author's possible reliance upon a disciple as a primary source. Principal limitation: Its conclusion that the evidence is good exceeds the compressed documentation presented for Petrine derivation, disciple identification, transmission integrity, and the historical content recoverable after evangelistic composition. Live burden: The bodily-resurrection route must show that plausible eyewitness roots survive redaction strongly enough to authenticate the particular identity and resurrection claims placed upon them. Locked score: The strong and competent band reflects excellent relevance, explicit qualification, and charitable accommodation of disputed Johannine authorship, balanced against limited supporting demonstration and an evidentiary inference that remains somewhat more confident than the supplied passage warrants.

Jesus's Kingdom Mission and Divine Identity

48:48–129:11

John Dominic Crossan — section score 85/100

Jesus emphasized God's already-present kingdom, communal participation, and the governing vision rather than presenting himself as the expected military Messiah.

Strongest feature: The move supplies a clear positive identity account centered on present kingdom proclamation, communal participation, and Jesus's relative lack of self-focus, rather than relying only upon criticism of divine-identity claims. Principal limitation: The contrast with ordinary military-Davidic expectation remains historically compressed and does not establish that kingdom proclamation excludes implicit divine authority, nonmilitary messiahship, or elevated self-understanding. Live burden: The metaphorical route must connect this well-defined public emphasis to its stronger conclusions about incarnation and resurrection while addressing early texts that place Jesus himself within the proclaimed vision. Locked score: The very strong band reflects excellent clarity, calibration, constructive relevance, and explicit limitation to ordinary expectations, tempered by the inferential distance between predominant emphasis and the exclusion of competing identity claims.

Michael Licona — section score 79/100

Pauline preexistence and creation language, apostolic agreement, and Markan divine deeds support Jesus's uniquely divine identity beyond messiahship alone.

Strongest feature: The reply directly counters the claim that Jesus's identity is exhausted by kingdom proclamation, assembling specific Pauline and Markan categories involving preexistence, creation, divine action, sonship, and apostolic testimony. Principal limitation: It moves too quickly from what early followers attributed to Jesus toward what Jesus personally claimed, while the independence, dating, and alternative interpretation of the cited materials receive compressed treatment. Live burden: The bodily-resurrection route must distinguish follower christology from recoverable self-understanding and define uniquely divine consistently across deeds, status, preexistence, and personal identity. Locked score: The strong and competent band recognizes focused responsiveness, concrete source engagement, and indispensable central relevance, while reflecting elastic terminology, slightly overstated confidence, and an incompletely defended bridge from early portrayal to Jesus's own historical claim.

Miracles and Extraordinary-Event Inference

62:25–73:56

John Dominic Crossan — section score 80/100

Gospel raising stories do not persuasively establish literal restorations, and John's Lazarus narrative culminates by transforming the meaning of death and resurrection.

Strongest feature: The move identifies a real literary shift in the Lazarus narrative, where resurrection and undying life acquire theological significance beyond the story's preceding use of ordinary physical death. Principal limitation: Calling the episode a parody is insufficiently demonstrated, and a nonliteral theological culmination does not itself exclude a literal restoration within the narrative world or answer the event-cause distinction. Live burden: The metaphorical route must show why layered spiritual meaning defeats rather than supplements literal narration and must directly address whether extraordinary-event claims remain historically investigable without settled supernatural causation. Locked score: The mixed band reflects relevant literary evidence and a recoverable qualified proposition, but also reduced responsiveness, somewhat overstated interpretation, limited charity toward a both-and reading, and failure to contact the selected methodological challenge.

Michael Licona — section score 86/100

Historians may verify an extraordinary bodily resurrection event while leaving its supernatural cause historically undetermined.

Strongest feature: The move draws a clean and important analytical distinction between establishing that an extraordinary event occurred and identifying God or another supernatural agency as its cause. Principal limitation: Its hypothetical illustration establishes methodological possibility rather than providing the evidence required to verify Jesus's bodily resurrection, and the historical standards appropriate to such an extraordinary event remain unstated. Live burden: The bodily-resurrection route must now demonstrate that the proposed event is the best warranted account of the evidence independently of any causal conclusion, while explaining how ordinary historical comparison handles extreme rarity. Locked score: The strong and competent band reflects outstanding conceptual coherence, careful causal restraint, and useful subsidiary relevance, balanced against modest evidentiary development and the fact that the central event inference remains entirely uncompleted here.

Bodily and Metaphorical Resurrection

73:56–108:56

John Dominic Crossan — section score 79/100

Resurrection is a consequential participatory metaphor: communities embody Jesus's continuing life through transformed conduct, collective hope, and resistance to violence.

Strongest feature: The move articulates resurrection as communal participation rather than ornamental symbolism, connecting divine vindication, transformed conduct, collective endurance, and allegiance to Jesus's nonviolent vision. Principal limitation: This participatory account establishes a dimension of resurrection meaning, yet neither excludes bodily transformation nor interprets Paul's coordinated language concerning Christ's resurrection and the resurrection of believers. Live burden: The metaphorical interpretation must explain physicality claims and demonstrate that participatory transformation accounts for their emergence better than a bodily occurrence accompanied by communal consequences. Locked score: The strong and competent band reflects sophistication, motion-level relevance, and constructive coherence, while preserving deductions for insufficient historical warrant, limited engagement with physical-resurrection testimony, and the compatibility of bodily and metaphorical dimensions.

Michael Licona — section score 88/100

Paul coordinates Christ's raising with believers' future bodily resurrection and transformation, exceeding an account limited to changed communal conduct.

Strongest feature: The reply squarely contacts the bodily-versus-metaphorical dispute by coordinating Paul's claims about believers' bodies, future transformation, and Christ's own resurrection rather than reducing the language to changed behavior. Principal limitation: The reconstruction of spirits returning to bodies is more specific than the presented Pauline warrant, and transformed embodiment still requires careful distinction from ordinary resuscitation or unchanged physicality. Live burden: The bodily route must define the continuity and transformation involved, establish that Paul's language describes Christ historically as well as believers prospectively, and integrate this evidence with independent narrative testimony. Locked score: The very strong band reflects exceptional direct responsiveness, textual specificity, logical coherence, and central relevance, with modest restraint for an overly detailed anthropological reconstruction and insufficient acknowledgment of alternative models of transformed existence.
