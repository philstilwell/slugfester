Overall assessment

William Lane Craig — Historical evidence supports resurrection: 84/100

Published strengths

  • Clearly separated proposed historical facts from the disputed explanatory inference, giving the debate a disciplined cumulative structure.
  • Presented focused evidentiary cases for burial, the empty tomb, postmortem experiences, and the emergence of resurrection belief.
  • Correctly showed that low antecedent probability alone cannot settle posterior probability without considering specific evidence and alternatives.
  • Answered direct-access objections by distinguishing epistemic inaccessibility from inferential inadmissibility.
  • Used explanatory scope, power, plausibility, and contextual fit to engage the motion's comparative demand.

Published weaknesses

  • The cumulative case sometimes treated disputed source independence and broad scholarly agreement as more securely established than the presented defense warranted.
  • The final comparative claim remained compressed: defeating categorical exclusion did not itself establish favorable resurrection probability or superiority over the strongest alternatives.
  • The description of resurrection as extremely plausible exceeded the brief contextual comparison offered against rival theological interpretations.

Bart Ehrman — History cannot establish resurrection: 83/100

Published strengths

  • Established clear probabilistic source criteria and applied dating, authorship, transmission, interest, and inconsistency objections to the documentary foundation.
  • Distinguished generic early burial testimony from specific attestation of Joseph of Arimathea.
  • Offered literary functions for Joseph and the women that directly weakened arguments from narrative awkwardness.
  • Separated postmortem appearance reports from the stronger conclusion of an externally embodied resurrection.
  • Developed removal, visions, reinterpretation, and narrative growth as historically accessible sources of underdetermination.
  • Raised the significant problem of selecting one divine explanation over rival theological interpretations using shared historical criteria.

Published weaknesses

  • The claim that miracles are definitionally least probable conflated antecedent and posterior probability, making the methodological prohibition substantially too categorical.
  • Broad Gospel unreliability claims did not consistently distinguish diminished confidence from the failure of each particular tradition.
  • The removal-and-visions and developmental alternatives remained plausible sketches rather than fully evidenced comparative explanations.
  • The demand for standards acceptable across every worldview sometimes slid toward an unnecessarily universal consensus condition.

Historical Sources and Evidentiary Standards

2:57–52:31

William Lane Craig — section score 85/100

The historical case should distinguish comparatively accepted evidence from the disputed claim that resurrection best explains it.

Strongest feature: The framework lucidly separates evidentiary propositions from explanatory adjudication, furnishing an exceptionally relevant and methodologically disciplined architecture for the entire motion-level comparison. Principal limitation: Describing the evidence as relatively uncontroversial compresses substantial disagreements about attestation, independence, interpretation, and evidential sufficiency without documenting the representativeness of the invoked scholarly agreement. Live burden: The case must establish each proposed fact independently and then demonstrate that resurrection comparatively outperforms naturalistic and theological alternatives under defensible, consistently applicable explanatory criteria. Locked score: The very-strong band reflects outstanding conceptual organization, precision, and direct burden contact, tempered by underdeveloped substantiation for the foundational consensus characterization and limited engagement with source-critical qualifications.

Bart Ehrman — section score 85/100

Historical confidence increases with numerous, contemporary, independent, mutually consistent, corroborating, and disinterested documentary sources.

Strongest feature: The move articulates clear, recognizable source-evaluation criteria and appropriately frames historical conclusions as probabilistic judgments strengthened by contemporaneity, independence, corroboration, consistency, and disinterest. Principal limitation: It leaves implicit that these characteristics are comparative evidential advantages rather than exceptionless prerequisites, creating some risk that imperfect ancient evidence could be dismissed too mechanically. Live burden: The critique must apply each criterion proportionately to specific traditions, distinguish dependence from agreement, and explain how cumulative deficiencies alter confidence in each asserted fact. Locked score: The very-strong band reflects methodological clarity, excellent calibration, and direct relevance to the documentary foundation, with only modest limitations arising from underexplained weighting and the criteria's incompletely specified interaction.

Burial and the Empty Tomb

4:18–59:00

William Lane Craig — section score 84/100

Early traditions and the alleged improbability of invention support burial of Jesus in Joseph of Arimathea's identifiable tomb.

Strongest feature: The argument supplies an identifiable burial claim with multiple-attestation and invention-improbability considerations, directly supporting the physical foundation required by the resurrection explanation. Principal limitation: Its confident historical formulation exceeds the abbreviated demonstration that the cited traditions are genuinely independent or that Joseph's literary usefulness makes Christian invention sufficiently improbable. Live burden: The case must distinguish early attestation from independent corroboration while answering communal-burial possibilities and the contention that later narrative development could explain Joseph's prominence. Locked score: The strong band recognizes coherent, highly relevant evidentiary reasoning and meaningful anticipation of invention concerns, while preserving substantial reservations about source dependence, calibration, and incomplete engagement with the strongest literary-development alternative.

Bart Ehrman — section score 89/100

Paul's generic burial statement does not attest Joseph, and later Joseph traditions require consistently applied source standards.

Strongest feature: The reply carefully separates Paul's generic burial reference from specific attestation of Joseph and exposes the importance of applying consistent standards to early and later traditions. Principal limitation: Pauline silence weakens independent attestation without proving Joseph absent, and the proposed communal-burial possibility receives less affirmative evidentiary development than the source-critical objection. Live burden: The challenge must show why Joseph's later appearance is better explained through invention or development than preservation, while accounting proportionately for generic early burial testimony. Locked score: The very-strong band reflects outstanding precision, calibration, direct responsiveness, and source-methodological relevance, with limited remaining uncertainty concerning arguments from silence and the positive evidentiary basis for alternative burial arrangements.

Appearances and the Origin of Resurrection Belief

8:45–93:38

William Lane Craig — section score 83/100

Early Pauline testimony, named recipients, group reports, and narratives support postmortem experiences of Jesus by individuals and groups.

Strongest feature: The move assembles early Pauline testimony, named recipients, group reports, and narrative traditions into a highly relevant evidentiary case that postmortem experiences require explanation. Principal limitation: The language of appearances and experiences remains ontologically underdetermined, so strong occurrence evidence does not independently establish external, embodied encounters rather than visionary phenomena. Live burden: The case must defend the reports' independence, clarify what experiential content the earliest evidence supports, and explain why collective or individual visionary interpretations are comparatively inadequate. Locked score: The very-strong band recognizes excellent burden contact, coherent cumulative presentation, and substantial early attestation, while withholding stronger endorsement because the bodily character and causal interpretation remain insufficiently demonstrated.

Bart Ehrman — section score 85/100

Ancient appearance reports need not entail a reanimated body, so they cannot alone establish bodily resurrection or divine action.

Strongest feature: The reply clearly separates evidence that people reported postmortem appearances from the stronger conclusion that they encountered an externally embodied, divinely resurrected Jesus. Principal limitation: Characterizing the target as reanimation risks understating the proponent's transformed-body conception, while extraordinary narrative properties still require more granular source and developmental analysis. Live burden: The objection must explain which earliest reports are compatible with visions, how group traditions should be evaluated, and whether embodied details represent independent memory or theological elaboration. Locked score: The very-strong band recognizes decisive underdetermination, excellent conceptual precision, and direct contact with the appearance inference, subject to a terminology concern and unresolved evidentiary questions about collective and physical details.

Miracle Probability and the Scope of History

31:58–75:46

William Lane Craig — section score 87/100

Resurrection's posterior probability depends on background information, specific evidence, and the comparative probability of naturalistic alternatives.

Strongest feature: The reply precisely diagnoses that antecedent improbability alone cannot determine posterior probability because specific evidence and the comparative performance of non-resurrection alternatives also matter. Principal limitation: Correcting the opponent's incomplete probability analysis does not itself demonstrate favorable resurrection probability, especially without defensible priors, likelihood estimates, or adequately established evidentiary inputs. Live burden: The case must move beyond formal completeness by showing that the relevant evidence is substantially more expected under resurrection than under the strongest competing hypotheses. Locked score: The very-strong band reflects exceptional logical coherence, calibration, responsiveness, and methodological importance, tempered only by the distinction between defeating a categorical objection and positively establishing the proposed conclusion.

Bart Ehrman — section score 75/100

Because historians establish probable events and miracles are defined as least probable, historical method cannot establish a miracle as probable.

Strongest feature: The argument squarely identifies the methodological tension between history's probabilistic conclusions and a miracle hypothesis presented as an extraordinarily improbable departure from ordinary experience. Principal limitation: Defining miracle as the least probable occurrence conflates low antecedent probability with posterior assessment and effectively assumes the exclusion that the argument needs independently to justify. Live burden: The position must explain how historians should incorporate specific discriminating evidence, competing explanations, theological priors, and uncertainty without transforming methodological caution into categorical prohibition. Locked score: The mixed band reflects exceptional relevance and diagnostic directness but substantial logical and evidential limitations arising from equivocal probability language, missing conditional analysis, and an overstated methodological boundary.

Competing Explanations, Underdetermination, and Context

16:30–107:09

William Lane Craig — section score 78/100

Resurrection allegedly explains burial, emptiness, appearances, and belief with greater scope, power, and plausibility than naturalistic alternatives.

Strongest feature: The move explicitly reaches the motion by applying explanatory scope, power, and plausibility to a unified hypothesis addressing burial, emptiness, appearances, and emergent belief. Principal limitation: Claiming no better explanation materially outruns the abbreviated comparison, especially because several foundational facts remain disputed and rival naturalistic or theological hypotheses receive limited individualized assessment. Live burden: The argument must quantify or otherwise discipline comparative plausibility, defend divine agency's admissibility, and evaluate the strongest alternatives against whichever facts survive source criticism. Locked score: The strong band reflects excellent relevance and an intelligible explanatory framework, constrained by compressed rival analysis, insufficiently defended priors, and an overconfident transition from cumulative adequacy to comparative superiority.

Bart Ehrman — section score 84/100

If burial and tomb claims remain disputed, ordinary body removal followed by visions offers a historically accessible alternative.

Strongest feature: The reply contacts both factual and comparative burdens by combining targeted disputes over burial and emptiness with a historically accessible removal-and-visions scenario. Principal limitation: Although appropriately modal, the alternative's component probabilities, causal sequence, evidentiary support, and explanatory coordination remain schematic rather than comparatively demonstrated. Live burden: The account must explain who removed the body, why discovery or correction did not follow, how individual and group experiences developed, and whether the combination is less ad hoc. Locked score: The strong band reflects excellent responsiveness, disciplined calibration, and genuine underdetermining force, tempered by limited affirmative support and an underdeveloped comparison between the scenario's multiple suppositions and the proposed unity of resurrection.
