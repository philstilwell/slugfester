Overall assessment

Trent Horn — The evidence supports their sincerity: 86/100

Published strengths

  • Horn narrows the martyrdom argument to unequal, person-specific evidence instead of defending every later apostolic death tradition.
  • He correctly separates sincerity from objective resurrection truth and identifies foreseeable suffering before arrest as the stronger evidential test.
  • He combines Acts, Paul, Josephus, Tacitus, and community context while repeatedly acknowledging where motive or continuity remains inferential.

Published weaknesses

  • Horn sometimes moves too quickly from collective apostolic language to continuous hazardous activity by every member of the eleven.
  • His use of later Roman procedures and later church discipline does not securely reconstruct Nero’s decisions or first-century recantation costs.

Paulogia — The evidence is insufficient for all: 87/100

Published strengths

  • Paulogia consistently separates evidence of death, motive, opportunity to recant, public preaching, and sincere firsthand experience.
  • He grants strong points, including Horn’s nuance and Paul’s sincerity, while preserving the narrower burden concerning every original witness.
  • He exposes cumulative uncertainty when sparse individual evidence is expanded through group labels, silence, and analogies into a universal conclusion.

Published weaknesses

  • Paulogia’s trauma account of Paul is expressly speculative and offers no case-specific evidence capable of improving comparative probability.
  • His minimal-experiences scenario remains too schematic to explain the early collective formulas, named leaders, and precise development of group claims.

Nero and Roman procedure

20:44–32:26

Trent Horn — section score 86/100

Horn reads Tacitus as indicating that Christians were punished not merely for Rome’s fire but also for their hated identity and alleged hostility toward humankind.

Strongest feature: Horn carefully notes that Tacitus does not portray every Christian punishment as a simple arson conviction, since hated Christian identity and the accusation of hostility toward humankind figure in the described persecution. Principal limitation: Tacitus names neither Peter nor Paul, records no individual examination, and provides no evidence that resurrection testimony or refusal to recant determined either leader’s fate under Nero. Live burden: Horn must connect the general environment of belief-directed hostility to identifiable choices and escape opportunities for particular witnesses, while Paulogia must allow that Christian identity itself could create genuine danger. Locked score: 85 recognizes a strong correction to an overly simple scapegoat account and responsible textual use, moderated because the passage establishes dangerous context much more securely than the individualized sincerity test under debate.

Paulogia — section score 86/100

Paulogia argues that Nero’s need for conspicuous scapegoats could make Peter’s or Paul’s recantation irrelevant, weakening death as evidence that either voluntarily maintained testimony.

Strongest feature: Paulogia shows that Nero’s political need for conspicuous victims could make the private conviction or last-minute denial of prominent Christians irrelevant, directly challenging the assumed inference from execution to voluntary steadfastness. Principal limitation: This remains an undocumented reconstruction of Peter’s and Paul’s treatment, and political scapegoating could coexist with ideological hostility, ordinary interrogation, or opportunities to disengage earlier. Live burden: Horn must provide case-specific reason to believe recantation, sacrifice, flight, or withdrawal would probably have prevented punishment; Paulogia need only show that an effective escape route is unestablished. Recantation, status, and accepted risk

32:25–43:34

Trent Horn — section score 87/100

Horn reframes the argument around leaders who continued public association despite knowing that Christian identification could bring mob violence, arrest, or death before any formal recantation scene.

Strongest feature: Horn improves the sincerity argument by shifting attention from undocumented courtroom recantation scenes to leaders who continued visible Christian activity after warnings, beatings, arrests, or circulating knowledge that identification could bring violence. Principal limitation: General awareness of danger does not establish every unnamed apostle’s later activity, perceived exposure, practical alternatives, or persistence, and people can accept risk for motives besides firsthand conviction. Live burden: Horn must document conduct and foreseeable cost person by person, then connect persistence specifically to resurrection belief; Paulogia must address anticipatory exposure separately from doubts about execution procedure. Locked score: 88 reflects a historically more defensible and dialectically responsive formulation, with a measured deduction because its force remains concentrated in documented leaders rather than the entire apostolic roster.

Paulogia — section score 85/100

Paulogia asks whether an available escape through denial would weaken the claim that ordinary participants incurred serious risk simply by joining or preaching within the movement.

Principal limitation: Recantation could still involve detention, humiliation, destroyed relationships, lost leadership, and uncertain official acceptance, so its availability would reduce rather than eliminate the evidential cost of participation. Live burden: Horn must establish how accessible and effective recantation was in each setting and why its social costs meaningfully tested sincerity; Paulogia must avoid assuming a universal, costless escape. Experience categories and sincerity scope

44:06–54:07

Trent Horn — section score 91/100

Horn clarifies that willingness to suffer addresses whether witnesses sincerely reported perceived resurrection experiences, not whether those experiences objectively verify a bodily resurrection.

Strongest feature: Horn sharply confines the martyrdom reasoning to whether alleged witnesses sincerely reported perceived resurrection experiences, thereby excluding irrelevant comparisons with later martyrs who inherited beliefs and avoiding a direct claim that suffering proves bodily resurrection. Principal limitation: Sincerity alone cannot determine an experience’s independence, sensory content, physicality, or cause, and Paul does not occupy exactly the same experiential category as pre-Ascension disciples. Live burden: Horn must identify who personally claimed what and show why costly conduct authenticates that report; Paulogia should contest those links without requiring this argument to prove objective resurrection. Locked score: 92 reflects outstanding conceptual precision, responsiveness, and protection against a common straw objection, with the small deduction reserved for unresolved variation among the firsthand claims grouped together.

Community memory and final inference

1:12:18–1:22:15

Trent Horn — section score 85/100

Horn argues that early Christians named defectors as warnings, so conspicuous abandonment by original apostles would likely have been noticed rather than silently concealed.

Strongest feature: Horn develops a plausible expectation that repudiation by a foundational eyewitness would be memorable and polemically useful, supporting continuity through comparison with Christian texts that explicitly name defectors as warnings. Principal limitation: Partisan sources preserve information selectively, their dating and authorship may be disputed, and quiet withdrawal need not produce the conspicuous repudiation that Horn’s expectation requires. Live burden: Horn must establish community knowledge, incentives, and reliable transmission before silence can support all eleven; Paulogia must acknowledge that visible defection by a major witness would probably matter. Locked score: 82 credits a relevant argument from expected evidence and an apt internal comparison, while the moderate score reflects substantial preservation bias and the inability of silence to prove lifelong preaching or dangerous persistence.

Paulogia says the unnamed disciples’ disappearance from reliable history is compatible with continued ministry, quiet membership, fishing, withdrawal, or later appropriation of their names.

Strongest feature: Paulogia correctly observes that the later silence about most disciples is compatible with continued ministry, private membership, return to ordinary work, withdrawal, or continued symbolic use of their names, preventing one outcome from being assumed. Principal limitation: These possibilities are not equally probable, and early collective activity plus stable group memory provides some positive evidence against treating later status as wholly unconstrained. Live burden: Horn must produce continuity evidence that narrows the range; Paulogia must keep illustrative possibilities hypothetical and incorporate the evidence preceding disappearance. Locked score: 86 reflects a disciplined defense of underdetermination and a useful check on narrative completion, reduced because multiplying possibilities does less evidential work than comparing their relative fit and can slide toward the agnosticism Horn appropriately criticizes.
