Overall assessment

Matt Dillahunty — Secular humanism is better for society: 83/100

Published strengths

  • Dillahunty consistently keeps the comparison focused on human flourishing in this life and asks how each framework guides public decisions.
  • His distinction between legal recognition and what a system says people should receive clarifies how institutions can violate their stated equality.
  • He answers the voting challenge with careful qualifications for individual disqualification, universal abolition, and impermissible sex-based exclusion.
  • His insistence that intuitions be tested rather than merely harmonized supplies a clear standard for revising moral judgments.

Published weaknesses

  • He declares equal human status as foundational without fully explaining why species membership carries that universal normative force.
  • He often asserts better secular outcomes and Christian present-life costs without supplying comparative evidence strong enough for the motion.
  • His claim that rights exist only through collective protection risks collapsing moral entitlement into enforceable legal recognition.

Randal Rauser — Christianity is better for society: 84/100

Published strengths

  • Rauser defines a qualified mere Christianity, concedes Christian failures, and builds from substantial common ground with secular humanism.
  • His image-of-God account directly addresses persons lacking developed capacities, self-valuation, or recognition by surrounding communities.
  • He exposes the distinction between possessing a moral entitlement and having institutions codify or protect that entitlement.
  • His account of fallible properly basic intuitions answers the charge that intuition must be treated as either infallible or rationalized.

Published weaknesses

  • He repeatedly shows coherence between Christian premises and moral judgments without independently establishing that this coherence yields superior social outcomes.
  • His historical and theological examples demonstrate possible Christian motivation, but do not compare representative results across the two frameworks.
  • The physics analogy obscures the difference between revising empirical beliefs and remaining consistent with a defining equality commitment.

Frameworks for present human flourishing

2:01–33:39

Matt Dillahunty — section score 85/100

Dillahunty defines the comparison around human flourishing during this life, making practical consequences for people sharing the world the motion’s controlling measure.

Strongest feature: Dillahunty establishes a precise, socially relevant criterion by centering the lives people presently share, thereby preventing speculative afterlife benefits from automatically deciding the comparison. Principal limitation: A present-life focus identifies what should be measured but does not itself demonstrate that secular humanism produces superior outcomes, especially because Christianity can also claim current practices of care, reconciliation, and solidarity. Live burden: He must connect human-centered assessment to comparative evidence showing that its institutions, correction methods, or incentives reliably improve flourishing beyond the qualified Christianity Rauser defends. Locked score: 85 The framing is clear, responsive, and indispensable to the motion, yet its considerable argumentative value remains primarily criterial until outcome claims receive stronger support.

Randal Rauser — section score 85/100

Rauser limits his defense to creed-centered discipleship of Jesus, concedes severe Christian failures, and argues that this qualified tradition better cultivates shared social goods.

Strongest feature: Rauser states a disciplined target, openly concedes that many Christians have performed worse than humanists, and prevents the defense from depending on every historical expression of Christianity. Principal limitation: The boundary of “mere Christianity” is partly stipulated, creating a risk that damaging manifestations are excluded while favorable exemplars represent the tradition, and concessions alone do not prove superiority. Live burden: He must show that creed-centered discipleship has identifiable, non-selective features that predict social benefit despite Christian diversity and institutional failure. Locked score: 86 The qualification responsibly addresses obvious counterexamples, but its evidential legitimacy ultimately depends on principled membership criteria that do not select favorable Christian examples while excluding damaging manifestations.

Revision, public reasons, and practical guidance

5:34–34:56

Matt Dillahunty — section score 81/100

Humanism may adopt demonstrably beneficial practices from any source and revise its methods through evidence, discussion, and debate when better consequences become available.

Strongest feature: Dillahunty presents revisability as an operational advantage, explaining how humanism can absorb successful practices from Christianity or elsewhere without preserving an unhelpful doctrinal package. Principal limitation: The account leaves “better results” and the procedure for resolving conflicts among autonomy, happiness, health, and equality insufficiently specified, so openness can describe adaptability without guaranteeing sound correction. Live burden: He must show how humanist communities rank competing goods, identify reliable evidence, and prevent fashionable majorities from redefining improvement against vulnerable people. Locked score: 84 The move gives secular humanism a plausible learning mechanism and directly answers fixed-dogma concerns, but its comparative force depends on a more determinate method for adjudicating contested values.

Randal Rauser — section score 81/100

Rauser asks whether humanism weakens environmental and animal concern by valuing nature, sentient creatures, and ecosystems only through their contribution to human flourishing.

Strongest feature: Rauser probes a genuine boundary of an explicitly human-centered ethic and asks whether derivative valuation can protect nonhuman beings and ecosystems when immediate human interests point elsewhere. Principal limitation: The motion concerns which framework better serves human society, so limited intrinsic scope does not itself show practical inferiority, and derivative concern may still generate extensive environmental and animal protection. Live burden: He must identify cases where Christian intrinsic or creation-based value yields better social guidance than humanism’s cooperative, ecological, and sentience-sensitive reasons. Locked score: 81 The question usefully tests conceptual reach, but it remains one step removed from the motion because derivative value can still support strong protection when ecological health and animal welfare affect enduring human interests.

Human worth and equal status

8:11–55:53

Matt Dillahunty — section score 83/100

Beginning from human flourishing directs society toward equal status across sex and ethnicity, bodily sovereignty, property equality, and personal authority over life’s purpose.

Strongest feature: Dillahunty translates an abstract human-centered outlook into concrete stakes involving legal status, bodily control, property, ethnicity, sex, and self-directed purpose, making the social comparison intelligible. Principal limitation: His contrast relies substantially on his former religious experience and the possibility of God-focused priorities, although divine orientation need not logically exclude attention to human interests or equal treatment. Live burden: He must establish that humanism grounds these commitments more securely or implements them more consistently than Rauser’s egalitarian mere Christianity, rather than simply announcing them as humanist values. Locked score: 81 The move supplies valuable practical specificity and a coherent direction of travel, but the claimed comparative advantage remains under-supported by representative institutional evidence or a deeper account of equality.

Randal Rauser — section score 86/100

The image of God grounds value across the human species independently of developed reason, language, imagination, self-consciousness, usefulness, recognition, or subjective self-valuation.

Strongest feature: Rauser directly addresses marginal cases by locating worth in a relation shared across the species, thereby avoiding standards that fluctuate with cognitive achievement, usefulness, or another person’s approval. Principal limitation: The Mark Twain stamp analogy illustrates transferred significance but cannot establish the divine relation, God’s evaluative authority, or why bearing that image generates equal moral status in the public comparison. Live burden: He must defend the grounding premise sufficiently for social use without shifting the agreed motion into a demand to prove Christianity true. Locked score: 83 The account coherently protects vulnerable humans more explicitly than capacity theories, although its public comparative force remains conditional on a disputed theological relation whose practical adoption outside the tradition is unexplained.

Moral intuition and warrant

19:23–51:37

Matt Dillahunty — section score 83/100

Moral intuitions should be investigated for correctness and consequences rather than rationalized, while unusual facts can change life-saving choices and rights remain defeasible for sufficient reasons.

Strongest feature: Dillahunty draws an important methodological distinction between seeking reasons that confirm an intuition and testing whether it survives counterexamples, consequences, and wider evidence. Principal limitation: Adding cancer-curing chimp blood changes the sparse hypothetical rather than evaluating the original judgment, and extended asides about dilemmas, divine plans, and punishment diffuse the central epistemic proposal. Live burden: He must state a workable testing procedure and explain how its results interact with the equal-status default when intuitions and predicted consequences diverge. Locked score: 83 The move advances a valuable anti-rationalization standard and appropriate fallibility, but its application remains improvised where incomplete information and uncertain consequences make moral correction neither immediate nor socially neutral.

Randal Rauser — section score 82/100

Original sin explains persistent individual and collective moral failure without fatalism, encouraging sober vigilance about anyone’s capacity to participate in grave social wrongdoing.

Strongest feature: Rauser gives original sin a practical, nonfatalistic interpretation as disciplined suspicion of personal and collective virtue, using moral catastrophe to motivate humility rather than resignation. Principal limitation: Human fallibility and susceptibility to atrocities are available to secular psychology and history as well, so the examples support vigilance without showing that this theological doctrine uniquely or more effectively cultivates it. Live burden: He must connect the doctrine’s specific content and communal practice to measurably better self-correction than humanist recognition of bias and institutional danger. Locked score: 79 The doctrine receives a serious social function, yet secular access to the same lesson about fallibility and the absence of matched institutional outcomes substantially limit the claimed Christian advantage in cultivating vigilance.

Rights, entitlement, and codification

41:34–1:07:44

Matt Dillahunty — section score 83/100

Humanists protect every human through equal rights and privileges they would want themselves, regardless of disability, while allowing separate debate about voluntary end-of-life choices.

Strongest feature: Dillahunty supplies a clear policy commitment that protects the infant as a human being and refuses to make actual cognitive capacity or another person’s affection a prerequisite for rights. Principal limitation: Reciprocal language about protections “we would like” shifts from intrinsic worth to a chosen social rule, and the assisted-dying aside distracts from the involuntary infant case without answering its grounding challenge. Live burden: He must explain why the universal policy binds when collective preference changes and distinguish voluntary autonomy from decisions imposed on nonconsenting vulnerable people. Locked score: 79 The practical answer blocks any inference that humanism abandons disabled infants, but its unnecessary assisted-dying aside and unexplained policy universality leave the central philosophical ground of protection insufficiently developed.

Randal Rauser — section score 83/100

Because God is identified with maximal goodness, image-bearing confers value, while the right not to be tortured should remain real even when a collective refuses recognition.

Strongest feature: Rauser joins his positive grounding claim to a forceful counterexample, showing why moral criticism seems to require entitlements that remain valid when law and public opinion become vicious. Principal limitation: Defining God as maximal goodness does not by itself establish that God exists, creates image-bearers, or transmits equal status, and calling Dillahunty’s question confused dismisses a legitimate explanatory request too quickly. Live burden: He must articulate the relation between divine goodness and human entitlement while preserving the agreed practical scope rather than demanding worldview proof. Locked score: 83 The torture example powerfully distinguishes objective entitlement from codification, but the proposed alternative remains under-defended until a credible account connects divine goodness, image-bearing, and each person’s equal claims.

Equality and the limits of revision

39:48–1:06:55

Matt Dillahunty — section score 86/100

Biblical rules concerning property, church speech, and wifely submission appear hierarchical, so Christianity must reject that hierarchy or explain why it benefits society.

Strongest feature: Dillahunty names concrete textual pressures instead of making a generic accusation, and he fairly allows that Rauser may defend an egalitarian Christianity rather than treating every Christian interpretation alike. Principal limitation: Different roles do not by themselves entail unequal human worth, and selected passages cannot establish how representative, necessary, or socially dominant the hierarchy is within Rauser’s qualified framework. Live burden: He must show that the cited structure genuinely compromises equal status or outcomes and then compare it with humanism’s own institutional failures. Locked score: 83 The objection is relevant and carefully conditional, but moving from disputed role prescriptions to systematic inequality requires stronger interpretive and comparative support across the textual and institutional forms under discussion.

Randal Rauser — section score 84/100

Genesis presents male and female together as God’s image, supporting egalitarian status, while the claim that women should vote implies entitlement prior to legal recognition.

Strongest feature: Rauser directly rejects complementarianism for his defended framework and then uses Dillahunty’s own normative wording to reopen the distinction between an existing moral claim and delayed institutional acknowledgment. Principal limitation: The egalitarian reading is contested and not shown uniquely required by Christianity, while historical revolutionary significance and theological assertions receive no independent verification within the debate. Live burden: He must demonstrate that image-bearing explains equal civic entitlement more securely than a humanist equality axiom and that his interpretation has practical formative force. Locked score: 86 The two-part answer is responsive and philosophically consequential, though its scriptural foundation and comparative social effectiveness remain conditional on Rauser’s preferred interpretation rather than independently established.

Solidarity, self-denial, and hope

9:41–1:09:31

Matt Dillahunty — section score 80/100

Humanists should assess actions by generalizing their social effects, seeking cooperation, happiness, health, autonomy, and minimal imposed constraint in a shared world.

Strongest feature: Dillahunty offers a recognizable practical test by asking what society would become if conduct were generally permitted, linking personal freedom to reciprocal effects on everyone sharing social space. Principal limitation: The test gathers several attractive goods without explaining how to decide when happiness, health, autonomy, cooperation, and limited government conflict, and the universalization illustration remains informal rather than demonstrative. Live burden: He must specify priority rules or institutional safeguards that convert this cluster into guidance for hard cases and then compare its reliability with Christian moral formation. Locked score: 81 The proposal is accessible and action-guiding at an introductory level, yet unresolved tradeoffs and limited comparative evidence constrain its capacity to establish humanism’s overall superiority.

Randal Rauser — section score 86/100

Rauser begins from shared commitments to humility, human nobility, and solidarity, arguing that Christianity overlaps substantially with humanism before claiming stronger cultivation.

Strongest feature: Rauser avoids a false dichotomy by granting extensive moral common ground, which makes his later comparison about cultivation rather than caricature and accurately acknowledges humanist aspirations. Principal limitation: Establishing overlap shows compatibility, not that Christianity produces these goods more effectively, and selected scriptural or historical illustrations cannot establish typical causal outcomes across communities. Live burden: He must explain what distinctive Christian mechanisms strengthen humility, nobility, and solidarity, then provide comparative reason to expect those mechanisms to outperform humanist analogues. Locked score: 86 The charitable framing materially improves the debate and identifies a fair target, although the superiority inference still needs evidence that ordinary Christian communities cultivate these shared values more reliably than comparable secular organizations.
