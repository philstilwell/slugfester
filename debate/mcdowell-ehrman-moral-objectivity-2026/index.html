Overall assessment

Sean McDowell — Moral realist: 86/100

Published strengths

  • McDowell repeatedly distinguished descriptive historical transformation from genuine moral improvement, keeping obligation and normative authority at the center of the motion.
  • His strongest replies granted Ehrman's historical claims before explaining why novelty, cognitive mediation, or evolutionary origin does not by itself decide moral objectivity.
  • The challenge from contingent evolutionary feelings to comparative authority directly contacted the con account's weakest bridge and carefully limited itself to insufficiency without denying every naturalistic supplement.
  • McDowell reframed cross-cultural commonality and historical transformation as comparative explanatory evidence rather than presenting either observation as a deductive proof of theism.

Published weaknesses

  • The proposed access route classified moral truths as immaterial without supplying a positive method for knowing them, leaving the epistemic defense substantially incomplete.
  • The guilt argument compressed psychological guilt, objective wrongdoing, theological sin, and forgiveness into one explanatory package that the presented evidence did not uniquely support.

Bart Ehrman — Secular historian: 82/100

Published strengths

  • Ehrman clearly specified Jesus's universalization of outgroup care and connected it to a historically consequential transformation without claiming that history settled metaethics.
  • The subjective-access objection directly exposed the missing epistemic step between defining mind-independent facts and establishing dependable human knowledge of moral truth.
  • His evolutionary account of guilt supplied a genuine alternative mechanism and thereby weakened the claim that guilt uniquely indicates an objective moral code.
  • The late scope clarification carefully separated a historical thesis available to believers and nonbelievers from stronger conclusions about objectivity or divine grounding.

Published weaknesses

  • The appeal to constitutive human inclination did not distinguish causal motivation from normative justification or explain how conflicting inclinations acquire comparative authority.
  • The claim that universal aid was absent through most human existence had sweeping temporal scope without representative historical or archaeological support in the locked evidence.
  • Rejecting a deductive inference from cross-cultural commonality did not answer McDowell's narrower claim that commonality might carry comparative explanatory weight.

Objective Truth and Human Access

10:03–17:36

Sean McDowell — section score 81/100

Moral objectivism means that moral facts remain true independently of anyone's beliefs, preferences, or attitudes, comparably to mind-independent historical and scientific facts.

Strongest feature: The move provides a precise and recoverable definition of moral objectivism, separating truth from individual belief, preference, and attitude while using familiar historical and scientific comparisons to clarify the intended independence relation. Principal limitation: As a reply, this definitional clarification does not contact the decisive historical claim that Jesus's teaching caused Western charity, and it does not itself establish either moral facts or reliable access to them. Live burden: The argument must connect the intelligible concept of mind-independent morality to positive grounding and explain why rejecting divine grounding need not be reduced to individual-opinion subjectivism. Locked score: The strong/competent band recognizes conceptual clarity, stable terminology, appropriate calibration, and central relevance, while retaining the substantial responsiveness limitation created by leaving the historical target unanswered.

Bart Ehrman — section score 84/100

Even if objective moral values exist, people can recognize them only through subjective human cognition and therefore possess no unmediated access to moral objectivity.

Strongest feature: The conditional formulation grants objective value for argument's sake and sharply identifies the missing epistemic step between mind-independent truth and human knowledge, directly challenging the practical consequence of McDowell's definition. Principal limitation: Establishing that cognition is mediated by human brains does not establish that the resulting access is unreliable, inadequate, or uniquely problematic, because historical and scientific knowledge is likewise cognitively mediated. Live burden: The objection must identify a relevant reliability deficit in moral cognition rather than relying exclusively upon the universal fact that human judgment occurs through subjective faculties. Locked score: The strong/competent band reflects exceptionally direct contact, stable distinctions, and diagnostic importance, tempered by an inference from necessary mediation toward epistemic limitation that remains somewhat stronger than its evidence warrants.

Cross-Cultural Commonality and Historical Variation

26:15–38:55

Sean McDowell — section score 82/100

Cross-cultural moral principles can endure while practices change, with Jesus expanding their application toward poor, disenfranchised, and outsider populations rather than originating morality itself.

Strongest feature: The principle-application distinction provides a coherent reconciliation between historical transformation and enduring morality, allowing Jesus's contribution to expand the scope of concern without requiring that every underlying moral principle began with him. Principal limitation: The claims that common principles existed across cultures and "from the beginning" remain historically broad, while the move does not show that earlier principles genuinely entailed later outgroup assistance rather than receiving retrospective reinterpretation. Live burden: McDowell must identify stable principles with representative cross-cultural evidence and explain the inferential bridge from those principles to universal charity. Locked score: The strong/competent band recognizes a useful conceptual distinction and relevant partial answer, while reflecting underqualified historical scope, limited support, and the continuing gap between commonality and objectivity.

Bart Ehrman — section score 77/100

Treating assistance to hungry and homeless strangers as a central moral priority appears historically recent rather than evident throughout most of Homo sapiens' existence.

Strongest feature: The move supplies a concrete instance of historical variation by contrasting a personally central commitment to aiding hungry and homeless strangers with the claimed absence of that priority across most human history. Principal limitation: The enormous temporal generalization extends far beyond the representative historical or archaeological evidence presented, and absence from surviving evidence cannot straightforwardly establish absence from human concern or practice. Live burden: Ehrman must define the relevant priority, distinguish institutional visibility from moral recognition, and substantiate the claim across heterogeneous societies without conflating limited documentation with nonexistence. Locked score: The mixed band reflects genuine relevance to historical contingency and a clearly identified value, alongside sweeping scope, inadequate displayed support, and failure to answer the immediate normative challenge concerning standards for progress.

Jesus and the Universalization of Charity

1:26–43:20

Sean McDowell — section score 92/100

Jesus's introduction of a universal ethic does not make morality subjective; care for poor people can instead rest on their objective dignity as bearers of God's image.

Strongest feature: The reply grants Jesus's historical role, correctly denies that historical novelty logically entails subjectivity, and presents a coherent positive grounding for universal concern in the value and dignity of persons made in God's image. Principal limitation: The divine-image proposal is asserted rather than independently defended, so the move explains the realist position without yet demonstrating that this grounding is preferable to secular accounts of human worth. Live burden: McDowell must support the theological premise and show how it produces determinate obligations while acknowledging that late emergence can remain evidence about recognition even without settling metaethics. Locked score: The very strong band reflects comprehensive contact, explicit distinctions, charitable treatment of the historical evidence, and a substantive grounding proposal, moderated by its undeveloped theological warrant.

Bart Ehrman — section score 86/100

Jesus taught an unusually universal responsibility to help unrelated people in need, and sustained Christian influence subsequently embedded that ethic within Western moral consciousness.

Strongest feature: The historically focused causal thesis directly supports the motion by identifying Jesus's universalized charity as the proposed origin of a consequential transformation in Western moral consciousness, while the phrase "so far as we can tell" appropriately acknowledges historical uncertainty. Principal limitation: The selected evidence supplies little comparative documentation for the unusually broad claims about earlier philosophers, Jewish tradition, or the many intervening centuries through which this influence supposedly operated. Live burden: The account must distinguish Jesus's contribution from antecedent traditions and demonstrate a credible transmission pathway from teaching to durable social conscience. Locked score: The strong/competent band reflects a clear, relevant, cautiously expressed thesis whose considerable historical scope remains incompletely substantiated within the presented support.

Inclination, Obligation, and Moral Progress

22:30–49:44

Sean McDowell — section score 90/100

Claims that beliefs are superior or historical changes constitute moral progress require a standard beyond contingent evolved inclinations if those judgments are categorical rather than preference-relative.

Strongest feature: The move exposes a consequential standard-of-comparison problem by placing Ehrman's inclination-based account beside his reported descriptions of superior beliefs and moral progress, then asking what could authorize those evaluations. Principal limitation: The apparent inconsistency depends upon interpreting "superior" and "progress" categorically, although both expressions could instead describe improvement relative to endorsed human purposes such as reducing suffering or expanding cooperation. Live burden: McDowell must show why purpose-relative evaluation is insufficient for the claims at issue and provide a positive account of how an objective standard grounds comparisons without merely restating objectivity. Locked score: The very strong band reflects precise diagnostic pressure, direct central engagement, and considerable normative importance, with an implicit interpretive condition preventing the challenge from becoming conclusive.

Bart Ehrman — section score 79/100

Human inclinations can present conduct as unquestionably right or wrong, but that psychological certainty does not demonstrate an objective standard beyond human inclination.

Strongest feature: The concise distinction between the force with which an inclination presents itself and the metaphysical status of its content correctly denies that felt certainty alone entails a mind-independent moral standard. Principal limitation: Although it contacts McDowell's definitional distinction, the response leaves unanswered his more recent domain-specific proposal and offers no developed account of why some inclinations deserve endorsement when they conflict. Live burden: Ehrman must supplement the non-entailment point with an epistemic and normative account explaining how human inclinations can be evaluated, prioritized, or corrected without presupposing external authority. Locked score: The strong/competent band reflects a logically proportionate denial, stable terminology, and meaningful central contact, while preserving the responsiveness deficit created by bypassing the latest methodological challenge.

Guilt, Moral Practice, and Explanatory Scope

59:44–63:14

Sean McDowell — section score 84/100

Guilt, conduct that presupposes a real moral code, and the perceived need for forgiveness may be better explained by objective wrongdoing than by inclination alone.

Strongest feature: The move introduces familiar moral phenomena into a comparative explanation and explicitly invites an alternative account, connecting guilt, realist-seeming practice, wrongdoing, and forgiveness to the broader grounding dispute. Principal limitation: The concepts of guilt, sin, moral code, and forgiveness combine distinct psychological, normative, and theological claims, while the phenomenology underdetermines whether its explanation is realist, evolutionary, cultural, or mixed. Live burden: McDowell must separate these phenomena, establish their relevant features, and show why objective wrongdoing explains them better than adaptive social emotions and learned communal norms. Locked score: The strong/competent band reflects a relevant and recoverable constructive challenge with comparative potential, tempered by conceptual compression, implicit qualifications, limited discriminating evidence, and an explanatory conclusion stronger than the presented observations uniquely support.

Bart Ehrman — section score 85/100

Guilt may have evolved because people disposed against harming their communities survived more successfully, allowing those dispositions and associated genes to propagate.

Strongest feature: The response directly supplies the requested rival explanation and carefully avoids purposive evolutionary language, describing differential survival of guilt-prone individuals whose behavior reduces damaging conduct within their communities. Principal limitation: The proposed selection history and genetic specificity are plausible but unsupported in the excerpt, and an adaptive origin for guilt can coexist with guilt sometimes tracking objective wrongdoing rather than excluding realism. Live burden: Ehrman must present evidence for the mechanism, distinguish biological from cultural transmission, address forgiveness separately, and explain what evidential comparison would favor the non-realist interpretation. Locked score: The strong/competent band recognizes a responsive, coherent, mechanistically specified alternative that weakens explanatory uniqueness, while preserving limitations in empirical warrant and in the inference from an available causal account to broader metaethical consequences.
