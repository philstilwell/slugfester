Overall assessment

Joe Schmid — Evidential divine-hiddenness critique: 88/100

Published strengths

  • Schmid consistently framed hiddenness as defeasible comparative evidence, preserving logical modesty while forcing both sides to explain whether naturalism or perfectly loving theism better predicts the data.
  • His demographic argument tested individualized Christian reasons against geographic clustering, converting flexible person-level explanations into an aggregate prediction and exposing their weak fit with accidents of birth.
  • He repeatedly argued that formation, testimony, and redemption can occur within an acknowledged divine relationship, directly challenging Paterson to establish hiddenness's necessity rather than its bare possibility.
  • His tragic-silence argument connected abstract hiddenness to present relational cost, while his final concession acknowledged the Christian response's coherence without surrendering naturalism's claimed simplicity advantage.

Published weaknesses

  • Schmid's comparative likelihoods remained qualitative: he identified naturalism's simpler expectations but offered no systematic measurement of demographic clustering, sincere nonbelief, religious experiences, or their evidential dependence.
  • His alternative-means replies plausibly showed that many goods can arise within relationship, but did not establish that every valuable motivational or developmental sequence survives unmistakable divine awareness.
  • The parental analogy sharpened the cost of tragic silence, yet it left relevant differences between human caregiving and temporally extended providential governance insufficiently developed.

Dan Paterson — Christian hiddenness defense: 83/100

Published strengths

  • Paterson conceded genuine seekers, real divine hiding, and some evidential pressure, thereby avoiding dismissive psychologizing and engaging the strongest probabilistic version of Schmid's position.
  • He offered several concrete temporary reasons for hiddenness, including formation, avoidance of apostasy, later testimony, humble disclosure, conscience-based access, and eventual redemptive fulfillment.
  • His calibrated-humility reply distinguished broad knowledge of possible divine reasons from exact knowledge of individual cases, avoiding the strongest self-undermining form of skeptical theism.
  • The cross-and-resurrection response supplied Christianity's most integrated countermodel, portraying felt abandonment as compatible with active redemption and the eventual defeat rather than denial of suffering.

Published weaknesses

  • Paterson's developmental, apostasy, testimony, and hardening explanations remained weakly evidenced counterfactual possibilities, rarely showing why clearer relationship could not secure the same goods.
  • Appeals to deathbed or postmortem access addressed ultimate salvation more effectively than present deprivation, leaving Schmid's concern about earthly relationship and flourishing substantially unanswered.
  • His humble-disclosure argument did not adequately distinguish unmistakable awareness from overwhelming spectacle, so it under-supported the inference that nonbelief is needed to preserve relational freedom.
