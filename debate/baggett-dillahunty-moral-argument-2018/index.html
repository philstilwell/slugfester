Overall assessment

David Baggett — Theistic moral explanation: 85/100

Published strengths

  • Landed a disciplined comparative framework spanning moral facts, knowledge, transformation, and rationality.
  • Landed a notably modest conclusion that treats morality as contributory evidence within the total record.
  • Landed the categorical-versus-instrumental distinction and a concrete animal-welfare scope challenge.
  • Landed clear qualifications concerning ordinary secular improvement and the rejection of arbitrary deity concepts.

Published weaknesses

  • The case did not fully establish that complete transformation is an explanation-demanding datum or specify its success conditions.
  • The loving-creator account depended on contested categorical authority and remained comparatively underdeveloped.
  • The replies on divine identification and hiddenness did not resolve reliable access, preventable harm, or less coercive clarification.

Matt Dillahunty — Secular moral inquiry: 86/100

Published strengths

  • Landed foundational challenges to explanandum status, divine candidacy, and the representativeness of the abductive candidate pool.
  • Landed secular mechanisms for ordinary transformation, conscience, convergence, and goal-relative assessment.
  • Landed the distinction between philosophical and methodological naturalism while allowing conditionally demonstrated supernatural causes.
  • Landed strong identification, access, and moral-hiddenness objections against the claimed theistic explanatory advantage.

Published weaknesses

  • The well-being account left its initial normative commitment, internal tradeoffs, and response to categorical obligation incompletely explained.
  • The empathy and convergence accounts explained causal origins and practical coherence more fully than objective truth or categorical authority.
  • The demonstrability language risked imposing a stronger evidential threshold than comparative abduction itself requires.

Objective Moral Facts and Categorical Authority

43:34–87:34

David Baggett — section score 87/100

Objective values concern intrinsic moral goodness, whereas objective duties are binding, prescriptive, authoritative requirements that are at least sometimes categorical.

Strongest feature: The definition carefully distinguishes evaluative goodness from deontic obligation and preserves the important qualification that categorical, exceptionless force belongs to at least some duties rather than all. Principal limitation: Terminological clarity cannot independently establish that such duties exist, possess irreducible authority, or require the personal explanatory ground subsequently proposed by the affirmative. Live burden: Baggett must move from stipulated categories to defensible moral phenomena and then demonstrate why instrumental, contractual, naturalistic, or axiomatic accounts cannot adequately accommodate their apparent authority. Locked score: The very strong band reflects outstanding precision, coherence, calibration, and central relevance to the moral explananda, while retaining an evidential reservation appropriate to a definition rather than an existence argument.

Matt Dillahunty — section score 85/100

Variable guilt cannot establish objective morality, while consequences can be assessed objectively relative to adopted goals such as survival and well-being.

Strongest feature: Dillahunty distinguishes subjective adoption of a goal from objective assessment of consequences, while cultural variability provides a relevant caution against treating guilt as an infallible moral detector. Principal limitation: Well-being remains expressly broad and ill-defined, and goal-relative objectivity does not fully answer Baggett's claim that some obligations bind categorically regardless of an agent's adopted ends. Live burden: The secular account must specify well-being, explain difficult tradeoffs, and preserve the narrower possibility that some guilt experiences defeasibly track wrongdoing despite considerable cultural distortion. Locked score: The strong and competent band reflects substantial coherence, careful boundedness, and direct relevance, while registering incomplete contact with categorical authority and limited development of the proposed evaluative standard.

Moral Knowledge, Guilt, and Conscience

53:04–94:33

David Baggett — section score 83/100

Guilt’s experienced authority can provide defeasible evidence of genuine wrongdoing and need not be explained exhaustively as cultural conditioning.

Strongest feature: Baggett carefully presents guilt as potential evidence rather than an infallible foundation, preserving a modest phenomenological claim while expressly denying that the entire argument depends upon it. Principal limitation: The support remains substantially introspective, and authoritative feeling can arise through cultural formation, psychological mechanisms, or mistaken conscience without reliably tracking objective moral truth. Live burden: The affirmative must identify reliability conditions, distinguish veridical from distorted guilt, and explain what the phenomenology contributes beyond evidence already supplied by independently accepted moral judgments. Locked score: The strong and competent band reflects commendable qualification, relevance, and preservation of the secular alternative, while appropriately limiting credit for evidential warrant and generalization from personal experience.

Matt Dillahunty — section score 87/100

Empathy, universalization, and role reversal can explain conscience and much apparent authority without religious commands or another supernatural foundation.

Strongest feature: The reply offers an intelligible psychological account of conscience through empathy and perspective-taking while avoiding the simplistic claim that moral experience merely repeats parental, communal, or religious instruction. Principal limitation: A causal explanation of why demands feel authoritative does not yet establish their truth, categorical force, or capacity to override self-interest when empathy becomes partial or absent. Live burden: Dillahunty must bridge motivational psychology and normative justification, explaining how role reversal supports reliable principles while accommodating conflicts among persons, interests, animals, and long-term consequences. Locked score: The strong and competent band reflects coherent secular explanatory contact, stable scope, and charitable engagement, while preserving a substantial unresolved bridge from empathic origins to objective authority.

Moral Transformation and Its Horizon

29:56–43:29

David Baggett — section score 78/100

The distinctive issue is whether secular naturalism can sustain realistic hope for complete moral transformation, not whether ordinary improvement plainly occurs.

Strongest feature: The move charitably concedes ordinary secular improvement and isolates a more ambitious transformative horizon, thereby preventing observable behavioral progress from automatically exhausting the affirmative's intended explanandum. Principal limitation: Complete moral transformation receives no sufficiently precise success condition, and the presentation offers limited support that such hope is realistic, widespread, or independently explanation-demanding. Live burden: Baggett must define completeness, establish why aspiration constitutes evidence about reality, and explain why attainable secular formation is insufficient for the motion's transformation component. Locked score: The strong and competent band reflects a clear conceptual distinction, explicit qualification, and constructive comparative relevance, while appropriately registering substantial uncertainty about specification, evidential warrant, and explanatory necessity.

Matt Dillahunty — section score 85/100

Ordinary moral improvement can arise through secular natural processes, while even false beliefs about divine supervision may motivate beneficial behavior.

Strongest feature: The reply supplies a concrete secular alternative for moral change and usefully separates a belief's behavioral effectiveness from its truth, directly undercutting distinctively theistic explanatory necessity. Principal limitation: Its examples principally concern ordinary behavioral improvement, whereas Baggett's later formulation concerns realistic hope for complete transformation, a substantially broader and more demanding prospective horizon. Live burden: The secular account must clarify whether the motion requires explaining observable improvement, aspirational perfection, or both, and identify mechanisms proportionate to whichever transformation concept remains relevant. Locked score: The very strong band reflects direct comparative contact, careful possibility-level calibration, and coherent causal reasoning, while preserving reservations about evidential development and incomplete engagement with total transformation.

Moral Rationality, Flourishing, and Scope

69:39–81:57

David Baggett — section score 89/100

Some obligations bind categorically and therefore cannot be reduced to instrumental reasoning about achieving goals an agent has already adopted.

Strongest feature: Baggett grants the legitimacy of goal-directed practical reasoning and then precisely isolates the remaining issue: whether at least some moral obligations bind independently of an agent's chosen ends. Principal limitation: The distinction identifies what instrumental accounts leave unexplained only if categorical obligations genuinely exist, so the move still depends upon contested phenomenology and foundational moral judgment. Live burden: The affirmative must defend categorical authority without stipulation, while the secular side must explain agents who reject well-being yet appear obligated by duties concerning cruelty, justice, and impartiality. Locked score: The very strong band reflects excellent diagnostic contact, disciplined qualification, conceptual clarity, and central relevance, while retaining an evidential reservation about the existence and scope of categorical requirements.

Matt Dillahunty — section score 82/100

Shared creaturely needs can support convergence on human-benefiting axioms that generate coherent ethical systems under entirely godless physical conditions.

Strongest feature: Dillahunty accepts foundational starting points and offers shared physical needs as a plausible secular explanation for convergence on principles favoring life, health, and reduced suffering. Principal limitation: The random-selection illustration obscures justificatory standards, while coherent system construction and practical convergence do not alone establish objective truth, categorical authority, or dependable handling of conflicts. Live burden: The account must explain why particular axioms deserve endorsement, how revisions occur, and whether common human interests reliably extend toward outsiders, animals, future persons, and costly impartial duties. Locked score: The strong and competent band reflects direct comparative engagement, possibility-level calibration, and useful explanatory economy, while preserving important reservations concerning epistemic warrant, normative authority, and underspecified convergence.

Principal limitation: The particular omni-properties and moral criteria remain underdeveloped, and using moral knowledge to identify morality's divine foundation risks circularity unless independent support distinguishes the candidate. Live burden: The affirmative must explain reliable identification, evidential access, and genuine discriminatory consequences, not merely describe the morally ideal being that would best fit the desired conclusion. Locked score: The strong and competent band reflects substantive candidate restriction, direct engagement, appropriate qualification, and central relevance, while retaining significant reservations about specification, independent warrant, and unresolved access.

Principal limitation: The term demonstrable may impose a stronger admission standard than comparative abduction requires, since explanatory hypotheses can receive provisional support without experimental demonstration or deductive confirmation. Live burden: The objection must articulate an appropriately abductive evidential threshold, while Baggett must show that classical theism contributes testable differentiation, explanatory constraints, or independent access beyond renaming unresolved mystery.
