Overall assessment

Eric Sampson — Non-naturalist moral realism is true: 82/100

Published strengths

  • Sampson defines a deliberately existential realism, then repeatedly preserves that limited scope against semantic pluralism instead of defending unnecessary uniformity across all moral discourse.
  • His rescue example and replies about welfare make stance-independent reasons concretely intelligible, while his distinction between personal justification and persuasion accurately absorbs Bush's strongest concession.
  • He manages burdens exceptionally well against speculative selection effects, granting that future confirmation could hurt realism while refusing to treat an empirical blueprint as present evidence.

Published weaknesses

  • Sampson repeatedly moves too quickly from objective-seeming intuitions to objective facts, without supplying a reliability account capable of defeating projection or incompatible Zorpian intuitions.
  • His answer to Zorpian parity largely relabels attitude-independent alien reasons as moral facts, leaving the actual problem of discriminating contradictory substantive judgments unresolved.
  • His parsimony reply risks assuming realism by counting normative facts among established explananda, rather than independently identifying phenomena that psychological anti-realism cannot explain.

Lance Bush — Moral realism is unintelligible or unjustified: 81/100

Published strengths

  • Bush consistently distinguishes first-order agreement from metaethical confirmation, showing that anti-realists can endorse rescue while denying that the shared verdict uniquely supports realism.
  • His private-evidence objection is carefully calibrated, conceding personal justification while explaining why unshared intuition cannot independently adjudicate a public dispute between rival metaethical theories.
  • The Zorpian case and psychological genealogy jointly create a serious reliability challenge by showing how culturally contingent processes might generate confident realist-seeming judgments with incompatible contents.

Published weaknesses

  • Bush's foundational relationalism leans heavily on personal unintelligibility and a desire-relative example, neither of which establishes the universal claim that all intelligible reasons are conditional.
  • His comparison with empirical disciplines demands independent corroboration without adequately defending why non-natural normative claims must satisfy engineering-style prediction or detection standards.
  • His academic selection and cultural-biological explanations remain largely prospective, lacking data, operational criteria, or a demonstrated link from causal contingency to epistemic unreliability or falsity.

Positions, semantics, and the scope of realism

2:38–9:52

Eric Sampson — section score 86/100

Sampson defines realism as cognitivism, non-nihilism, and the stance-independent truth of at least some moral beliefs, thereby fixing a deliberately limited affirmative target.

Strongest feature: The three-part definition cleanly separates truth-evaluable moral belief, the existence of some moral truths, and independence from individual or cultural reactions, establishing an unusually precise motion-level target. Principal limitation: Definition supplies conceptual organization rather than evidence, and Sampson does not show that his taxonomy exhausts relevant realism or that actual moral discourse instantiates every identified component. Live burden: He must progress from the coherent possibility of stance-independent truth to warranted belief in such truth, while preserving his careful existential scope against Bush's semantic and genealogical challenges. Locked score: The move excels in logical coherence, responsiveness, precision, and calibration, but its evidential contribution remains properly modest because fixing the proposition cannot establish that proposition's truth.

Lance Bush — section score 79/100

Bush rejects any uniform semantics for ordinary moral claims, instead proposing an unfinished mixture of relativist, error-theoretic, and noncognitive interpretations across different speakers.

Strongest feature: Bush carefully resists forcing every moral utterance into one anti-realist model, and his pluralism fairly recognizes that speakers may express propositions, attitudes, or locally relativized commitments differently. Principal limitation: The excerpt contrasts possible semantic theories without presenting representative linguistic evidence, and its promised positive combination remains insufficiently specified to explain which uses receive which analysis. Live burden: Bush must demonstrate actual variation, formulate principled classification criteria, and explain why pluralism threatens Sampson's existential thesis that merely some communities make shared truth-evaluable claims. Locked score: The move is coherent, responsive, and admirably calibrated, yet limited evidential warrant and incomplete positive detail prevent semantic diversity from establishing either global indeterminacy or the motion-level rejection of realism.

Whether stance-independent reasons are intelligible

26:16–31:34

Eric Sampson — section score 81/100

Sampson argues that a drowning child's welfare, threatened suffering, and the minimal cost of rescue are intelligible reasons to act regardless of the rescuer's goals.

Strongest feature: The rescue case answers Bush with concrete situational facts, identifying the endangered child's welfare, familial suffering, comparative benefit, and minimal cost as recognizable considerations favoring immediate intervention. Principal limitation: Saying that the child matters embeds the contested normative premise, so the example illuminates how external reasons appear without independently proving that their authority is stance independent. Live burden: Sampson must distinguish eliciting a powerful shared intuition from explaining its truth conditions and show why relational, constructivist, or psychological accounts cannot accommodate the same verdict. Locked score: The move is exceptionally relevant, clear, and responsive and gives substantial intuitive warrant for intelligibility, although its logical bridge from apparent importance to objective reason remains contestable.

Lance Bush — section score 77/100

Bush contends that reasons become intelligible only relative to desires or standards, using hunger to illustrate why desire-independent reasons purportedly make no sense.

Strongest feature: The food example gives Bush's relational alternative concrete structure, clearly contrasting reasons generated by an agent's wants with Sampson's proposed reasons that apply regardless of those wants. Principal limitation: One intelligible desire-relative reason does not establish that every intelligible reason must be relational, while Bush's personal incomprehension cannot itself demonstrate contradiction, emptiness, or universal unintelligibility. Live burden: He must define the governing standard of intelligibility, exclude primitive favoring relations without presupposing relationalism, and engage the strongest external-reason account rather than merely reporting unfamiliarity. Locked score: The move directly contacts a central affirmative burden and is relevant if proven, but weak universal warrant, incomplete conceptual analysis, and limited charity substantially constrain its force.

Corroboration, intuition, and prima facie justification

32:06–41:18

Eric Sampson — section score 81/100

Sampson replies that non-natural moral facts predictably lack empirical detectors, so failure to supply engineering-style tests provides little evidence against his ontology.

Strongest feature: Sampson correctly notes that evidence must fit the proposed ontology, making absence of a physical moral-property detector expected under non-naturalism rather than a surprising failure that straightforwardly disconfirms it. Principal limitation: Classifying moral facts as non-natural blocks one evidential demand but supplies no alternative access method, and may insulate realism unless independent warrant constrains that classification. Live burden: He must explain how competing normative claims can be assessed, identify reliability conditions for intuition, and show that ontological mismatch does not become immunity from meaningful epistemic scrutiny. Locked score: The response is logically coherent, precise, calibrated, and directly responsive, yet it protects realism from an unsuitable test without positively meeting Bush's broader corroboration challenge.

Lance Bush — section score 85/100

Bush contrasts morality with engineering, biology, and medicine, arguing that realism lacks comparable independent consequences capable of corroborating theories and adjudicating disagreements.

Strongest feature: The cross-disciplinary comparison crystallizes a serious epistemic asymmetry, asking what publicly accessible consequence could discriminate rival moral accounts when first-order intuitions and judgments remain fundamentally contested. Principal limitation: Empirical disciplines may set an inappropriate standard for non-natural facts, and Bush does not establish that every justified domain requires detection, prediction, or practical confirmation of the illustrated kind. Live burden: He must specify a suitably general corroboration requirement, defend its applicability to normativity, and explain whether coherent reflection or explanatory success could satisfy it without becoming circular. Locked score: The move strongly advances the public-adjudication burden through clear and relevant comparisons, but incomplete defense of their comparability prevents absent empirical testing from independently defeating realism.

Deep disagreement and the Zorpian comparison

Eric Sampson — section score 79/100

Sampson accepts that intuitions may fail to persuade others while maintaining that they remain genuine prima facie evidence for the individuals experiencing them.

Strongest feature: Sampson preserves the exact scope of his claim by distinguishing personal justification from interpersonal persuasion, incorporating Bush's strongest concession rather than pretending that private evidence automatically resolves public disagreement. Principal limitation: He asserts intuition's evidential status without developing reliability, independence, or resistance to genealogical defeat, so the distinction protects a modest entitlement rather than realism generally. Live burden: Sampson must show that his seeming is undefeated, explain why its origin plausibly tracks normative truth, and supply resources for choosing between equally sincere incompatible intuitions. Locked score: The reply demonstrates excellent logical coherence, responsiveness, precision, and calibration and successfully defends prima facie personal justification, while openly leaving public corroboration and comparative metaethical confirmation unresolved.

Lance Bush — section score 85/100

Bush grants that realist intuition may personally justify Sampson while denying that such private evidence can substantially resolve a shared metaethical dispute.

Strongest feature: Bush accepts Sampson's carefully limited justificatory claim and sharply distinguishes evidence rationally available to its possessor from evidence capable of adjudicating disagreement among participants lacking the same seeming. Principal limitation: The Bigfoot comparison vividly illustrates privacy but may obscure important differences between isolated perceptual experience and pervasive reflective judgment, while private evidence need not be epistemically negligible. Live burden: Bush must identify why moral intuition is unreliable or comparatively weak, not merely unshared, and explain what public evidence could address metaethical questions without presupposing his preferred standards. Locked score: The reply is highly responsive, precise, charitable, and powerful against dialectical overreach, although it challenges interpersonal force more successfully than Sampson's conceded personal justification.
