Overall assessment

William Lane Craig — Christian theism: 73/100

Published strengths

  • Craig presented a broad cumulative case with distinct cosmological, moral, historical, and experiential routes to the motion.
  • His numbered premises and explicit intermediate conclusions often made the inferential structure unusually easy to reconstruct and test.
  • He repeatedly engaged Dacey’s objections, especially by distinguishing belief from love, material from productive causation, and causal priority from temporal priority.

Published weaknesses

  • Craig repeatedly stated disputed scientific and historical conclusions with near certainty, weakening otherwise serious arguments through avoidable overclaiming about consensus and alternatives.
  • His evolutionary replies conflated a particular outcome’s improbability with the probability of an explained selection process, then inaccurately characterized Dacey’s response as faith.

Austin Dacey — Atheism: 82/100

Published strengths

  • Dacey built a positive probabilistic case by comparing expected worlds under relational theism and atheism across several domains.
  • His strongest replies carefully limited their burden, using underdetermination to block Craig’s inference without pretending to supply complete rival cosmologies.
  • He sharply distinguished mental dependence from reductive identity and heavenly compensation from moral justification, directly answering Craig’s formulations.

Published weaknesses

  • The resurrection critique sometimes replaced one underdeveloped account with speculative burial or removal possibilities whose historical likelihood was not adequately established.
  • His closing catalogue attributed views about damnation and divine intentions more broadly than Craig had established, reducing precision in an otherwise comparative case.

Cosmic existence and origin

6:21–1:33:32

William Lane Craig — section score 78/100

Craig argues that contingent cosmic reality needs an external explanation, then identifies a timeless, nonphysical, personal cause as the most plausible terminus.

Strongest feature: The argument clearly distinguishes necessary existence from contingent existence and connects cosmic contingency to an explanatory demand instead of treating the universe as an unexplained stopping point. Principal limitation: Even if contingent reality needs an explanation, Craig does not demonstrate that its terminus must be personal; describing a nonphysical cause leaves the crucial transition from necessary ground to purposive agent unsupported. Live burden: Craig must justify both the principle that every contingent totality has an explanation and the further inference that the explanation possesses mentality, rather than merely naming an unknown metaphysical foundation. Locked score: 79 The move directly advances the explanatory-foundation burden with a coherent argument, but its personal-cause conclusion remains less established than its initial demand for an explanation.

Austin Dacey — section score 82/100

Dacey replies that Big Bang theory describes early cosmic development without establishing an absolute beginning from nothing, because current physics fails near the earliest boundary.

Strongest feature: The response attacks the empirical premise at exactly the point Craig needs, distinguishing a hot early state and finite model from metaphysical creation ex nihilo with appropriate scientific caution. Principal limitation: Unresolved quantum gravity shows underdetermination, not that the universe lacked a beginning; Dacey therefore blocks Craig’s certainty without providing positive evidence for an eternal or uncaused cosmos. Live burden: Dacey must show that present cosmology cannot bear Craig’s theological conclusion, while Craig retains the burden of establishing an absolute origin rather than appealing to scientific authority beyond its scope. Locked score: 87 The move strongly undermines the explanatory-foundation argument’s empirical bridge through precise methodological restraint, even though it leaves the ultimate origin entirely open at the decisive cosmological boundary.

Fine-tuning and evolutionary design

12:29–1:36:32

William Lane Craig — section score 66/100

Craig treats the narrow life-permitting range of physical constants and initial conditions as evidence that intelligence designed the universe for embodied observers.

Strongest feature: The argument identifies a specific datum—the apparent sensitivity of life to physical parameters—and explicitly compares necessity, chance, and design instead of relying on a general impression of cosmic order. Principal limitation: Craig lacks a justified probability distribution over possible constants, does not eliminate deeper physical constraints, and offers limited reason to expect a divine designer to select precisely this life-permitting arrangement. Live burden: The pro side must show that the fine-tuning data are substantially likelier under theism than under naturalistic possibilities, including unknown laws or selection effects, without assuming the designer’s intentions. Locked score: 74 The move supplies meaningful cosmic-design evidence, but unresolved probability measures and uncertain divine predictions keep its comparative inference suggestive rather than conclusive.

Austin Dacey — section score 83/100

Dacey argues that evolutionary waste, extinction, and dysfunctional structures fit blind natural selection better than creation by an unlimited, rational, purposive designer.

Strongest feature: The move cites recognizable biological patterns and compares them with ordinary expectations about competent design, converting the general problem of imperfection into a specific worldview likelihood argument. Principal limitation: The human-engineering analogy does not establish what methods or goals an unlimited creator would choose, and apparent inefficiency could be compatible with unknown ecological, developmental, or providential purposes. Live burden: Dacey must defend the relevance of functional standards to divine design, while Craig must explain why the observed waste and defects are at least as expected under purposeful creation as under selection. Locked score: 82 The argument materially advances naturalistic fit through concrete evidence, although uncertainty about divine aims prevents imperfection from becoming a decisive disproof within this explicitly comparative assessment.

Moral reality and suffering

15:46–1:40:07

William Lane Craig — section score 76/100

Craig argues that objective moral values and duties require God, distinguishing how people know moral truths from what could make those truths objectively binding.

Strongest feature: The move correctly separates moral epistemology from moral ontology and presses a serious explanatory question: why adaptive human attitudes would correspond to stance-independent obligations if atheistic naturalism were true. Principal limitation: The argument assumes rather than establishes that only a divine nature can ground value, while secular accounts based on welfare, reasons, relationships, or valuable features remain available alternatives. Live burden: Craig must demonstrate that objective normativity cannot arise from nondivine facts and explain how God’s nature generates obligations without reducing goodness to arbitrary preference or circularly defining God as good. Locked score: 74 The argument directly serves the moral-ground burden and avoids a crude appeal to moral ignorance, but its exclusivity premise remains substantially underargued.

Austin Dacey — section score 84/100

Dacey argues that severe apparently pointless suffering, especially preventable childhood agony, counts against a perfectly good and powerful God who offers neither justification nor reassurance.

Strongest feature: The move targets the attributes at issue directly and combines moral severity with experienced abandonment, showing why suffering challenges both benevolent governance and the promised relationship with persons. Principal limitation: Human ignorance prevents confident claims that no sufficient reason exists, and Dacey cannot exclude remote goods or constraints unavailable to finite observers, even when no justification is evident. Live burden: Dacey must establish that apparently gratuitous suffering is substantially less probable under perfect goodness and power, while Craig must supply more than the bare possibility of unknown reasons. Locked score: 86 The move powerfully serves the suffering burden because the examples create serious comparative pressure, yet its inference remains evidential rather than logically demonstrative in this morally consequential comparison.

Divine hiddenness and relationship

30:40–1:35:28

William Lane Craig — section score 76/100

Craig replies that adequate evidence may already exist and that unmistakable disclosure might not promote freely chosen relationship, while omniscience would select the best revelation.

Strongest feature: The response engages Dacey’s relational standard rather than denying that God seeks persons, and it distinguishes evidence sufficient for belief from evidence that would actually cultivate morally significant love. Principal limitation: Craig’s cumulative arguments and appeal to inward witness do not show that sincere nonbelievers possess adequate access, while hypothetical drawbacks of clearer disclosure remain asserted rather than demonstrated. Live burden: Craig must explain persistent nonresistant nonbelief under a universally loving God and show why additional clarity would impede or fail to improve relational opportunity for those presently unconvinced. Locked score: 76 The reply meaningfully protects the pro case by identifying a gap between disclosure and love, but it does not fully answer the antecedent problem of unavailable relationship for sincerely receptive nonbelievers.

Austin Dacey — section score 83/100

Dacey argues that widespread sincere nonbelief is improbable if a loving God wants relationship with every capable person and could disclose himself more clearly.

Strongest feature: The argument derives a testable expectation from relational love and uses sincere seekers and religious diversity as evidence, while modestly claiming comparative improbability rather than a logical contradiction. Principal limitation: Dacey cannot directly determine every nonbeliever’s openness or God’s complete reasons, and he gives no precise estimate of how much clearer revelation would improve freely chosen relationship. Live burden: The con side must show that nonresistant nonbelief is sufficiently common and unexpected under relational theism, while answering the possibility that available evidence is adequate for God’s purposes. Locked score: 84 The move strongly advances the divine-relationship and comparative-evidence burdens because disclosure appears prerequisite to relationship, though uncertainty about individual circumstances limits its reach.

Scientific explanation and divine action

33:36–1:31:18

William Lane Craig — section score 73/100

Craig replies that rational divine creation predicts lawful nature and permits exceptional miracles, so science’s natural explanations are compatible with theism rather than evidence against it.

Strongest feature: The response separates ordinary law-governed inquiry from exceptional historical intervention and supplies a plausible theistic reason for stable, intelligible regularities that make scientific investigation possible. Principal limitation: Compatibility is weaker than comparative prediction; Craig does not show that theism expects science’s complete practical independence from divine action, and resurrection remains a disputed example rather than independent support. Live burden: Craig must explain why an active relational creator would leave no generally recognized causal signature in mature science, while Dacey must justify expecting such a signature under theism. Locked score: 65 The reply establishes coexistence between science and belief more successfully than equal likelihood, so it provides only limited defense against Dacey’s naturalistic-fit argument.

Austin Dacey — section score 81/100

Dacey argues that science’s success through natural causes favors atheism because an active relational creator might be expected to leave detectable interventions in explanatory history.

Strongest feature: The argument uses an extensive public practice—successful natural explanation—as comparative evidence and focuses on the absence of needed divine action rather than claiming that science formally disproves God. Principal limitation: Scientific methods ordinarily restrict themselves to natural causes, and theism can predict stable laws; Dacey therefore needs additional support for expecting divine interventions to appear within scientific explanations. Live burden: Dacey must show that the relevant form of theism predicts detectable causal activity more strongly than naturalism predicts science’s methodological pattern, rather than inferring ontology directly from disciplinary practice. Locked score: 76 The move contributes to naturalistic fit with a clear empirical observation, but the disputed prediction about divine action keeps its worldview comparison moderate.

Minds, brains, and personhood

35:14–1:31:52

William Lane Craig — section score 69/100

Craig replies that brain correlations permit an immaterial, causally effective self and that identity, intentionality, and agency resist reduction to physical properties.

Strongest feature: The response identifies a live metaphysical alternative—interactionist dualism—and correctly notes that Christianity need not uniformly require it, preventing Dacey from treating correlation as straightforward disproof. Principal limitation: Craig offers little explanation of how immaterial causation works or why localized damage predictably alters particular capacities, and introspective confidence does not resolve those empirical dependencies. Live burden: Craig must show that interactionist dualism explains detailed brain dependence at least comparably to physicalist accounts, while Dacey need only support dependence rather than complete reduction. Locked score: 71 The move usefully narrows Dacey’s inference and preserves compatibility, but it does not neutralize the empirical pattern that made mentality appear strongly brain-dependent under the specifically comparative evidential standard.

Austin Dacey — section score 85/100

Dacey argues that the close dependence of mental life on brains fits atheism better than substance dualism, citing injury, localization, genetics, and obscure interaction.

Strongest feature: The argument draws on convergent correlations between physical changes and mental capacities and requires only dependence, avoiding the stronger and unnecessary claim that mental properties are reducible to physical properties. Principal limitation: Dependence is compatible with some theistic views and does not by itself exclude an immaterial self that operates through a brain, so the evidence underdetermines the metaphysics of mind. Live burden: Dacey must explain why detailed brain dependence is appreciably less expected if God exists, while Craig must show that interactionist dualism predicts the observed vulnerability and localization rather than merely accommodating them. Locked score: 81 The move strongly supports naturalistic fit against independent-soul expectations, but Christianity’s compatibility with materialist or dependent-mind accounts narrows its force against theism generally.

Resurrection and religious experience

19:39–1:38:16

William Lane Craig — section score 72/100

Craig presents Jesus’s resurrection as the best explanation for the empty tomb, reported appearances, and the disciples’ belief, while rejecting familiar naturalistic alternatives.

Strongest feature: The move offers a concrete abductive argument from three historical claims and evaluates competing explanations, thereby connecting generic theism to the specifically Christian conception of God defended in the debate. Principal limitation: The independence and reliability of the sources, the extent of scholarly agreement, and the adequacy of burial or visionary alternatives remain contested, so the evidential basis is less secure than Craig suggests. Live burden: Craig must establish the historical facts with appropriately independent evidence and show that resurrection predicts them better than ordinary alternatives despite the intrinsically extraordinary character of miraculous causation. Locked score: 76 The move materially advances Christian confirmation, but disputed sources and unresolved comparative explanations prevent the historical case from carrying the full weight assigned to it.

Austin Dacey — section score 78/100

Dacey argues that uncertain burial history and dependent resurrection sources leave mundane relocation and nonveridical appearance experiences more probable than divine bodily resurrection.

Strongest feature: The response engages the empty tomb, appearances, and disciples’ belief separately, questioning both factual support and explanatory comparison instead of relying solely on the antecedent improbability of miracles. Principal limitation: The common-grave scenario remains a possibility rather than a well-supported reconstruction, and Dacey does not fully explain the origin, variety, or persistence of reported appearances and collective belief. Live burden: Dacey must keep plausible natural alternatives open and expose evidential dependence, while Craig must establish source reliability and show resurrection outperforms those alternatives despite its extraordinary supernatural commitment. Locked score: 78 The move substantially weakens Christian confirmation by challenging its historical foundation, but the proposed natural account remains incomplete and partly speculative.
