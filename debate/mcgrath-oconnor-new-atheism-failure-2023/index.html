Overall assessment

Alister McGrath — New Atheism has failed: 73/100

Published strengths

  • McGrath established a clear motion-level metric by contrasting New Atheism’s early promise of a new age with its later fading from ordinary public awareness.
  • His scientific-underdetermination examples gave concrete substance to the narrower corrective that popular appeals to science can conceal genuine second-level judgments among empirically equivalent theories.
  • The twelve conversion cases were carefully calibrated as demonstrations of possible counterproductive influence, not inflated into unsupported claims about prevalence or net religious movement.
  • He repeatedly favored intellectual humility at difficult moments, most notably accepting the need to check the Leviticus citation rather than manufacturing an immediate textual defense.

Published weaknesses

  • The covenant answer addressed whether ancient law binds Christians now but missed O’Connor’s central burden: explaining why the same morally perfect God could formerly permit slavery.
  • McGrath’s tentative suggestion that slavery may once have been morally permissible supplied no historical facts or moral principle, deepening the internal problem instead of resolving it.
  • His published characterization of Leviticus 25 as prohibiting slavery was confronted by contrary chapter language, and the debate ended with only a promise to investigate.

Alex O'Connor — Critical reassessment of New Atheism: 83/100

Published strengths

  • O’Connor’s distinction between popular success and philosophical fruitfulness gave the motion a disciplined framework and prevented fading cultural visibility from proving comprehensive failure.
  • He consistently separated criticism of Dawkins and New Atheism from criticism of atheism, preserving positive arguments from evil and hiddenness without defending every movement excess.
  • The internal moral critique was exceptionally precise: an atheist need not first ground objective morality to test whether Christianity’s own God-grounded ethic can accommodate slavery.
  • His exact Leviticus comparison produced the debate’s strongest evidential moment, directly matching McGrath’s published words against the chapter’s authorization of inheritable lifelong slaves.

Published weaknesses

  • The reverse-causation account of secularization driving New Atheist popularity remained a plausible suspicion rather than an evidenced causal finding, weakening his attribution of popular success.
  • His explanation of abrasive rhetoric through evangelical media culture made the style understandable but did not answer McGrath’s claim that shame was ineffective and socially harmful.
  • The hiddenness argument shifted between divine existence and goodness and relied heavily on personal nonexperience without fully specifying expected revelation under competing hypotheses.

Morality, scripture, and internal critique

Alister McGrath — section score 66/100

The new covenant leaves most Old Testament law nonbinding, so ancient slavery codes do not prescribe present Christian conduct under Christianity’s different hermeneutical scheme.

Strongest feature: McGrath clearly states the Christian hermeneutical distinction between covenants and explains why Old Testament slavery legislation does not function as presently binding instruction for Christians. Live burden: McGrath needed to defend the former moral permissibility, explain principled continuity and discontinuity, and show how a benevolent divine character remains coherent across covenants rather than only correcting Dawkins’s claims about contemporary practice. Social legacy, polemics, and religious harms

New Atheism’s arrogance, overstatement, and stridency created cultural pressure that pushed believers toward society’s edges and discouraged candid discussion of their faith.

Strongest feature: McGrath identifies a plausible social mechanism linking polemical rhetoric to believers’ reluctance to disclose faith, and connects contributors’ reported reactions to his desired restoration of respectful public discussion. Live burden: McGrath needed comparative testimony or social evidence showing that New Atheist rhetoric materially produced marginalization, then explain why this cultural cost outweighs benefits from making religious authority more publicly contestable.
