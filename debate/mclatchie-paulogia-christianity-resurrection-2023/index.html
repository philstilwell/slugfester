Overall assessment

Jonathan McLatchie — Physical resurrection is the best explanation: 83/100

Published strengths

  • McLatchie corrected the central burden from deductive necessity to comparative probability and kept his later case focused on the best-explanation question.
  • He integrated source interconnections, detailed encounters, and costly proclamation into a cumulative trilemma rather than treating any single consideration as decisive.
  • He made unusually careful concessions about discrepancies, minimal-facts underdetermination, martyrdom evidence, Nero’s motives, and the limited role of persecution.

Published weaknesses

  • His case depended heavily on the contested transfer from mundane textual reliability to the authenticity of detailed resurrection narratives without sufficiently calibrating that transfer.
  • Several claims offered to raise the miracle prior—prophecy, contemporary miracles, Israel, and Jesus’ identity claims—were introduced without enough debate-specific defense.

Paulogia — Natural explanations remain preferable: 80/100

Published strengths

  • Paulogia repeatedly separated sincerity from truth and showed why persecution cannot resolve the live possibility of honest mistake.
  • He exposed the dependence of the maximal-data case on eyewitness proximity and rejected any automatic move from accurate historical kernels to supernatural accuracy.
  • His Acts-aside request for named witnesses, precise claims, and documented risks tested the narrowed persecution case without confusing nonfatal hardship with martyrdom.

Published weaknesses

  • His opening often treated the survival of any natural possibility as sufficient, even though the motion required a comparative case for the best explanation.
  • His favored single-sincere-origin hypothesis remained underdescribed, weakening its support for naturalistic preference even though withholding assent did not require a complete causal reconstruction.

Burdens and competing explanations

Jonathan McLatchie — section score 86/100

McLatchie reframes the motion around comparative probability, correcting Paulogia’s repeated suggestion that resurrection must be logically required or uniquely possible.

Strongest feature: McLatchie reframed his obligation as comparative explanation: resurrection need only outperform live alternatives, not become deductively unavoidable, and this corrected Paulogia’s repeated use of required. Principal limitation: The clarification came after the opening burden had already drifted, however, and McLatchie still needed to specify a common measure by which textual reliability, sincere error, fabrication, and resurrection could be compared. Live burden: His remaining task was therefore to show that the maximal Gospel data deserved enough confidence and discriminative force to make the natural competitors less probable, while avoiding any inference from mere survival or unexplained detail. Locked score: 92/100 — Because he identified the proper motion-level standard clearly and used it consistently thereafter, yet left probability assignments implicit, this decisive correction earns 92 rather than a perfect score.

Paulogia — section score 74/100

Paulogia argues that accurate and mistaken resurrection beliefs can motivate identical commitment, limiting what later Christian endurance establishes about the initiating event.

Strongest feature: Paulogia observed that Christian commitment cannot distinguish an accurate resurrection belief from a sincere but mistaken one, since either belief can motivate sacrifice, evangelism, and persistence across centuries. Principal limitation: That point sharply limits appeals to Christianity’s subsequent cultural success, but it says much less about the earliest named proclaimers whose access, experiences, and risks were the contested evidence. Live burden: He still had to connect motivational equivalence to a grounded origin scenario and demonstrate that an initial sincere error could generate the encounter traditions without relying only on abstract possibility. Locked score: 84/100 — The argument efficiently blocked an overinference from endurance to truth, while its reach stopped short of the earliest evidential links, so the locked score of 84 reflects strong relevance with incomplete explanatory development.

Sources and eyewitness proximity

Jonathan McLatchie — section score 84/100

McLatchie links John’s foot-washing episode with Luke’s dispute over greatness, presenting their incidental fit as evidence of connected historical circumstances.

Strongest feature: McLatchie’s foot-washing example produced an interlocking detail: Luke supplies the dispute over greatness that explains Jesus washing the disciples’ feet in John, although neither writer overtly constructs the connection. Principal limitation: This is evidence of remembered circumstances or shared historical material, but the fit can arise through tradition, source knowledge, or narrative development and does not by itself identify eyewitness authorship. Live burden: He had to establish independence and casualness across examples, then explain why reliability in ministry episodes should increase confidence in resurrection appearances rather than only in a historical kernel. Encounter detail and honest mistake

11:26–30:10

Jonathan McLatchie — section score 83/100

McLatchie argues that repeated multisensory group encounters resist honest mistake, provided the Gospel descriptions substantially preserve what earliest witnesses reported.

Strongest feature: McLatchie argued that public group appearances, conversations, touch, eating, and repeated events across forty days would be difficult for the witnesses to misperceive, giving the honest-mistake horn concrete evidential pressure. Principal limitation: The force of this argument is conditional on the later Gospel descriptions preserving early testimony; without that premise, narrative detail measures the final texts rather than the originating experience that Paulogia hypothesized. Live burden: He therefore still had to establish source proximity and independence strongly enough to block hallucination, misidentification, visionary interpretation, and gradual embellishment at the historical stage. Locked score: 80/100 — The score of 80 reflects substantial explanatory power once the reports are admitted and a clear answer to vague mistake proposals, discounted because the crucial authenticity condition remained disputed throughout the debate.

Paulogia — section score 69/100

Paulogia’s opening model demands that McLatchie make every alternative accuracy-and-sincerity combination impossible, using one sincere mistake as his favored challenge rather than an established history.

Strongest feature: Paulogia’s accuracy-and-sincerity graph made one sincerely mistaken originator a concrete counterexample to any claim that resurrection was required, and it asked what evidence could eliminate that possibility. Principal limitation: He demanded that every other point on the graph be impossible, a burden mismatch because McLatchie defended resurrection as best or most probable rather than logically necessary; Peter appeared only later as a hypothetical candidate. Live burden: For nonuniqueness, Paulogia needed only keep sincere mistake possible; for his chosen naturalistic preference, he needed evidence that such an origin and subsequent transmission were comparatively more credible than McLatchie’s account. Locked score: 69/100 — The hypothesis exposed underdetermination, but the impossibility demand and sparse historical development sharply limited its motion-level force.

Persecution and sincerity

11:51–1:16:10

Jonathan McLatchie — section score 81/100

McLatchie argues that voluntary hardship supports early proclaimers’ sincerity, although his initial formulation lacks secure witness-by-witness links among experience, testimony, and risk.

Strongest feature: McLatchie used voluntary hardships, imprisonments, dangers, and conduct to argue that early proclaimers sincerely believed their resurrection testimony, appropriately directing the evidence against deliberate deception rather than treating suffering as proof of truth. Principal limitation: The initial formulation spoke broadly of apostles and leaned on Paley, leaving uncertain which witnesses made which claims, what costs they knowingly accepted, and how those links are documented. Live burden: He still needed named, source-specific chains connecting an alleged eyewitness to a resurrection assertion and voluntary persistence under avoidable pressure, while separating later Christian persecution from first-generation evidence. Locked score: 76/100 — The score of 76 recognizes the valid sincerity principle and cumulative relevance, but reflects that broad categories of costly proclamation did not yet meet Paulogia’s demand for discriminating particulars.

Paulogia — section score 91/100

After McLatchie narrows martyrdom, Paulogia asks hypothetically without Acts which named eyewitnesses demonstrably preached resurrection claims and placed themselves at risk.

Strongest feature: After McLatchie had already narrowed martyrdom, Paulogia’s Acts-aside hypothetical demanded names, precise resurrection claims, reliable preaching evidence, and documented risk, exposing gaps that broad references to apostles can conceal. Principal limitation: Temporarily setting Acts aside clarified source dependence but could not establish that its evidence was worthless, and a sincerity case can draw on nonfatal hardship without requiring an execution choice. Live burden: Paulogia needed only show that generic persecution claims lacked secure witness-specific links; favoring his natural account further required explaining how the remaining named evidence fits sincere mistake. Locked score: 90/100 — The questioning exercised excellent source control and burden discipline, with a small deduction for pressing the hypothetical beyond what it alone could establish.

Miracle priors and testimony

Jonathan McLatchie — section score 83/100

McLatchie argues that divine signs must deviate from nature and invokes wider Christian evidence to raise resurrection’s prior probability above automatic exclusion.

Strongest feature: McLatchie argued that miracles must deviate from nature to function as divine signs and that Christian evidence—Paul’s conversion, prophecy, Jesus’ identity claims, contemporary miracles, and Israel—could raise the prior probability of resurrection. Principal limitation: This supplied a coherent Bayesian response to automatic dismissal, but most supporting categories were mentioned rather than defended within the debate and risked expanding the case far beyond the shared evidential record. Live burden: He still had to show that these considerations genuinely favor Christian divine action without presupposing disputed theology, and then combine that prior with the reliability of the resurrection testimony. Locked score: 75/100 — The score of 75 recognizes a relevant framework and religious-context explanation, while heavily discounting unsupported auxiliary claims that could not bear their assigned prior-shifting role here.

Paulogia contrasts common error and deception with rare miracles, setting a demanding testimonial threshold that his later answer explicitly leaves uncertain.

Strongest feature: Paulogia highlighted the frequency of error and deception against the rarity of miracles, explaining why testimonial failure initially appears more probable than resurrection and why Gospel testimony faces a demanding threshold. Principal limitation: This is a legitimate prior-probability concern, but his initial formulation approached an absolute ceiling and compared raw daily frequencies without accounting for evidence quality, independence, specificity, or divine-context hypotheses; his later uncertainty qualified that stance. Live burden: He needed a calibrated account of how the prior should update under different conditions and an assessment of the sources without converting methodological naturalism into an absolute prohibition.
