Overall assessment

William Lane Craig — Theistic moral grounding: 76/100

Published strengths

  • Craig presents a recognizable positive foundation in a necessarily holy and loving divine nature rather than relying only on objections to secular realism.
  • His strongest exchange distinguishes value-bearing capacities from an explanation of their objective value, exposing a genuine unresolved step in Kagan's agency account.
  • He clearly separates moral ontology from fallible moral access, preserving objective truth despite disagreement and selfishness.
  • His substitutionary-atonement reply directly supplies a mechanism intended to reconcile salvation with justice rather than evading the doctrinal tension.

Published weaknesses

  • The extinction argument moves from absent permanent cosmic effects to absent objective significance without answering the immediate importance of finite suffering and love.
  • The prudence argument assumes that conflict with self-interest makes morality rationally subordinate, leaving Kagan's comparative-weight objection unanswered.
  • The divine-lawgiver and atrocity arguments insufficiently engage ideal contractarianism and categorical reasons as objective rather than merely conventional alternatives.

Shelly Kagan — Secular moral realism: 85/100

Published strengths

  • Kagan defines secular realism through universal categorical reasons and supplies substantive harm-and-help content rather than reducing morality to preference.
  • His non-contradiction analogy directly challenges the necessity of a personal lawgiver while leaving room for further debate about moral and logical normativity.
  • His reflective-agency account explains responsibility clearly, and his finite-suffering and finite-love replies decisively separate objective importance from permanence.
  • His response on prudential conflict precisely identifies that competing reasons do not establish self-interest's rational supremacy.

Published weaknesses

  • The categorical-reasons account characterizes objective authority more clearly than it explains the metaphysical source or binding force of that authority.
  • The ideal-contract proposal leaves perfect rationality, bargaining constraints, determinacy, and the authority of hypothetical agreement materially compressed.
  • The moral-progress account needs independent truth-tracking criteria to distinguish discovery from later consensus, altered preference, or shifts in power.

Objective Value and Intrinsic Human Worth

4:38–31:48

William Lane Craig — section score 77/100

Craig grounds objective goodness in God's holy and loving nature, then argues that contingent evolutionary adaptation cannot adequately explain intrinsic human moral worth.

Strongest feature: The move offers an intelligible positive standard in divine character and directly asks what explains intrinsic human worth rather than merely cataloguing valued capacities or inherited attitudes. Principal limitation: It shifts between grounding goodness generally and grounding persons specifically, while compressing secular realism into evolutionary preference without closely confronting irreducible value, welfare, or categorical-reason alternatives. Live burden: The theistic case must explain how divine properties establish goodness without circularity and why comparable secular stopping points are inadequate, while distinguishing evolutionary explanations of belief from defeaters for moral truth. Locked score: The strong performance band reflects central relevance, a recognizable grounding proposal, and genuine contact with secular claims, while substantial deductions remain for comparative incompleteness, overstated exclusivity, and limited representational charity.

Shelly Kagan — section score 83/100

Kagan proposes that wrong action harms people or unjustifiably withholds help, while right action avoids harm and provides assistance under relevant circumstances.

Strongest feature: The harm-and-help account supplies concrete, intelligible moral content and directly advances the secular objective-reasons burden without pretending to deliver an exhaustive ethical theory. Principal limitation: Its references to relevant circumstances and appropriate assistance leave justification thresholds, competing harms, demandingness, and distributive priorities substantially underdefined. Live burden: The account must explain why these welfare-centered considerations possess stance-independent authority and how conflicts among victims, beneficiaries, permissions, and obligations should be resolved without merely importing disputed intuitions. Locked score: The strong performance band reflects excellent relevance, coherent practical orientation, and appropriately modest presentation, while preserving meaningful deductions for compressed foundations, incomplete boundary conditions, and limited argument for the objective authority of the proposed standard.

Moral Duty, Categorical Reasons, and Lawgiving

10:54–35:46

William Lane Craig — section score 76/100

Craig grounds objective duties in commands necessarily expressing God's moral nature and contrasts those obligations with social conditioning, convention, and contractual agreement.

Strongest feature: The account clearly joins divine commands to a necessarily moral nature, avoiding a purely arbitrary decree model while directly addressing the source of categorical duty. Principal limitation: The inference from objective obligation to personal command remains substantially unsupported, and ideal rational agreement is treated too readily as ordinary social convention rather than a proposed objective procedure. Live burden: The theistic case must explain why impersonal reasons cannot obligate, how divine authority produces duties beyond identifying values, and why necessary divine character avoids merely relocating the normative foundation. Locked score: The strong performance band reflects clear construction, indispensable burden contact, and an internally intelligible command framework, while deductions remain for weak comparative engagement, compressed warrant, and incomplete answers to logical-normativity and hypothetical-contract objections.

Shelly Kagan — section score 83/100

Kagan offers impartial agreement among perfectly rational agents behind a veil of ignorance as a secular foundation for objective rules governing mutual conduct.

Strongest feature: The ideal-contract proposal offers an impartial procedure linking moral requirements to what rational agents would prescribe without knowing their identities, advantages, or vulnerabilities. Principal limitation: Perfect rationality, admissible bargaining information, agreement conditions, and the transition from hypothetical choice to categorical obligation remain insufficiently specified. Live burden: The account must show that its procedural constraints are independently justified, yield determinate principles rather than multiple reasonable settlements, and bind actual agents who never participated in the imagined agreement. Locked score: The strong performance band reflects direct engagement with the secular-duty burden, coherent procedural architecture, and appropriately illustrative calibration, while preserving deductions for compressed derivation, underdeveloped authority, and uncertainty about which substantive rules ideal bargainers would necessarily select.

Rational Agency and Naturalistic Grounding

43:28–50:04

William Lane Craig — section score 89/100

Craig grants distinctive human capacities but argues that identifying reflection, love, creativity, and communication does not explain why persons possessing them are objectively valuable.

Strongest feature: The reply precisely separates locating value-bearing characteristics from explaining their objective value, exposing an important omitted inference in the naturalistic capacity account. Principal limitation: The challenge does not itself demonstrate that an irreducible secular value fact is illegitimate or that divine nature provides a more explanatory and less primitive stopping point. Live burden: The secular side must defend its foundational stopping rule, while the theistic side must articulate comparative explanatory criteria showing why grounding value in divine nature succeeds where grounding value in persons or reasons fails. Locked score: The very strong performance band reflects diagnostic precision, excellent responsiveness, strong charity, and direct contact with a central bridge, with modest deductions because identifying an explanatory gap is not yet a complete positive demonstration of theism.

Shelly Kagan — section score 84/100

Kagan locates moral responsibility in rational agents' capacity to reflect upon reasons and regulate conduct, distinguishing them from lions and very young children.

Strongest feature: The reflective-agency distinction is concrete, stable, and highly responsive, explaining why responsibility attaches differently to reason-responsive adults, nonreflective animals, and immature children. Principal limitation: The account explains eligibility for responsibility more fully than it explains why reflective capacity, its possessors, or the reasons recognized have objective value and authority. Live burden: The secular case must connect deliberative control with valid moral standards and degrees of accountability, including impaired agency, while avoiding a slide from capacity to recognize reasons into a foundation for those reasons. Locked score: The very strong performance band reflects excellent directness, coherent differentiation, representative support, and measured scope, with remaining deductions because the answer resolves the agency question more decisively than the underlying value-grounding challenge.

Finite Life, Extinction, and Moral Significance

54:53–89:27

William Lane Craig — section score 64/100

Craig argues that universal extinction and absent ultimate accountability deprive moral choices of enduring significance because nothing finally leaves the universe better or worse.

Strongest feature: The extinction scenario vividly raises a legitimate question about enduring consequence, cosmic permanence, and whether ultimate accountability contributes a distinctive dimension of significance. Principal limitation: The inference equivocates among eternal effect, final-state difference, accountability, and objective importance, without showing why temporally located harms, relationships, or achievements cannot genuinely matter. Live burden: The theistic case must defend permanence as a necessary condition for moral significance rather than one valuable kind of significance, and must answer concrete counterexamples involving finite suffering and love. Locked score: The weak performance band reflects direct central-burden relevance and a clearly recoverable concern, but substantial deductions follow from the unsupported permanence requirement, limited contact with secular reasons, unstable terminology, and inadequate representation of finite-value realism.

Shelly Kagan — section score 92/100

Kagan argues that torture objectively matters to victims, families, and communities even when their suffering never alters the universe's eventual cosmic endpoint.

Strongest feature: The victim-centered counterexample directly defeats the inference from absent eternal consequences to absent present importance by identifying unmistakable subjects and relationships affected by torture. Principal limitation: The example establishes that finite suffering matters more readily than it supplies a complete secular metaphysics explaining the stance-independent status of that importance. Live burden: The secular position must connect the compelling case to a general account of objective value, while the theistic position must narrow its claim to a defensible kind of ultimate significance or explain why victim-centered importance is insufficient. Locked score: The very strong performance band reflects exceptional responsiveness, precise scope, coherent counterexample reasoning, strong warrant, and charitable engagement, with only limited deductions for leaving the deeper realist foundation comparatively compressed.

Accountability, Salvation, and Prudential Conflict

59:30–74:14

William Lane Craig — section score 74/100

Craig argues that divine accountability ultimately harmonizes prudence and morality, whereas naturalism permits self-interest to conflict with morally required sacrifice.

Strongest feature: The proposal identifies a genuine practical tension between moral sacrifice and personal advantage and explains how ultimate accountability could align their eventual outcomes. Principal limitation: It treats prudence, self-interest, and rational authority too interchangeably, then assumes that conflict makes prudence dominant rather than establishing the comparative weight of competing reasons. Live burden: The theistic case must show why secular moral reasons cannot rationally outweigh self-interest and why externally aligned consequences enhance moral authority rather than merely incentivize compliance. Locked score: The weak performance band reflects subsidiary relevance and a recognizable harmonizing mechanism, but substantial deductions remain because the decisive ranking premise is unsupported, central secular claims go unanswered, and the presentation overstates what the mere possibility of prudential conflict establishes.

Shelly Kagan — section score 89/100

Kagan argues that conflict between morality and prudence does not establish self-interest's supremacy because moral reasons may possess greater rational weight.

Strongest feature: The reply isolates the missing premise with exceptional economy: conflict between moral and prudential reasons does not determine which category rationally outweighs the other. Principal limitation: It defeats the claimed priority of self-interest more clearly than it supplies a developed account of how categorical moral reasons acquire or exercise superior rational weight. Live burden: The secular position must explain comparative weighting and demanding sacrifice, while the theistic position must argue rather than assume that unresolved divergence between morality and welfare makes moral compliance irrational. Locked score: The very strong performance band reflects precise diagnostic contact, strong coherence, accurate representation, and proportionate argumentative force, with modest deductions because the positive theory of rational priority remains less developed than the successful rebuttal of prudential supremacy.

Moral Knowledge, Progress, and Practical Application

66:28–82:43

William Lane Craig — section score 75/100

Craig distinguishes believing in God from grounding morality in God, arguing that objective criticism of religious atrocities requires a transcendent standard beyond social belief.

Strongest feature: The move carefully distinguishes moral knowledge and personal belief from ontological grounding, preventing religious wrongdoing from directly refuting the proposed divine foundation. Principal limitation: Recognizing atrocities as objectively wrong supports realism but does not establish that the needed standard must be divine, especially when secular categorical reasons and ideal agreement are already offered. Live burden: The theistic case must show why transcending actual opinion entails dependence on God and must directly test whether secular criteria can condemn religious and political atrocities without contradiction. Locked score: The mixed performance band reflects a useful clarification, relevant transcultural application, and stable formulation, while substantial deductions remain because the necessity inference is asserted, the strongest secular alternative receives little engagement, and the response does not complete its comparative burden.

Shelly Kagan — section score 81/100

Kagan argues that impartial rational bargainers would objectively prohibit killing innocent people, so Nazi atrocities violate ideal contractual terms regardless of actual approval.

Strongest feature: The reply clearly separates actual social acceptance from ideal rational agreement and produces a determinate condemnation of victimizing innocent people under the contractual procedure. Principal limitation: The asserted prohibition is plausible but compressed, because the bargaining constraints, derivation, and categorical authority of hypothetical agreement are not demonstrated in detail. Live burden: The secular case must show why every appropriately situated rational bargainer would select the prohibition and why that selection binds actual offenders, while the theistic case must engage ideal rather than conventional agreement. Locked score: The strong performance band reflects direct response, stable scope, practical determinacy, and charitable handling of the convention objection, while deductions remain for abbreviated derivation and the unresolved bridge from correct output to ultimate normative authority.
