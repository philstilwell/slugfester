Overall assessment

John Ferrer — Divine grounding: 67/100

Published strengths

  • Precisely distinguished nature’s descriptive facts from categorical moral prescriptions, showing why scientific accounts of consequences alone cannot establish which ends agents genuinely should adopt.
  • Precisely separated sincere desires and beliefs about goodness from goodness itself, exposing why moral action requires truth conditions independent of an agent’s attitudes.
  • Distinguished effective means toward chosen ends from categorically moral action, exposing that conditional goals alone may not explain why goodness itself warrants conduct.
  • Used the sociopath case to separate moral knowledge from empathy and fairness motivation, challenging accounts that treat prosocial impulses as the basis of moral recognition.

Published weaknesses

  • Posited a nonreducible soul as escape from material circularity without establishing its existence, mechanism, or superior reliability, thereby merely relocating the epistemic problem.
  • Claimed Christianization drove abolition and reforms without comparative evidence separating religious influence from political, economic, philosophical, or institutional causes, weakening the inference that morality requires God.
  • Asserted that an unchanging necessary divine mind grounds moral facts, but did not justify its existence or explain why divine attitudes escape relocated subjectivism.

Matt Dillahunty — Secular morality: 83/100

Published strengths

  • Showed that even a chosen wellbeing goal enables publicly assessable comparisons of consequences, providing practical objectivity while candidly acknowledging that wellbeing remains incompletely defined.
  • He rebutted global cognitive skepticism by showing how investigation, replication, prediction, and independent confirmation expose errors, preserving the possibility of publicly grounded knowledge.
  • He showed that incompatible first-person revelations lack a shared resolution procedure, undermining claims that appealing to God automatically settles moral disagreement or motivation.
  • Exposed tension in biblical moral guidance by contrasting minor prohibitions with permitted human ownership, making divine legislative selectivity difficult to dismiss as mere context.
  • Operationalized impartial moral reasoning through the veil of ignorance, universalization, and corrigibility, showing how secular agents can reduce bias despite incomplete information.

Published weaknesses

  • By reducing morality to desire and belief, he initially omitted a correctness standard, leaving moral authority vulnerable to preference and mistaken judgments.
  • His chess analogy established that uncertainty need not defeat goal-relative objectivity, but bounded rules and shared goals left plural moral disagreements insufficiently addressed.
  • He left the prescription explicitly conditional on caring about wellbeing, so it cannot obligate indifferent agents and does not fully answer the challenge of categorical normativity.

Moral foundations, facts, and oughts

07:34–02:00:32

John Ferrer — section score 76/100

Christian ethics grounds mind-independent moral facts in an unchanging, necessary divine mind rather than preferences or natural outcomes, thereby providing morality with a maximally stable foundation.

Strongest feature: Ferrer directly addresses the central grounding burden by identifying an unchanging necessary consciousness as the proposed truth maker for moral facts independent of contingent human attitudes. Principal limitation: The presentation characterizes divine necessity as maximal grounding but supplies insufficient independent justification that this mind exists or that locating value within its attitudes avoids subjectivism rather than relocating it. Live burden: He must establish a principled connection between divine nature and normative authority while explaining why necessary consciousness generates obligations that impersonal facts or shared human interests cannot generate. Locked score: The locked score is 72 in the Competent band because the proposal is relevant coherent and clearly formulated yet remains evidentially underdeveloped at its decisive metaphysical transition.

Matt Dillahunty — section score 81/100

Dillahunty argues God is unnecessary for morality because its operative package needs only a desire to do good and beliefs about goodness, shifting debate to their correctness.

Strongest feature: Dillahunty immediately contests divine necessity by separating motivation from grounding and identifying desire plus belief as operationally sufficient ingredients for recognizable moral conduct. Principal limitation: This formulation risks defining morality through the psychology of agents without yet explaining what makes their beliefs correct or why desired goodness possesses authority beyond preference which Ferrer expressly disputes. Live burden: He must supplement the practical package with a defensible standard for evaluating mistaken desires and beliefs while showing that such evaluation remains genuinely moral rather than merely instrumental coordination. Locked score: The locked score is 68 in the Competent band because the move efficiently narrows the dispute and advances independence but initially leaves the central truth and normativity questions substantially unresolved.

Moral knowledge, reason, and revelation

17:14–01:29:45

John Ferrer — section score 59/100

Ferrer argues that survival-shaped brains must use their own allegedly unreliable judgments to assess evidence, making any naturalistic certification of moral knowledge inescapably circular.

Strongest feature: Ferrer raises a genuinely important epistemic question about whether cognitive systems shaped by survival pressures can independently validate their own reliability when every validation uses those systems. Principal limitation: The argument moves from acknowledged fallibility to broad distrust without evidence that evolution generally disconnects adaptive cognition from truth tracking and its anecdotes do not establish moral unreliability. Live burden: He must demonstrate a systematic defeater for natural cognition rather than universal cognitive mediation while also showing that theistic cognition escapes equivalent dependence on the faculties being assessed. Locked score: The locked score is 60 in the Developing band because the challenge is relevant and intelligible but its central inference overreaches the evidence and threatens skeptical consequences for both positions.

Matt Dillahunty — section score 85/100

Dillahunty argues that revelation offers no way to distinguish divine communication from mistaken conviction, so it cannot provide publicly verifiable moral knowledge for others.

Strongest feature: Dillahunty identifies a decisive public verification problem because observers need criteria that distinguish authentic revelation from psychologically sincere error without assuming the disputed divine source. Principal limitation: The objection addresses accessible knowledge more directly than metaphysical grounding and occasionally compresses sophisticated theological methods into the broad category of massaging inherited texts. Live burden: He must show that publicly correctable secular inquiry performs better in specifically moral disagreements while allowing that privately sourced claims might later obtain independent evidential support. Locked score: The locked score is 81 in the Strong band because the verification challenge is coherent concrete and highly responsive although its rhetorical generalization slightly reduces charity toward stronger revelation based epistemologies.

Scripture, slavery, and historical practice

09:01–01:35:49

John Ferrer — section score 65/100

Ferrer argues societies generally abolished slavery only after thorough Christianization, while Christianity helped end infanticide and gladiatorial games and establish universities, supporting its civilizational moral influence.

Strongest feature: Ferrer broadens the assessment from abstract grounding to historical practice by pointing toward abolition institutional development and reforms as potentially observable fruits of Christian moral formation. Principal limitation: The sweeping historical generalization receives little comparative evidence and cannot separate Christian causation from political economic philosophical or institutional conditions within predominantly Christian societies. Live burden: He must substantiate representative cases address Christian defenses of slavery compare equivalent social conditions and connect favorable historical influence to the stronger claim that morality requires God. Locked score: The locked score is 63 in the Developing band because the examples are relevant to practical influence but evidentially thin and substantially peripheral to exclusive metaphysical necessity.

Matt Dillahunty — section score 83/100

Dillahunty argues Christian abolitionists cannot erase biblical permissions to own and beat slaves, because a perfectly good moral source would unequivocally prohibit treating humans as property.

Strongest feature: Dillahunty distinguishes later Christian abolitionism from the content of the moral guide itself and presses the powerful counterfactual that perfect goodness could clearly prohibit human ownership. Principal limitation: The presentation summarizes several textual permissions without fully developing their legal contexts and moves quickly from morally objectionable passages to conclusions about the source's overall authority. Live burden: He must defend the relevant interpretations against contextual mitigation while explaining why regulation gradualism and competing liberty passages cannot reconcile the texts with perfect moral guidance. Locked score: The locked score is 83 in the Strong band because the objection is direct consequential and highly responsive to Ferrer's historical appeal although fuller textual demonstration would strengthen its evidential foundation.

Motivation, fairness, and practical application

10:58–01:21:40

John Ferrer — section score 65/100

Ferrer argues secular ethics often center on one or two principles like liberty, pleasure, or survival, leaving too little foundation for sacrifice, courage, loyalty, and martyrdom.

Strongest feature: Ferrer directs attention to motivational depth and correctly asks whether a moral framework can justify demanding commitments that exceed immediate pleasure liberty survival or narrow personal advantage. Principal limitation: The characterization of secular ethics as commonly minimalist is asserted without representative evidence and overlooks frameworks containing duties virtues contractual obligations impartiality and concern for future generations. Live burden: He must compare developed secular theories rather than simplified examples and demonstrate that Christian grounding uniquely justifies proportionate sacrifice without also licensing destructive loyalty or needless martyrdom. Locked score: The locked score is 60 in the Developing band because the practical challenge matters but rests on an underdeveloped comparison that insufficiently represents the resources available to the opposing position.

Matt Dillahunty — section score 82/100

Dillahunty argues chess’s invented rules and goal still distinguish better moves despite unknown optimal play, showing incomplete moral knowledge need not prevent objective comparisons.

Strongest feature: The chess analogy separates uncertainty from arbitrariness: invented rules and avoiding defeat still make positions and sequences objectively better, even when players cannot identify the optimal move. Principal limitation: Chess supplies bounded rules, determinate outcome, shared participation, whereas the excerpt establishes neither a precise moral goal nor a method for comparing interpersonal consequences and rival improvements. Live burden: Dillahunty must show that morality has shared ends and evaluative constraints for comparable goal-relative objectivity, especially when disagreement concerns the governing goal itself rather than incomplete knowledge about which means advances it. Locked score: The locked score remains 84 in the Strong band because the analogy directly defeats the inference from imperfect understanding to evaluative ignorance, while leaving moral goal-selection and application substantively unresolved.
