Overall assessment

Eric Hernandez — A nonphysical human soul exists: 72/100

Published strengths

  • Hernandez precisely distinguished neural correlation, causal dependence, and metaphysical identity, producing the pro side's strongest move against an overextended physicalist inference.
  • He directly challenged Jump's reasons-or-randomness dichotomy with a recognizable agent-causal alternative and consistently connected rational agency to the soul question.
  • His private-access argument accurately identified how neuroscience depends on first-person reports when mapping phenomenal experience, preserving a genuine epistemological limitation.

Published weaknesses

  • The personhood argument assumed that categorical status cannot supervene on graded physical organization, then inferred a transcendent ground without excluding threshold alternatives.
  • The property-difference case shifted among beliefs, neurons, and whole brains, leaving its Leibniz-law comparison vulnerable to level and category objections.
  • Calling interaction primitive supplied no psychophysical law, test, or constraint, so the proposal defended possibility more effectively than explanatory superiority.

Tom Jump — Free will and consciousness do not establish a nonphysical soul: 78/100

Published strengths

  • Jump's arithmetic response decisively separated reasons-responsive determination from arbitrary output, directly answering the magic-eight-ball undercutter.
  • His emergence examples supplied concrete countermodels to the inference that properties absent from components require an additional nonphysical substance.
  • He repeatedly advanced a comparative predictive standard, explaining how neural models remain testable despite private experience and logically possible alternatives.

Published weaknesses

  • The round-square analogy treated agent causation as contradictory without deriving a contradiction, weakening an otherwise important coherence challenge.
  • The spectrum reply did not fully distinguish graded personhood-related capacities from a potentially discrete threshold status or equal moral standing.
  • The truth-external-to-brain response compressed truth-bearers, truth-makers, and neural representations, leaving substantial portions of Hernandez's property argument unanswered.

Determinism and rational justification

3:10–3:49

Eric Hernandez — section score 68/100

Hernandez compares a deterministic brain with a magic eight ball, arguing that mechanically produced beliefs lack truth-directed justification and thereby undercut confidence in determinism.

Strongest feature: Hernandez presents a memorable epistemological undercutter, contending that determinism threatens not merely freedom but confidence in the truth-directed reliability of every belief generated by deterministic cognition. Principal limitation: the magic-eight-ball comparison assimilates causal determination to arbitrary entertainment machinery, although deterministic cognitive processes may remain systematically evidence-sensitive, inferentially disciplined, and substantially more reliable than random outputs. Live burden: he must establish that causal determination specifically prevents epistemic warrant, explain why libertarian origination improves reliability, and connect that result to a nonphysical soul rather than simply to indeterministic cognition. Locked score: 68 points, reflecting substantial clarity, direct relevance, and argumentative ambition, alongside insufficient support for the crucial transition from causally produced belief to epistemically discredited belief.

Tom Jump — section score 83/100

Jump answers that reasons determine his belief that two plus two equals four, showing determination can track truth rather than operate like arbitrary machinery.

Strongest feature: Jump directly distinguishes determination by rationally relevant considerations from arbitrary mechanical production, using elementary arithmetic to demonstrate that constraint can constitute cognitive competence rather than epistemological corruption. Principal limitation: mathematical calculation is an especially favorable domain involving exceptionally determinate standards, so the example alone does not establish compatibilist agency across morally significant deliberation, uncertainty, or conflicting practical reasons. Live burden: he must generalize reasons-responsive determination beyond necessary arithmetic, specify how determined deliberation retains meaningful authorship, and clarify why defeating Hernandez's undercutter reduces the evidential case for a nonphysical soul. Locked score: 83 points, recognizing excellent reply contact, economical counterexample construction, and strong conceptual precision, while reserving credit because the broader theory of agency remains incompletely developed.

The coherence of libertarian free will

23:02–28:40

Eric Hernandez — section score 72/100

Hernandez proposes himself as a first mover who freely chooses according to reasons, offering agent causation as an alternative to determination and randomness.

Strongest feature: Hernandez identifies the precise exhaustiveness premise requiring denial and supplies a recognizable agent-causal alternative in which a person originates action while remaining responsive to intelligible reasons. Principal limitation: describing the person as a first mover does not explain how an undetermined selection remains controlled rather than arbitrary, and the asserted counterexample therefore risks renaming the disputed possibility instead of demonstrating it. Live burden: he must specify how reasons influence without necessitating, provide a noncircular criterion distinguishing authorship from randomness, and establish why this originating agent must be nonphysical. Locked score: 72 points, crediting unusually direct responsiveness and a philosophically established countermodel, but withholding stronger credit because its decisive control relationship and connection to the soul remain underexplained.

Tom Jump — section score 67/100

Jump argues every decision follows reasons and is determined, or lacks reasons and is random; choosing reasons recursively reproduces the same exhaustive dilemma.

Strongest feature: Jump formulates a compact reasons-or-no-reasons dilemma and anticipates regress by observing that selecting which considerations govern a decision seemingly requires either further reasons or their absence. Principal limitation: the dichotomy assumes that reasons must be causally sufficient or explanatorily absent, leaving insufficient engagement with agent-causal accounts where reasons guide, justify, and probabilistically influence without necessitating an outcome. Live burden: he must demonstrate that nondetermining rational explanation is incoherent rather than definitionally excluding it, then show why failure of libertarian freedom bears specifically upon soul existence. Locked score: 70 points, reflecting considerable relevance, structural economy, and pressure on unexplained origination, moderated because the dilemma's purported exhaustiveness remains the central contested premise rather than an independently established conclusion.

Empirical evidence about consciousness

31:46–37:12

Eric Hernandez — section score 81/100

Hernandez distinguishes neural correlation or causation from identity, arguing that brain damage affecting consciousness does not establish that consciousness itself is physically identical.

Strongest feature: Hernandez precisely identifies the inferential distance between neural dependence, causal interaction, correlation, and strict metaphysical identity, directly conceding the observations while disputing what they establish. Principal limitation: defeating a deductive identity inference does not neutralize abductive evidence, because systematic intervention effects may still favor physical realization unless dualism independently predicts their detailed structure comparably well. Live burden: he must formulate discriminating expectations for a nonphysical consciousness, explain why lesion and intervention patterns fit those expectations, and advance beyond compatibility toward positive evidence for a soul. Locked score: 86 points, reflecting exceptional reply contact, logical precision, calibrated concession, and relevance to the disputed inference, with residual limitations concerning comparative confirmation and the absence of independently derived dualist predictions.

Tom Jump — section score 80/100

Jump argues that material production predicts brain damage will alter consciousness; observed alterations therefore provide empirical reason to prefer a physical account of consciousness.

Strongest feature: Jump presents an empirically oriented model-comparison argument in which a material-production hypothesis generates a concrete expectation that physical brain damage should systematically alter conscious functioning. Principal limitation: the observed dependence does not uniquely select production over interaction, transmission, or embodiment models, each of which may also anticipate impairment when the neural interface is damaged. Live burden: he must identify predictions that differ across those hypotheses, provide more detailed intervention evidence, and justify moving from confirmation of neural dependence to rejection of a nonphysical subject. Locked score: 79 points, recognizing strong relevance, testability, and legitimate evidential support for material processes, while discounting the move because its stated prediction is not exclusive and therefore cannot independently resolve the debate's ontology.

Mind, brain, identity, and private experience

48:41–94:06

Eric Hernandez — section score 73/100

Hernandez argues consciousness is nonphysical because mental properties are not properties of brains, and brain properties are correspondingly absent from conscious mental states.

Strongest feature: Hernandez offers a formally recognizable property-difference route to nonidentity, invoking a legitimate principle that genuinely identical entities cannot possess incompatible properties under the same description and conditions. Principal limitation: the move initially stipulates rather than establishes the relevant differences, and nonidentity between mental descriptions and brain descriptions would not alone entail a separately existing nonphysical substance. Live burden: he must compare properly matched identity candidates, address relational and representational predicates, exclude emergence or property dualism, and demonstrate the additional inference from mental irreducibility to a soul. Locked score: 70 points, recognizing clear relevance, concise structure, and a potentially powerful metaphysical framework, while reflecting limited evidential warrant and substantial unresolved alternatives at both major inferential stages.

Tom Jump — section score 79/100

Jump compares brains with oceans and stars, arguing that collections possess properties absent from individual components without becoming separate nonphysical substances.

Strongest feature: Jump supplies concrete counterexamples to the inference from property difference to substance difference, showing that organized wholes routinely possess characteristics not attributable to their individual constituent particles. Principal limitation: the examples establish the coherence of emergence generally but do not explain subjective experience specifically, and comparing consciousness with individual neurons may miss an identity claim concerning the organized brain as a whole. Live burden: he must map the cited mental properties onto system-level physical organization, answer Hernandez's strongest same-level formulation, and explain rather than merely label phenomenal emergence. Locked score: 85 points, reflecting excellent responsiveness, familiar evidence, conceptual economy, and a decisive challenge to the original inference, with remaining deductions for the unresolved explanatory gap surrounding consciousness itself.

Personhood as a route to the soul

80:09–83:45

Eric Hernandez — section score 61/100

Hernandez argues losing limbs or having only part of a brain does not make someone fractionally personal, so non-degreed personhood cannot be grounded physically.

Strongest feature: Hernandez contrasts continuously variable bodily composition with apparently categorical personhood, using limb loss and reduced brain volume to raise a genuine question about unity and numerical identity. Principal limitation: categorical classifications can supervene upon graded physical structures or organizational thresholds, and the examples do not establish that every amount of neurologically significant loss leaves personhood wholly unchanged. Live burden: he must define personhood, separate moral status from cognitive capacity, defend strict non-gradability across difficult cases, and establish why only a nonphysical soul can ground the category. Locked score: 67 points, recognizing intuitive relevance and a concrete metaphysical challenge, while reflecting weak warrant for the universal premise and an unsupported inference from physical variability to transcendent personhood.

Tom Jump — section score 75/100

Jump cites total brain absence and progressively reduced brain capacity, arguing personhood-related consciousness and interaction form a spectrum even when boundaries remain uncertain.

Strongest feature: Jump tests Hernandez's categorical premise against neurologically significant limiting cases, noting that consciousness, interaction, viability, and other personhood-relevant capacities plausibly diminish across a physical spectrum. Principal limitation: graded underlying capacities do not automatically establish that personhood itself is graded, because a categorical status may arise at a threshold whose precise boundary remains epistemically uncertain. Live burden: he must specify constitutive criteria, explain how their aggregation produces personhood, and address moral equality and persistence without presupposing that neurological dependence settles the ontology. Locked score: 75 points, crediting substantial reply contact, empirically grounded pressure, and appropriate acknowledgment of boundary uncertainty, while reflecting the unresolved distinction between continuous person-making properties and discrete personal status.

The soul-body interaction problem

68:32–105:23

Eric Hernandez — section score 77/100

Hernandez argues nonphysical consciousness is possessed by an indexical self not identical to brain or body, identifying that conscious possessor as the soul.

Strongest feature: Hernandez finally articulates the cumulative bridge to the motion, identifying the soul with the first-person subject that possesses consciousness and is allegedly irreducible to brain and body. Principal limitation: the move depends upon earlier property and privacy arguments whose irreducibility conclusions remain contested, while possession language does not establish that consciousness requires a separate substantial owner. Live burden: he must demonstrate subject unity beyond physical integration, exclude property-dualist and emergent alternatives, and provide identity conditions showing how this possessor persists and interacts. Locked score: 69 points, recognizing direct relevance, synthetic value, and conceptual clarity, but reflecting limited independent warrant and an inference that substantially redescribes the desired conclusion instead of conclusively establishing nonphysical existence.

Tom Jump — section score 82/100

Jump argues an entity that cannot be independently assessed is evidentially indistinguishable from imagination, leaving no adequate reason to believe the proposed soul exists.

Strongest feature: Jump converts the soul's proposed invisibility into an epistemological challenge, demanding some independently assessable consequence that distinguishes the entity from indefinitely many imaginable additions to reality. Principal limitation: direct assessment is not required for every legitimate theoretical entity, which may be inferred through explanatory consequences, and the objection does not yet show that unity, agency, or privacy supply no indirect evidence. Live burden: he must formulate an appropriate testability standard, evaluate the pro side's abductive considerations individually, and demonstrate that the soul adds no constrained explanatory success. Locked score: 74 points, reflecting strong relevance, clarity, and useful evidential discipline, tempered by limited charity toward indirect metaphysical inference and an overbroad suggestion that present unobservability approximates imagination.
