Overall assessment

William Lane Craig — Resurrection case: 86/100

Published strengths

  • Framed the resurrection as a comparative historical explanation of an explicit five-part dataset.
  • Replaced a categorical extraordinary-evidence slogan with a precise likelihood-ratio principle.
  • Conditioned miracle priors on independently supported theism rather than assuming a naturalistic background.
  • Answered hallucination and legendary-development alternatives through explanatory scope, power, chronology, and early attestation.
  • Distinguished experiential warrant from indefeasibility and expressly acknowledged genuine factual defeaters.

Published weaknesses

  • The equal-standard reply did not answer the decisive bones hypothetical and left the relationship between experiential warrant and conclusive counterevidence insufficiently explicit.
  • Several central claims compressed scholarly agreement, source independence, psychological limits, and rival comparisons beyond what the displayed evidence established.

Alex O'Connor — Historical skepticism: 88/100

Published strengths

  • Directly tested whether public factual contradiction would override private experiential warrant.
  • Turned alleged divine intention into an expected-evidence challenge to ordinary historical standards.
  • Identified the unresolved prior-probability gap between weak naturalistic alternatives and a probable resurrection.
  • Separated sincere conviction from truth and preserved grief-related error as a live possibility.
  • Challenged the reliability of the underlying reports before accepting them as facts requiring explanation.
  • Used increasing narrative elaboration to develop a concrete legendary-growth alternative.

Published weaknesses

  • The low-prior objection left its reference class, magnitude, and treatment of independently supported theistic background largely unspecified.
  • The grief-hallucination proposal remained underdeveloped as a complete explanation of group claims, bodily interpretation, and the disputed empty tomb.
  • The legendary-development inference did not fully exclude literary selection, genre, or an early stable core as alternative explanations of variation.

Historical Proof, Evidential Standards, and Prior Probability

33:37–41:23

William Lane Craig — section score 88/100

Even ordinary evidence can establish a low-prior extraordinary event when it is far likelier if that event occurred than if it did not.

Strongest feature: The move replaces an evidential slogan with a formally-explicit likelihood-ratio principle, explaining that ordinary-form evidence can overcome a low prior when substantially expected under resurrection than under its negation. Principal limitation: The abstract principle supplies no likelihood estimates for the evidence and only partially answers O'Connor, leaving unresolved his claim that a God seeking belief should provide public evidence. Live burden: Craig must apply the framework to historical data, alternative hypotheses, and expectations generated by divine intention, rather than resting on the formal possibility of a sufficiently favorable ratio. Locked score: The very strong band reflects conceptual precision, careful calibration, central relevance, and charitable probabilistic framing, moderated by incomplete responsiveness and the remaining application gap.

Alex O'Connor — section score 87/100

If an omnipotent God strongly wants resurrection belief, merely ordinary historical evidence falls below what that purposive hypothesis predicts.

Strongest feature: The reply challenges equal treatment of ordinary and supernatural historical claims: an omnipotent agent strongly desiring belief should predict unusually clear public evidence, making divine intention evidentially relevant rather than rhetorical. Principal limitation: God's asserted desire remains insufficiently defended, while the move neither specifies the evidence that intention predicts nor addresses competing divine purposes involving freedom, distance, or noncoercive disclosure. Live burden: O'Connor must independently justify the theological premise and articulate a precise comparative expectation showing why the surviving historical record is substantially less probable under purposive divine action. Locked score: The very strong 85–94 band reflects direct central-burden contact, careful interrogative calibration, strong responsiveness, and charitable engagement, tempered by an implicitly bounded intention premise and limited evidential specification.

Core Historical Data and Source Reliability

41:40–56:59

William Lane Craig — section score 85/100

Crucifixion, burial, empty tomb, appearances, and transformed disciples are historical data best explained by the disciples' resurrection hypothesis.

Strongest feature: The constructive enumerates the historical data and links them to resurrection through explanatory criteria, supplying the architecture for a comparative best-explanation case rather than an isolated appeal to testimony. Principal limitation: Broad scholarly acceptance is implicitly qualified, sources are compressed, and rival accounts receive insufficient development, so the claimed best explanation outruns the displayed defense of the data and comparison. Live burden: Craig must establish every indispensable fact with independent evidence and demonstrate that resurrection outperforms source-critical, psychological, and legendary alternatives without presuming disputed reliability. Locked score: The strong/competent band reflects structure, coherent relevance, and a recoverable comparative thesis, while limited warrant, incompletely qualified consensus, and no substantive strongest-alternative charity test constrain this constructive span.

Alex O'Connor — section score 90/100

Disciple beliefs, group appearances, and post-resurrection reports may be unreliable rather than established facts already demanding a supernatural explanation.

Strongest feature: The move confronts explanatory arguments at their evidential foundation, citing Paul's isolated five-hundred reference and uneven gospel appearance reporting while carefully presenting unreliability as a possibility rather than alleging fabrication. Principal limitation: Its discussion of source dependence, chronology, genre, and tradition remains abbreviated, because variation among accounts alone cannot establish that minimal content is historically unreliable. Live burden: O'Connor must distinguish later elaboration from a recoverable core, identify which indispensable claims fail historical criteria, and explain how correlated testimony, divergent reporting, and silence should be weighed. Locked score: The very strong band recognizes exceptional responsiveness, calibrated skepticism, identification of disputed reports, relevance, and charitable insistence that the data be established first, tempered by incomplete source-critical development.

Fabrication, Hallucination, and Legendary Development

47:39–61:27

William Lane Craig — section score 88/100

Hallucination neither naturally explains bodily-resurrection belief nor accounts for the publicly inspectable empty tomb, leaving explanatory power and scope deficient.

Strongest feature: The response grants hallucination as the strongest sincere-error proposal and evaluates it under two explanatory criteria, addressing bodily-resurrection belief and the public empty-tomb datum instead of dismissing the alternative by label. Principal limitation: Its categorical insufficiency exceeds the psychological and historical support provided, because hallucination phenomena receive no analysis while the empty tomb's independent establishment and inspectability remain contested premises. Live burden: Craig must substantiate psychological limitations, establish the empty tomb independently, and compare hallucination combined with source development or misinterpretation instead of testing a single-cause version. Locked score: The very strong band reflects coherent two-part analysis, central responsiveness, precise burden contact, and charity, while evidential compression and reliance on disputed data restrain the conclusion.

Alex O'Connor — section score 87/100

Disciples may sincerely die for a mistaken resurrection belief, perhaps originating in grief-related hallucination, without knowingly promoting falsehood.

Strongest feature: The reply cleanly distinguishes known falsehood from sincere mistake, grants that costly commitment legitimately evidences sincerity, and proportionately presents grief-related hallucination as a possibility without claiming historical establishment. Principal limitation: The suggested psychological mechanism remains underdeveloped, offering no treatment of multiple experiences, group reports, bodily-resurrection interpretation, the empty tomb, or how grief phenomena could generate and stabilize the broader tradition. Live burden: O'Connor must construct a sincere-error account explaining the accepted data while defending which reports demand explanation and which may be later or unreliable. Locked score: The very strong band reflects responsiveness, precision, calibration, and representational charity, tempered by comparatively thin positive evidence and an incomplete account of the entire dataset.

Experiential Warrant, Falsification, and Practical Stakes

23:23–33:40

William Lane Craig — section score 78/100

Personal experience of God can rationally ground Christian belief without argument, although genuinely contradictory facts should defeat that belief.

Strongest feature: The move distinguishes immediate experiential warrant from indefeasibility, permits factual contradiction to overturn belief, and articulates a coherent, recoverable, qualified epistemic position instead of unrestricted private conviction. Principal limitation: Its warrant remains asserted, since the passage provides no explanation for why divine experience deserves this epistemic standing or how misleading evidence can be reliably differentiated from an authentic defeater. Live burden: Craig needs an intelligible procedure for identifying veridical religious experience and reconciling conflicts between personal seeming and public historical evidence while preserving meaningful correction for both categories. Locked score: The strong/competent band reflects exceptional conceptual clarity and calibration alongside constrained evidential development and merely subsidiary engagement with the debate's central historical burden.

Alex O'Connor — section score 90/100

If Jesus's bones were conclusively identified, that public factual contradiction should override religious experience and defeat resurrection belief.

Strongest feature: The stipulated discovery tests Craig's factual-defeater condition, isolates a checkable contradiction of bodily resurrection, and charitably examines whether experiential warrant would yield under the pertinent factual counterexample. Principal limitation: Because the example is hypothetical and conclusive, it illuminates falsifiability's structure more effectively than it provides present evidence against resurrection, leaving authentication standards for purported remains unexplored. Live burden: O'Connor must connect this conditional test to attainable historical evidence or formulate an adjudicative principle explaining when less-than-conclusive public evidence should outweigh powerful personal experience. Locked score: The very strong band reflects exceptional responsiveness, coherence, precision, and charitable engagement with Craig's stated qualification, while recognizing a subsidiary falsification test that does not independently resolve the historical case.
