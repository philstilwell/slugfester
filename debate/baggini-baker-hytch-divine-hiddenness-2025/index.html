Overall assessment

Julian Baggini — Atheistic hiddenness critique: 80/100

Published strengths

  • Baggini's status-versus-existence distinction and geographic-luck objection directly confront the noncoercive relationship defense, making this unusually focused response his strongest scored rebuttal within the exchange.
  • He consistently accepts probabilistic standards, concedes generic ultimacy and religion's practical goods, and thereby presents a calibrated critique rather than claiming deductive disproof.
  • His cultural-contingency argument connects birthplace, historical setting, and involuntary character formation to a substantive challenge for doctrines making correct recognition morally or eternally crucial.
  • His account of culturally shaped experience clearly distinguishes private psychological force from public discriminatory evidence, directly answering the shared premise that experience often grounds faith.

Published weaknesses

  • The opening simplicity inference does not specify expected divine disclosure or compare competing divine purposes, leaving its evidential warrant materially below its direct motion relevance.
  • The claim that historical evidence for Jesus's divinity requires prior faith overstates the historical skepticism and insufficiently distinguishes ordinary biographical claims from supernatural conclusions.
  • The discussion of religion's communal goods largely redirects from Baker-Hytch's origin-versus-truth distinction, producing weak response contact despite its charitable and nuanced ethical framing.

Max Baker-Hytch — Christian evidential-ambiguity defense: 82/100

Published strengths

  • Baker-Hytch's deductive-versus-probabilistic distinction concedes hiddenness as powerful evidence while properly requiring comparison with positive evidence, thereby anchoring his strongest central framework for evaluating the dispute.
  • His historical reply carefully separates academically secure claims about Jesus from the much larger inference to incarnation, combining strong response contact with exceptional calibration.
  • He repeatedly narrows claims rather than overreaching, conceding experiential diversity, culturally accidental doctrine, honest error, and the inability of religious experience alone to decide among religions.
  • His final cumulative case integrates philosophical, historical, and experiential considerations without calling them proof, directly answering the demand for evidence on the other side of hiddenness.

Published weaknesses

  • The noncoercive relationship defense relies on an imperfect disguised-king analogy and unsupported psychology, without explaining why clearer existence would necessarily distort free relational response.
  • The sincere-seeker reply establishes uncertainty about motives but offers no positive account of genuinely open nonbelief, leaving the central hiddenness case substantially unanswered.
  • The selective-skepticism discussion states a sound general principle but never demonstrates that Baggini actually applies unequal scrutiny to theistic and atheistic evidence in this debate.
