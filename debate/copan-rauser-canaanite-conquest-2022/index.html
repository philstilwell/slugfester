Overall assessment

Paul Copan — The conquest can be morally defended: 82/100

Published strengths

  • Integrates enemy-love and severe judgment within one qualified canonical framework rather than denying either strand.
  • Uses multiple textual patterns of hyperbole and surviving peoples to narrow literal extermination claims.
  • Distinguishes religious-political institutions from ethnicity through examples of inclusion and continuing Canaanite presence.

Published weaknesses

  • Never supplies conquest-specific necessity and proportionality sufficient to justify any residual civilian deaths.
  • The hijacked-airplane analogy leaves direct targeting materially different from foreseen collateral loss.

Randal Rauser — The moral defense is inadequate: 85/100

Published strengths

  • Concedes hyperbole and Copan’s nuance before isolating the unresolved civilian-harm burden.
  • Maintains Christian inspiration commitments while articulating a coherent alternative account of human moral error.
  • Presses specific textual and moral evidence rather than relying only on totalizing conquest formulas.

Published weaknesses

  • Moves too quickly from coerced identity removal to the technical legal classification of genocide.
  • Uses another scholar’s future-war openness in a way that risks overextending criticism to Copan.

Scripture and interpretive method

4:34–45:40

Paul Copan — section score 80/100

Copan argues that Christ-centered interpretation should preserve one divine character across both Testaments, because Jesus and New Testament authors retain Mosaic authority despite acknowledged tensions.

Strongest feature: Copan constructs a coherent canonical framework that joins Jesus’s enemy-love, Mosaic authority, and acknowledged interpretive tension without pretending that every difficult passage has been resolved. Principal limitation: The cited continuity demonstrates that New Testament writers retain Old Testament authority, but it leaves substantially implicit the morally decisive bridge from that general continuity to the permissibility of conquest conduct involving civilians. Live burden: Copan still must show why the particular judgment, rather than judgment in principle, accords with the loving divine character his Christ-centered method treats as controlling. Locked score: 82 reflects a well-calibrated and directly relevant opening framework whose theological coherence exceeds its act-specific evidential support and whose qualifications properly constrain its conclusion.

Randal Rauser — section score 86/100

Rauser separates divine purpose from fallible human intent, arguing that inspired scripture can contain morally mistaken portrayals while Jesus and God-given intuition guide interpretation.

Strongest feature: Rauser provides a coherent Christian route between uncritical endorsement and rejection of scripture by distinguishing divine inclusion of a text from fallible human intention while expressly affirming plenary inspiration. Principal limitation: His analogies and appeal to God-given moral intuition make the proposal intelligible, but they do not yet supply a sufficiently discriminating method for identifying when an authorial moral judgment is erroneous rather than merely difficult. Live burden: Rauser must explain how his fallible intuition and fallible hermeneutic can adjudicate this case without simply allowing the preferred moral conclusion to determine the textual reading. Locked score: 86 reflects an unusually responsive, charitable, and precisely qualified alternative whose remaining vulnerability lies in the evidential criteria governing its application.

Historicity and war rhetoric

22:57–39:24

Paul Copan — section score 87/100

Copan uses ancient victory exaggeration, merisms, localized battles, and later survivors to argue that Joshua preserves a historical core without reporting literal total extermination.

Strongest feature: Copan combines conventional war boasts, totalizing merisms, localized battle descriptions, and internal survivor evidence into a mutually reinforcing textual case against uniformly literal extermination language. Principal limitation: These considerations substantially reduce the scope of the reported destruction, but they do not establish that no civilians were killed or that the remaining violence was morally justified. Live burden: Copan must move from genre-sensitive limitation of numerical claims to an account of the actual targets, residual victims, and morally sufficient reasons for whatever historical violence remains. Locked score: 87 reflects a strong, specific, and well-calibrated central argument whose cumulative evidence successfully narrows the problem while appropriately stopping short of resolving the separate and morally decisive civilian-harm burden.

Randal Rauser — section score 86/100

Rauser contrasts uncertainty about Joshua’s composition with firmer gospel dating and Christian centrality, concluding that Jericho need not carry the historical weight borne by resurrection claims.

Strongest feature: Rauser carefully limits his claim to unequal evidential and theological weight, using the contrast with Mark to justify caution without asserting that Joshua is fictional or disproved. Principal limitation: A wide compositional dating range and lower doctrinal centrality do not themselves determine whether the narrated conquest has a historical core, so the comparison offers only indirect evidence about the reliability of the particular events described in the disputed conquest narrative. Live burden: Rauser must connect literary uncertainty to a warranted estimate of what occurred, or else show why moral interpretation can proceed independently of unresolved historical questions. Locked score: 79 reflects a calibrated and clearly scoped cautionary argument that legitimately opens interpretive alternatives but remains subsidiary to the motion and underdeveloped as historical evidence.

Civilian harm and moral responsibility

39:22–48:25

Paul Copan — section score 81/100

Copan argues that military citadels, the priority of driving Canaanites out, and abundant survivors make indiscriminate civilian extermination a misleading account of the campaign.

Strongest feature: Copan offers a concrete alternative account of the targets by joining Hess’s military-citadel thesis, the priority of expulsion, and textual survival despite language of complete obedience. Principal limitation: The archaeological support for identifying these sites as military citadels remains authority-dependent and does not exclude civilian presence, while the reply does not fully resolve Rauser’s specific evidence concerning Ai and less-mobile inhabitants. Live burden: Copan must establish that civilians were neither intended targets nor predictably treated as such, and must morally account for any residual noncombatant deaths that the military-site classification leaves possible. Locked score: 81 reflects a central and relevant response that materially challenges an indiscriminate-extermination description but remains evidentially incomplete at the exact point where civilian presence matters most.

Randal Rauser — section score 90/100

Rauser answers the citadel thesis with Ai’s men and women, absent sparing instructions, and close-contact weapons, arguing that reduced numbers still leave intentional civilian killing.

Strongest feature: Rauser directly confronts the citadel defense by combining the textual reference to men and women, the absence of a sparing directive, and the intentional mechanics of sword-and-spear killing. Principal limitation: The argument strongly supports a residual civilian burden within the presented record, but it cannot determine exact numbers and should not absorb the more sweeping genocide defenses that Rauser explicitly distinguishes from Copan’s nuanced view. Live burden: Rauser must preserve the distinction between direct targeting and foreseen collateral death while showing that the cited narrative makes the former the better account of any noncombatants present. Locked score: 90 reflects highly responsive, precise, and charitable motion-level pressure whose evidential conclusion is powerful even while properly limited to indeterminate civilian harm.

Identity removal, herem, and genocide

48:25–1:07:07

Paul Copan — section score 85/100

Copan distinguishes dismantling corrupt religious-political institutions from destroying an ethnicity, citing Rahab, covenant inclusion, possible peace, and continued Canaanite presence as evidence.

Strongest feature: Copan supports the ideology-versus-people distinction with several internal examples of inclusion, possible peace, covenant participation, and continuing Canaanite presence rather than relying only on the Nazi comparison. Principal limitation: The examples show that some Canaanites could survive and join Israel, but they do not alone establish that coercive removal of identity markers avoided wrongful group harm or that every campaign action targeted institutions rather than persons. Live burden: Copan must connect his inclusive textual evidence to the actual conduct and demonstrate that dismantling pernicious practices did not function through prohibited treatment of civilians. Locked score: 86 reflects a coherent, well-supported, and directly responsive distinction whose strong evidential breadth leaves a separate unresolved burden concerning methods and residual harm.

Randal Rauser — section score 81/100

Rauser argues that forcible expulsion and cultural erasure can destroy a religious or cultural group even without universal killing, invoking genocide language and residential schools.

Strongest feature: Rauser shows that grave group destruction need not require killing every member, connecting forcible expulsion, cultural erasure, a stated convention definition, and the residential-school analogy. Principal limitation: The move shifts too quickly from the moral seriousness of identity destruction to the technical classification of genocide, without fully establishing the required protected-group category, destructive intent, or precise fit between the convention’s enumerated acts and the historically disputed Canaanite case. Live burden: Rauser must defend the legal-moral bridge rather than treating any coerced removal of religious identity as sufficient by definition, while retaining his conceded hyperbole and Copan’s nuance. Locked score: 81 reflects a relevant and forceful challenge whose central moral insight exceeds the precision and calibration of its categorical legal conclusion.

Christological alternatives and exceptional judgment

1:13:59–1:25:19

Paul Copan — section score 78/100

Copan argues that canonical severity, conflicting intuitions, and wider contextual facts can revise initial moral judgments, illustrated by Abraham expecting Isaac’s return.

Strongest feature: Copan coherently explains the general epistemic point that additional facts can revise an initial moral intuition, using Abraham’s expectation about Isaac to illustrate how context changes an apparent command. Principal limitation: The analogy establishes defeasibility in principle but supplies no comparably specific conquest facts showing necessity, proportionality, last-resort status, or protective safeguards sufficient to override the powerful intuition against civilian harm. Live burden: Copan must provide the case-specific information that performs the overruling work, rather than asking humility and canonical severity to preserve a bare possibility of exception. Locked score: 77 reflects a notably charitable and conceptually legitimate reply whose general principle remains underwarranted and insufficiently responsive to Rauser’s concrete child-killing objection.

Randal Rauser — section score 83/100

Rauser contrasts Jesus embracing a child with guiding close-contact killing, arguing that fallible but basic moral intuition makes such a divine command Christologically implausible.

Strongest feature: Rauser makes the shared Christian stakes exceptionally concrete by contrasting Jesus embracing a child with Jesus guiding a spear, while explicitly acknowledging that both intuition and interpretation are fallible. Principal limitation: The vivid thought experiment gains much of its force from assuming the disputed residual-civilian and direct-targeting premises, and moral intuition remains a contested warrant rather than an independently defended decision procedure. Live burden: Rauser must show why this intuition should govern when it conflicts with Copan’s canonical-severity evidence, without treating emotional vividness as immunity from contextual qualification. Locked score: 84 reflects a precise, memorable, and strongly relevant Christological test whose responsiveness and clarity exceed the developed evidential basis for making the intuition decisive.
