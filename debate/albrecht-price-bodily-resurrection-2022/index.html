Overall assessment

William Albrecht — Bodily resurrection case: 78/100

Published strengths

  • Established an early Pauline proclamation of burial, resurrection, and named appearances with direct central-burden relevance.
  • Used Ignatius's fleshly-and-spiritual predicates to rebut the claim that spiritual necessarily means immaterial.
  • Separated ambiguous Attis iconography from later explicit writings with careful scope and calibration.
  • Distinguished Justin's recognition of similarities from chronological priority and narrative equivalence.

Published weaknesses

  • The experiences argument did not adequately distinguish sincere postmortem experiences from externally caused bodily encounters.
  • The martyrdom and growth replies shifted among witnesses and believers while leaving provenance and ordinary social explanations substantially unanswered.

Robert Price — Historical-analogy skepticism: 80/100

Published strengths

  • Clearly distinguished divine possibility or faith from historical evidence that an event actually occurred.
  • Directly challenged traditional authorship and independent verification of the asserted apostolic transmission chain.
  • Pressed Paul's immediate context and the apparent tension between spiritual-body and flesh-and-bones language.
  • Separated martyr sincerity from factual truth while supplying ordinary mechanisms for movement growth.

Published weaknesses

  • The comparative-religion case insufficiently defined equivalence among Attis, Osiris, Baal, and the Christian resurrection claim.
  • Several source objections became overly categorical, including claims of total unverifiability or near-complete ignorance concerning apostles and traditions.

Historical Method, Miracle Possibility, and Evidential Burden

14:43–35:38

William Albrecht — section score 74/100

Scholarly concessions that disciples experienced appearances are offered as historical evidence, while a merely visionary interpretation is rejected as insufficiently descriptive.

Strongest feature: The argument secures meaningful common ground by citing scholarly acknowledgment of postmortem experiences, placing a historically consequential datum directly before the motion-level question of what best explains the appearances. Principal limitation: Experience, appearance, vision, and bodily encounter remain insufficiently differentiated, so conceding sincere experiences cannot by itself establish their sensory character, external cause, or resurrection-producing significance. Live burden: The affirmative must comparatively evaluate bereavement visions, religious experiences, memory development, and physical encounter hypotheses rather than treating the absence of explicitly visionary wording as a decisive discriminator. Locked score: The mixed band reflects exceptional relevance and a recoverable historical premise, but also materially overstated calibration, limited alternative testing, and an unresolved inferential bridge from experiences to bodily resurrection.

Robert Price — section score 88/100

Historical inference should follow credible experience; absent believable modern resurrections and available ancient mythic narratives therefore weigh against this resurrection report.

Strongest feature: The reply directly confronts the motion-level inference by introducing experiential analogy, comparative testimony, and the conspicuous absence of credible modern resurrection cases as historically relevant controls. Principal limitation: Credibility, cultic advocacy, resurrection, and mythic analogy remain loosely specified, while movement from absent familiar cases toward probable fiction proceeds faster than the supplied comparative evidence warrants. Live burden: The skeptic must articulate applicable base rates, evaluate the actual quality and dependence of the Christian testimony, and show why ordinary explanations outperform resurrection without dismissing unusual evidence merely because it lacks routine modern analogues. Locked score: The strong competent band reflects excellent responsiveness, substantial methodological relevance, and acknowledged uncertainty, moderated by compressed comparisons, partially unstable terminology, and a somewhat overstated conclusion from experiential scarcity.

Source Provenance, Apostolic Transmission, and Multiple Attestation

7:05–74:21

William Albrecht — section score 80/100

Paul presents received tradition as an already widespread proclamation that Christ was buried, raised, and subsequently seen by numerous named witnesses.

Strongest feature: The Pauline received-tradition formulation directly establishes an exceptionally early, named, and historically consequential proclamation, thereby contacting the central testimony burden with unusually focused chronological relevance. Principal limitation: Its universal-transmission characterization and implied eyewitness dependability exceed what this excerpt independently demonstrates, because circulation, dating, source dependence, and testimonial reliability remain substantially compressed rather than separately established. Live burden: The argument still must connect early proclamation to trustworthy occurrence by defending provenance, independence, witness access, and the inference from transmitted appearance reports to bodily resurrection against plausible experiential alternatives. Locked score: The strong competent band appropriately reflects excellent burden contact, recoverable terminology, and coherent textual grounding alongside meaningful overstatement concerning universality, historical security, and direct evidential access.

Robert Price — section score 81/100

Disputed authorship and anonymous origins among New Testament and patristic writings prevent traditional attributions from automatically establishing a critically secure witness chain.

Strongest feature: The reply identifies disputed attribution and anonymous Gospel origins as direct obstacles to treating traditional names and institutional transmission as an already established historical chain. Principal limitation: It groups diverse New Testament, apostolic-father, and patristic documents too broadly, occasionally suggesting near-universal attribution failure without differentiating their individual dates, textual histories, or evidentiary usefulness. Live burden: The affirmative must defend particular links rather than inherited labels, while the skeptic must assess each source's content, dating, dependence, and witness connection instead of assuming anonymity erases all historical value. Locked score: The strong competent band recognizes a powerful diagnostic challenge, high central relevance, and direct contact with provenance, moderated by generalized scope, slightly excessive force, and insufficient document-by-document discrimination.

Pagan Resurrection Parallels and Comparative Context

38:14–46:43

William Albrecht — section score 86/100

The Attis comparison rests on ambiguous pottery, whereas explicit written accounts of resurrection appear later and supply no secure pre-Christian documentation.

Strongest feature: The reply precisely separates ambiguous iconography from explicit written testimony, confines its challenge to Attis, and frames the documentary conclusion as a qualified request for confirmation. Principal limitation: The absence of an early written account does not eliminate every evidential contribution from iconography, ritual continuity, or later texts that might clarify an earlier motif, although those sources remain weaker. Live burden: The comparison's defender must provide securely dated pre-Christian evidence and justify retrospective interpretation, while the affirmative must avoid converting a documentary gap into categorical proof that no earlier Attis concept existed. Locked score: The very strong band reflects unusually disciplined scope, direct responsiveness, stable terminology, careful calibration, and a decisive challenge to the stated Attis evidence without overstating what documentary silence alone establishes.

Robert Price — section score 73/100

Attis evidence is described as suggestive, while older Osiris and Baal traditions are presented as pre-Christian examples of divine resurrection narratives.

Strongest feature: The move broadens the comparative environment through Attis, Osiris, and Baal, cautiously qualifying the ambiguous Attis material while identifying potentially relevant pre-Christian dying-and-rising traditions. Principal limitation: It applies resurrection across importantly different divine narratives without defining equivalence, and its broader chronological assertions receive less qualification and documentation than the tentative Attis interpretation. Live burden: The skeptical case must establish dating, narrative similarity, cultural availability, and explanatory relevance, then show whether comparison supports dependence, genre expectation, or merely a general religious background. Locked score: The mixed band reflects legitimate contextual relevance and commendable caution about one evidentiary fragment, but also a relevant nonanswer to earlier textual arguments, unstable comparative terminology, and underdeveloped warrants for treating diverse traditions as materially parallel.

Martyrdom, Conversion, and Early Christian Growth

William Albrecht — section score 65/100

Strongest feature: The reply clearly preserves the legitimate inference that persecution can evidence sincere belief in a physically living, bodily risen Christ rather than conscious fabrication. Principal limitation: Its shifting reference to believers, apostles, martyrs, and Gospel witnesses obscures who personally saw what, while sincerity supplies no independent bridge to factual truth or secure eyewitness provenance. Live burden: The affirmative must identify witnesses, establish their access and costly commitments, and comparatively explain why sincere mistake, inherited testimony, or legend is less plausible. Locked score: The mixed band reflects genuine subsidiary relevance and partial contact with the sincerity issue, but also materially overstated calibration, deferred source verification, unstable evidentiary scope, and failure to answer the decisive distinction between costly conviction and historical correctness.

Robert Price — section score 79/100

Variable apostle lists and late legend-laden acts weaken attempts to ground resurrection credibility in securely identified apostolic witnesses and martyrdoms.

Strongest feature: The reply identifies variable apostle lists and late, legend-laden apocryphal acts as concrete reasons to question whether alleged apostolic martyrdoms securely support resurrection testimony. Principal limitation: Claims that nothing is known and that relevant traditions derive exclusively from later acts are substantially overbroad, because legendary elaboration does not establish universal lateness or falsity. Live burden: The affirmative must isolate earlier and better-attested identities, activities, and deaths, while the skeptic must evaluate those cases individually rather than extending deficiencies in several apocryphal works to every tradition. Locked score: The strong competent band reflects direct source-reliability contact and a materially important challenge to costly-witness reasoning, moderated by categorical phrasing, incomplete evidentiary differentiation, and only indirect engagement with the earlier transmission-focused move.
