Overall assessment

Matthew Adelstein — Stance-independent moral facts exist: 79/100

Published strengths

  • Adelstein advances several distinct routes to realism, linking intuitions, reflective findings, experience, and historical patterns rather than relying exclusively on immediate conviction.
  • He accepts specialist disagreement as counterevidence and distinguishes a reason's authority from its power to persuade, making his commitments substantially clearer.
  • He answers the detector hypothetical consistently and reciprocally rejects harmful-character accusations against anti-realists, preserving the difference between philosophical commitments and actual approval.

Published weaknesses

  • The predictive case repeatedly asserts an advantage over psychological explanations without establishing their comparative likelihoods or validating the relevant measures of utilitarian commitment.
  • The experiential route moves from suffering and introspective confidence toward independent badness without resolving the alternative interpretation of evaluation as an additional attitude.
  • Favorable expert opinion and selected historical anticipations receive weight without a fully consistent account of contrary specialist opinion, selection effects, or independent progress.

Lance Bush — Stance-independent moral facts do not exist: 86/100

Published strengths

  • Bush separates survey questions from inferred intuitions and applies sampling cautions to congenial studies, substantially improving the evidential discipline of the consensus exchange.
  • He directly tests the links between reflective responses, utilitarian commitment, and independent truth, offering psychological alternatives while distinguishing anti-realism from a causal theory.
  • He corrects the evolutionary-debunking attribution and explicitly rejects harmful-character inferences from the detector hypothetical, protecting accurate interpretation at important points in the discussion.

Published weaknesses

  • Categorical descriptions of normative concepts as nonsense exceed the demonstrated distinction between his own incomprehension and a general failure of intelligible reference.
  • The psychological and historical alternatives sometimes move from plausible mechanisms to claims of explanatory superiority or completeness without the detailed empirical comparison required.
  • Questions about why an agent should care occasionally blur motivational indifference with absence of authority, leaving room for Adelstein's distinction between persuasion and reasons.
