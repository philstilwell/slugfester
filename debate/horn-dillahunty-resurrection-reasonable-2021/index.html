Overall assessment

Trent Horn — Resurrection belief is reasonable: 82/100

Published strengths

  • Horn supplied a clear three-part standard linking factual consistency, expected evidence, and comparative explanation directly to the motion's reasonableness threshold.
  • He correctly defended testimony as an indispensable historical evidence category and resisted treating natural regularity as proof that exceptions are impossible.
  • His Elvis response identified concrete disanalogies, while his later burden distinction between reasonableness and personal conviction directly addressed the debate's governing threshold.
  • He accepted that costly commitment establishes sincerity rather than truth directly, then reformulated sincerity as an explanatory datum requiring further analysis.

Published weaknesses

  • The comparative resurrection case treated contested group appearances, source independence, rapid verification, and empty-tomb claims as jointly secure without sufficiently defending each premise.
  • The metaphysical argument moved too quickly from pure actuality to a personal, knowledgeable, good agent capable of intentionally producing this historical resurrection.
  • The costly-testimony reply exaggerated the negative position as threatening ancient history generally and left sincere error, witness independence, and firsthand access insufficiently answered.

Matt Dillahunty — Resurrection belief is unreasonable: 83/100

Published strengths

  • Dillahunty clearly distinguished defeating reasonable acceptance from proving nonoccurrence, keeping the negative burden accurately aligned with the motion.
  • He repeatedly separated sincerity, experience, interpretation, and truth, especially in the martyrdom and final consensus replies, preserving ordinary error without alleging dishonesty.
  • His source critique identified concrete limitations involving authorship, witness accessibility, reconciliation, corroboration, and the possibility of distorted transmission.
  • He required supernatural agency to receive independent warrant before functioning as an explanation and connected background regularity to comparative probability rather than strict impossibility.

Published weaknesses

  • The courtroom analogy and emphasis on physical corroboration imposed requirements that were not sufficiently distinguished from ordinary standards of historical justification.
  • The publicity argument shifted from repeatable modern resurrections to a unique ancient event, weakening its direct relevance to the surviving historical evidence.
  • The relational-disclosure argument asserted a strong expectation of universally sufficient evidence without developing a detailed account of divine aims, constraints, or predicted disclosure.

Reasonableness Standards and Debate Burdens

2:34–102:36

Trent Horn — section score 88/100

An unusual-event belief is reasonable when it contradicts no established subject-specific fact, lacks no evidence that should exist if true, and has no equally adequate ordinary explanation.

Strongest feature: The enumerated framework is unusually clear, motion-directed, and operational, connecting factual consistency, evidential expectations, and comparative explanation within one intelligible standard for evaluating extraordinary historical beliefs. Principal limitation: The presentation illustrates each requirement more successfully than it establishes their collective sufficiency, leaving unclear why satisfying these three conditions exhausts every relevant dimension of epistemic reasonableness. Live burden: The framework must specify how expected evidence and explanatory equality are measured when surviving historical materials are dependent, incomplete, contested, and interpreted against radically different background probabilities. Locked score: The supplied very-strong band is justified by exceptional relevance, recoverability, and structural coherence, while appropriately retaining space for the underdeveloped sufficiency bridge and evidential measurement problem.

Matt Dillahunty — section score 88/100

A claim is reasonable when it remains consistent with what disciplined inquiry establishes as true and possible within reality, regardless of whether the claim ultimately proves true.

Strongest feature: The response offers a concise competing standard grounded in shared inquiry, background knowledge, and bias-reducing methods, thereby contacting the affirmative's foundational account of reasonable unusual-event belief. Principal limitation: What qualifies as known possibility or consistency with reality remains underspecified, potentially making unfamiliar event types inadmissible before sufficiently strong evidence can revise the relevant background understanding. Live burden: The negative must explain how its criterion permits evidence-driven expansion of recognized possibilities while still assigning appropriately demanding evidential requirements to claims conflicting with extremely stable experience. Locked score: The supplied strong-and-competent band fits the move's high relevance, clear alternative criterion, and calibrated acknowledgment of residual subjectivity, while preserving reservations about definitional incompleteness and only partial comparison with the affirmative's three conditions.

Historical Testimony and Source Reliability

Strongest feature: The argument correctly treats testimony as evidentially admissible and connects early proclamation, community formation, and reported experience into a historically recognizable foundation rather than dismissing ancient evidence categorically. Principal limitation: Its broad reliability language and appeal to universal scholarly agreement compress important distinctions among source proximity, literary dependence, sincere experience, accurate interpretation, and the specific content allegedly experienced. Live burden: The affirmative must demonstrate that the accessible materials reliably support sufficiently independent postmortem experiences and not merely an early, sincere, rapidly transmitted conviction whose underlying cause remains uncertain. Resurrection and Ordinary Alternative Explanations

8:16–113:01

Trent Horn — section score 83/100

Legend, deception, and hallucination allegedly explain reported group appearances, conversions, empty-tomb evidence, and sincere proclamation less adequately than the hypothesis that Jesus was resurrected.

Strongest feature: The move accepts the proper comparative task and identifies several concrete explanatory targets, including group appearances, unexpected conversions, early proclamation, sincerity, and the reported empty tomb. Principal limitation: It treats multiple contested report features as jointly established while insufficiently separating source dependence, later development, misidentification, interpretive error, and combinations of ordinary mechanisms that need not preserve every detail. Live burden: The affirmative must defend the reliability and independence of each explanandum before showing that a resurrection hypothesis explains the resulting evidence better than appropriately mixed naturalistic alternatives. Locked score: The supplied strong-and-competent band recognizes direct contact with the central comparative bridge and a coherent explanatory structure, while registering materially overstated premises, limited rival analysis, and comparatively weak evidential documentation.

Matt Dillahunty — section score 89/100

Mistake, deception, interpretive error, or inaccurate transmission remains more probable than resurrection when supernatural resurrection has not independently been shown possible or probable.

Strongest feature: The argument squarely addresses comparative probability, identifies several ordinary mechanisms that require no deliberate dishonesty, and emphasizes that alternative explanations can combine rather than operate in isolation. Principal limitation: It moves too quickly from lacking independent demonstration of resurrection to preferring ordinary error, without quantitatively or qualitatively comparing how well each alternative explains the affirmative's disputed evidence. Live burden: The negative must show that plausible mixtures of mistake, interpretation, and transmission fit the independently warranted historical facts better than resurrection, rather than relying principally on unfamiliarity. Locked score: The supplied very-strong band reflects precise conditional framing, strong central relevance, and charitable recognition of sincere error, while appropriately reserving credit because the prior-probability premise and feature-by-feature comparison remain incomplete.
