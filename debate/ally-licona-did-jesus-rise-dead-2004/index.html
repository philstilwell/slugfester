Overall assessment

Michael Licona — Jesus bodily rose from the dead: 83/100

Published strengths

  • Built a clear cumulative route through death, empty tomb, sincere appearances, early tradition, and explanatory comparison.
  • Presented convergent historical, procedural, and medical considerations supporting death by crucifixion.
  • Directly challenged rescue through provenance, evidential fit, and ad hocness rather than relying on incredulity.
  • Used comparative attestation effectively against selectively retaining a favorable Pilate premise.
  • Traced resurrection proclamation to early Pauline and pre-Pauline material, keeping chronology central.

Published weaknesses

  • The closing declared alternative theories unsuccessful without explicit criteria and concentrated too heavily on rescue, leaving stronger underdetermination, visionary, and developmental objections insufficiently compared.
  • Several constructive claims compressed source independence, universal medical agreement, bodily semantics, and provenance instead of demonstrating those warrants in detail.

Shabir Ally — Bodily resurrection is not established: 85/100

Published strengths

  • Kept the claimant's burden tied to the specifically physical conclusion rather than accepting sincere belief as physical proof.
  • Distinguished spiritual vindication and recreated-body possibilities from an inference requiring the original tomb to be empty.
  • Used Paul's inclusion in the appearance list to undermine any necessary assumption of uniform physical phenomenology.
  • Presented concrete source-layering challenges involving Mark, John, Pilate, crucifixion duration, and later physical detail.
  • Integrated death, empty-tomb, appearance, and development uncertainties into a coherent motion-level insufficiency case.

Published weaknesses

  • The crucifixion-duration challenge underdeveloped trauma, Roman execution practice, medical evidence, and early death traditions that supported the affirmative's convergence.
  • The developmental case did not adequately distinguish theological or literary elaboration from invention of essential physical content.
  • The recreated-body interpretation remained more conceptually possible than historically demonstrated within the earliest Jesus tradition.

Death by Crucifixion

9:05–95:52

Michael Licona — section score 87/100

Licona combined non-Christian attestation, Roman crucifixion's lethality and preceding torture, and medical opinion as convergent support that Jesus died on the cross.

Strongest feature: The argument coordinates mutually reinforcing historical, procedural, physiological, and medical considerations into an intelligible evidential convergence supporting the indispensable death premise with considerable directness and argumentative economy. Principal limitation: Its characterization of medical agreement as universal and its compressed appeal to multiple attestation leave evidential independence, source quality, exceptional survivability, and relevant uncertainty substantially underexplained. Live burden: The affirmative still must demonstrate that these considerations establish irreversible death in this particular case while answering the short-duration, Pilate, source-layering, and narrative-construction objections without overgeneralization. Locked score: The supplied very strong band appropriately reflects excellent relevance, coherent cumulative organization, and substantial evidential support, moderated by compressed qualification, incompletely defended universality, and limited engagement with contrary particulars.

Shabir Ally — section score 77/100

Ally cited Mark's unusually brief crucifixion and Pilate's doubt, contrasting them with John's later spear detail, to question whether death was firmly established.

Strongest feature: The reply supplies concrete textual particulars—brief crucifixion duration, Pilate's reported doubt, and the later Johannine spear detail—that directly pressure the affirmative's indispensable death premise rather than offering abstract skepticism. Principal limitation: Its generalization about ordinary crucifixion duration lacks distributional support and insufficiently incorporates severe preceding trauma, Roman execution controls, or the affirmative's non-Gospel and medical considerations. Live burden: The negative must show that these particulars generate material case-specific uncertainty after the full evidential convergence is considered, and must address early death traditions rather than relying principally on Gospel comparison. Locked score: The supplied strong/competent band appropriately reflects coherent and relevant source criticism with tangible contrary details, moderated by incomplete responsiveness, insufficient baseline evidence, and limited charity toward trauma-based rapid death.

Burial and the Empty Tomb

12:21–113:10

Michael Licona — section score 80/100

Licona argued that Jerusalem proclamation, opponents alleging body theft, and ancient resurrection's bodily meaning jointly support the inference that Jesus's tomb was empty.

Strongest feature: The argument presents three distinguishable indicators—Jerusalem proclamation, hostile acknowledgment through a stolen-body explanation, and bodily resurrection semantics—that jointly address whether Jesus's body remained available in its burial place. Principal limitation: It leaves insufficiently established whether authorities knew and could access the relevant tomb, whether the hostile explanation is independently early, and whether bodily language necessarily entails this particular tomb's emptiness. Live burden: The affirmative must connect each indicator to reliable historical circumstances and show that their dependence does not exaggerate convergence, especially against spiritual-vindication and recreated-body interpretations of the earliest proclamation. Locked score: The supplied strong/competent band appropriately reflects clear central relevance and plausible cumulative support, tempered by partly unstable scope, implicit qualifications, and unresolved access, provenance, and semantic assumptions.

Shabir Ally — section score 89/100

Ally distinguished spiritual vindication from bodily revival, arguing that belief Jesus was alive with God did not itself require discovery of an empty tomb.

Strongest feature: The reply precisely identifies a non-entailment: belief that Jesus was alive with God could communicate vindication without logically requiring revival of the buried body or discovery of an empty tomb. Principal limitation: Conceptual possibility alone does not establish that this was the historically operative meaning of the earliest Jesus tradition, particularly if contemporary resurrection terminology ordinarily carried bodily continuity implications. Live burden: The negative must connect its spiritual-vindication reading to the relevant linguistic, cultural, and testimonial evidence, while the affirmative must show that ordinary bodily semantics warrant this specific inference rather than merely support it probabilistically. Locked score: The supplied very strong band appropriately reflects excellent diagnostic clarity, careful possibility-level calibration, and direct engagement with the empty-tomb bridge, moderated by underdeveloped historical anchoring for the alternative interpretation.

Appearances and Bodily Physicality

16:55–100:04

Michael Licona — section score 84/100

Licona maintained that disciples, Paul, and James sincerely believed the resurrected Jesus appeared to them, furnishing a central fact for his cumulative explanation.

Strongest feature: The move carefully establishes a historically relevant claim about sincere appearance belief among disciples and former opponents, rather than immediately treating sincerity as conclusive verification of bodily resurrection. Principal limitation: The selected support compresses the provenance, independence, phenomenology, and evidential quality of the reports, while leaving the crucial distinction between sincere experience and physically veridical encounter largely implicit. Live burden: The affirmative must show why these heterogeneous reports favor encounters with Jesus's resurrected body over visionary, spiritual, revelatory, or otherwise underdetermined experiences, particularly for Paul and James. Locked score: The supplied strong/competent band appropriately reflects coherent formulation, direct contact with a central bridge, and meaningful cumulative relevance, moderated by incomplete source demonstration and unresolved physicality.

Shabir Ally — section score 88/100

Ally argued that Paul's inclusion of his spiritually described experience alongside earlier appearances weakens any necessary inference that all listed encounters were bodily.

Strongest feature: The reply targets a specific inferential vulnerability: Paul's self-inclusion within the appearance list makes uniform physical phenomenology less secure when his own experience is described in revelatory or luminous terms. Principal limitation: Shared list membership need not imply phenomenological identity, and reliance on Acts introduces an additional source whose terminology, narrative purpose, and relationship to Paul's own testimony require careful differentiation. Live burden: The negative must show that Paul's inclusion materially lowers the probability of physical encounters for earlier recipients, while avoiding the stronger unsupported conclusion that every appearance shared Paul's reported experiential mode. Locked score: The supplied very strong band appropriately reflects outstanding responsiveness, precise non-necessity framing, strong representational charity, and substantial evidential warrant, moderated by underdefined terminology and unresolved source-comparison questions.

Early Tradition and Narrative Development

47:59–153:48

Michael Licona — section score 80/100

Licona invoked pre-Pauline creedal material and repeated resurrection language to argue that the earliest tradition concerned transformation of the same body that was buried.

Strongest feature: The reply directly joins chronology and semantics, contending that material predating Paul already described resurrection as transformation of the buried body and therefore cannot be dismissed as late physicalization. Principal limitation: The numerous references are counted rather than individually demonstrated, leaving their precise wording, provenance, dating, independence, and specifically bodily implications insufficiently transparent within the selected argument. Live burden: The affirmative must identify representative early formulations and establish that they convey numerical bodily continuity rather than divine vindication alone, while distinguishing early belief from later narrative elaboration. Locked score: The supplied strong/competent band appropriately reflects substantial responsiveness and a coherent answer to spiritualization, moderated by compressed documentation, partly unstable scope, implicit qualification, and only subsidiary contact with the ultimate motion.

Shabir Ally — section score 84/100

Ally argued that increasingly severe and explicit Gospel portrayals show narrative evolution, requiring proof that later physical details preserve an earlier historical core.

Strongest feature: The reply compares Gospel portrayals and converts their intensification into a focused historical challenge: later physical specificity cannot automatically function as uncomplicated eyewitness confirmation of death or bodily resurrection. Principal limitation: The term evolution remains ambiguous among literary difference, theological emphasis, narrative supplementation, and historical invention, while increasing explicitness is compatible with preservation of a stable earlier physical core. Live burden: The negative must distinguish development in presentation from development in essential event content and demonstrate why the observed trajectory materially reduces the reliability of the relevant physical claims. Locked score: The supplied strong/competent band appropriately reflects excellent responsiveness and meaningful cross-source evidence, moderated by semantic instability, an incompletely warranted inference from intensification to non-eyewitness origin, and insufficient engagement with clarification.
