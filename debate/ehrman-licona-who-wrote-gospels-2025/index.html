Overall assessment

Michael Licona — Traditional authorship: 81/100

Published strengths

  • Built a cumulative case combining external testimony with text-specific internal considerations rather than relying upon one evidential category.
  • Directly confronted anonymity, literacy, Synoptic dependence, and selective Petrine reporting instead of leaving major objections unanswered.
  • Used appropriately qualified formulations in several central moves, especially the natural-fit synthesis and hyperbolic reading of Papias.
  • Offered concrete comparative pressure against a naming scheme by asking why Mark and Luke would be selected over more prestigious eyewitnesses.

Published weaknesses

  • The collaboration proposals often established general possibility without supplying evidence that the named figures actually participated in producing these particular texts.
  • The external-attestation case did not sufficiently separate geographic distribution from informational independence or shared transmission.
  • Several internal indicators supported compatibility or candidate narrowing but did not fully bridge eyewitness source, narrator, author, and authorizing authority.

Bart Ehrman — Anonymous later authorship: 87/100

Published strengths

  • Kept the motion focused on identity-specific authorship evidence rather than allowing reliability or possible transmission to substitute for identification.
  • Developed a strong chronological challenge from narrative anonymity and the later appearance of securely identifiable traditional labels.
  • Answered the Mark-and-Luke obscurity objection with a coherent collective-apostolic-authority rationale involving Peter and Paul.
  • Precisely distinguished evidence for short dictated letters from evidence for independent composition of lengthy literary narratives.
  • Used Barnabas and Second Clement to show that stable unanimous naming is not sufficient by itself to establish authentic authorship.

Published weaknesses

  • The proposed institutional naming event was narrated more confidently than the direct historical evidence for its occurrence and coordination warranted.
  • The literacy argument generalized across figures with different identities and possible opportunities, while personal incapacity did not alone exclude authorized mediation.
  • Some underdetermination replies risked evaluating internal details separately when the affirmative expressly presented them as a cumulative case.

Timing, independence, and reach of external attribution

8:49–99:01

Michael Licona — section score 85/100

Six comparatively early witnesses from several Christian regions consistently associate the canonical Gospels with their traditional authors, supporting preserved authorship information.

Strongest feature: The presentation identifies six witnesses, supplies a meaningful chronological range, names five geographically separated regions, and connects their agreement directly to the central external-attestation burden with unusually clear argumentative organization. Principal limitation: Geographic dispersion does not automatically establish informational independence, and the emphatic conclusion leaves dating controversies, possible literary dependence, and shared ecclesiastical transmission insufficiently differentiated or expressly qualified. Live burden: The case must demonstrate that these witnesses preserve multiple historically informative channels rather than geographically repeated reception of one attribution convention, while comparing that hypothesis against later coordinated naming. Locked score: The strong and competent band reflects substantial relevance, recoverable evidence, and impressive constructive responsiveness, moderated by understated dependence questions and somewhat stronger confidence than the excerpted warrant independently supports.

Bart Ehrman — section score 88/100

The Gospels do not identify their authors within the narratives and initially circulated without attached names, whereas securely identifiable traditional attributions appear substantially later.

Strongest feature: The argument clearly distinguishes internal authorial anonymity from securely identifiable external naming, grounds the distinction in the absence of Mark, Luke, and John from their narratives, and makes chronology central to the attribution question. Principal limitation: Narrative anonymity and the absence of surviving early labels cannot independently demonstrate that every early community lacked oral knowledge, lost paratext, or other attribution information accompanying circulation. Live burden: The negative case must show why the surviving pattern is more expected under initially unknown authorship than under known but formally unnamed biography, while carefully separating evidential absence from universal historical absence. Locked score: The very strong band reflects exceptional central relevance, disciplined calibration, and a clear chronological challenge, moderated by the unavoidable incompleteness of the surviving record and plausible unpreserved attribution channels.

Literacy, language, and mediated composition

21:26–121:40

Michael Licona — section score 76/100

Matthew may have shaped or authorized the surviving Greek Gospel through sources and assistance after an earlier Semitic composition disappeared, analogously to Josephus.

Strongest feature: The proposal directly confronts linguistic and source-dependence objections by integrating Markan use, substantial additional material, a lost Semitic precursor, collaborative production, and a concrete Josephus comparison within one historically intelligible model. Principal limitation: Shaping, supervising, authorizing, and receiving assistance describe materially different relationships, while the Josephus analogy establishes that mediated bilingual production was possible without demonstrating that Matthew underwent a comparable process. Live burden: The model needs text-specific or historical evidence identifying Matthew's actual participation and a stable account of which compositional responsibilities belonged to him, his sources, and any Greek collaborator. Locked score: The strong and competent band recognizes focused problem-solving, cautious probability language, and considerable relevance, while reflecting that an explanatory possibility remains less evidentially developed than an attribution-specific historical conclusion requires.

Bart Ehrman — section score 86/100

Aramaic-speaking rural disciples lacked the advanced education and Greek literary training apparently required for the canonical Gospels' extended narrative composition.

Strongest feature: The argument identifies a concrete historical mismatch between the reported social and linguistic background of the Galilean disciples and the sustained Greek literary competence required for sophisticated narrative composition. Principal limitation: Its terminology concerning elite education and sophistication needs finer specification, and extending one profile across Matthew, John, Mark, and Luke overlooks differences in identity, later opportunity, mobility, and proposed authorial relationship. Live burden: The case must distinguish personal composition from authorization and demonstrate why education acquired later, source dependence, translation, or substantive collaboration remains historically improbable rather than merely unproven. Locked score: The strong and competent band reflects considerable evidential force, immediate relevance, and a serious capacity challenge, tempered by generalized social assumptions and the argument's incomplete exclusion of mediated production models permitted by the motion.

Internal textual indicators of authorial identity

17:52–93:16

Michael Licona — section score 82/100

First-person travel narration indicates participation by a Pauline companion, and traditional external attribution makes Luke the proposed narrator's most reasonable identification.

Strongest feature: Licona distinguishes companion participation from personal identification, grounding the former in first-person travel narration and presenting Luke as a probabilistic inference rather than certainty. Principal limitation: The identification remains dependent upon disputed external attribution, while the excerpt neither discriminates Luke from Pauline coworkers nor eliminates literary convention, source incorporation, or another companion. Live burden: The argument must demonstrate why autobiographical narration is historically preferable to compositional alternatives and why converging evidence specifically selects Luke instead of merely establishing an unidentified Pauline associate. Locked score: At 82, this strong, centrally relevant, unusually precise constructive earns considerable credit for its restrained conclusion and internal evidence, although underdetermination at the crucial identification step prevents a higher evidential-warrant assessment.

Bart Ehrman — section score 90/100

Matthew's detached third-person call story does not identify its author, while Acts' unnamed first-person narrator provides no unique reason to identify that person as Luke.

Strongest feature: Ehrman precisely contrasts Matthew’s conspicuously detached third-person self-description with Acts’ anonymous first-person narration, directly challenging both proposed internal identifications through readily intelligible and textually anchored observations. Principal limitation: The argument establishes underdetermination more securely than competing authorship, because third-person narration can coexist with mediated self-reference and first-person participation may acquire cumulative significance alongside independent external testimony. Live burden: Licona must explain why these formally nonidentifying narrative features, separately or cumulatively, uniquely support Matthew and Luke rather than merely remaining compatible with inherited attribution traditions. Locked score: At 90, this is a very strong, exceptionally responsive central reply whose disciplined negative scope, stable terminology, and proportionate probabilistic force outweigh its limited treatment of cumulative evidence.

Anonymous circulation and the formation of Gospel names

42:35–89:06

Michael Licona — section score 80/100

A fabricated authority scheme would seemingly prefer prestigious eyewitness names over the comparatively less marketable non-eyewitness figures Mark and Luke.

Strongest feature: The reply tests the rival naming hypothesis with a concrete counterfactual, contrasting Mark and Luke against Peter, James, Thomas, and Mary while invoking the prestigious naming pattern of later apocryphal works. Principal limitation: The comparison assumes that inventors would maximize direct eyewitness prestige, although associates of Peter and Paul could strategically represent broader apostolic authority and thereby make Mark and Luke advantageous selections. Live burden: The argument must compare these competing attribution strategies and show that the traditional pairings are less expected under invention than under preserved historical memory, not merely less famous individually. Locked score: The strong and competent band reflects a pointed, relevant, and comparatively framed rebuttal with useful historical texture, moderated because the counterfactual preference remains contestable and the alternative collective-authority rationale is coherent.

Bart Ehrman — section score 81/100

Churches facing rival apostolic writings could have legitimized anonymous canonical Gospels by assigning two disciple names and two names associated with leading apostles.

Strongest feature: Ehrman presents a historically coherent mechanism linking rival apostolic writings, the churches’ consequent need for comparable authority, and the natural selection of two disciples plus two associates of leading apostles. Principal limitation: the institutional motive and naming decision remain hypothetical, because plausibility supplies no direct evidence that assignment occurred and insufficient chronology to exclude inherited authorship traditions. Live burden: the argument establishes a credible possibility supporting later attribution, but must compare transmission evidence and explain why these exact four traditions arose and stabilized, beyond showing why authoritative names were institutionally useful. Locked score: 77 appropriately recognizes coherence, precision, and subsidiary relevance while discounting materially overstated warrant, unacknowledged uncertainty, and incomplete engagement with the strongest alternative explanation.

Source dependence, oral transmission, and apostolic proximity

128:16–139:40

Michael Licona — section score 78/100

Papias's statement that Mark recorded everything remembered from Peter is reasonably hyperbolic because historians and biographers characteristically select among available source material.

Strongest feature: The reply directly addresses the apparent inconsistency between Papias's expansive wording and Mark's selectivity, offering an ordinary biographical convention as a concise and intelligible resolution rather than denying the omission. Principal limitation: General authorial selectivity supports a nonliteral reading but does not determine exactly how much historical content Papias intended to claim or independently validate the Petrine-source tradition itself. Live burden: The response must show that the hyperbolic interpretation best fits Papias's language and context while keeping separate the limited defense against literal exhaustiveness from the larger authorship inference. Locked score: The very strong band reflects excellent target contact, stable terminology, appropriate plausibility language, and a charitable resolution of the objection, with evidential caution because genre selectivity explains compatibility without establishing the underlying attribution's historical accuracy.

Bart Ehrman — section score 94/100

Reliable traditions, oral transmission, and plausible collaboration do not identify Matthew or John without evidence specifically connecting those figures to the canonical texts.

Strongest feature: Ehrman isolates the decisive inference clearly: credible traditions, oral transmission, source proximity, and collaborative composition do not identify Matthew or John as authoring or authorizing canonical texts. Principal limitation: The challenge establishes an evidentiary gap more readily than anonymity, and its treatment of later attribution does not evaluate whether external testimonies, internal correspondences, and transmission patterns could cumulatively contribute identity-specific likelihood. Live burden: Licona must demonstrate that each consideration discriminates Matthew or John from alternatives and that their combination supplies a grounded connection, rather than converting general reliability or compositional possibility into personal attribution. Locked score: The very-strong 94 recognizes near-complete burden contact, extraordinary responsiveness, disciplined calibration, representational charity, and coherent diagnosis, while retaining evidentiary caution.
