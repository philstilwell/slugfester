Overall assessment

Zac Sechler — Christian plausibility defense: 72/100

Published strengths

  • Sechler strengthened his case by rejecting hard exclusivism, allowing sincere truth-seeking to save, and preferring annihilation to eternal conscious torment as a more plausible Christian position.
  • Sechler's evidential-chip framing sensibly treated the debate as a cumulative adjustment of confidence, setting a modest standard of Christian plausibility rather than deductive certainty.
  • He candidly acknowledged that Clifton’s parental-love objection puts real pressure on eternal torment and gives him reason to prefer annihilation over defensively preserving inherited doctrine.

Published weaknesses

  • His chosen-hell account did not answer Clifton’s symmetry test about Jesus refusing substitution, leaving the proposed Christlike standard unsupported and weakening its comparative force.
  • Appealing to widespread theism did not answer Clifton’s challenge that even one sincere nonbeliever remains deprived of the relationship God supposedly offers.
  • Sechler identified inquiry, fellowship, confession, and cooperation as goods of uncertainty, but never showed that less relationally costly alternatives could not preserve them.

Scott Clifton — Hiddenness and hell challenge: 83/100

Published strengths

  • Clifton made divine hiddenness concrete by comparing God's absence to an unknown mother and arguing that genuine freedom requires awareness of the relational option.
  • His replies consistently tested proposed defenses: annihilation still withholds an infinite good, involuntary nonbelief cannot be culpable, and perfect love should not permit relational ignorance.
  • Clifton distinguished ordinary settled belief from infallible certainty, directly clarifying what divine disclosure would require for a person to recognize and freely choose a relationship with God.

Published weaknesses

  • Clifton’s eternal-reward objection too quickly equated heavenly compensation with self-interest without addressing sacrifices motivated by love for another person, weakening its comparative force.
  • The maternal analogy did not establish how closely divine and human relationships must resemble one another across differences in power, embodiment, and cognitive access.
  • The alternative-goods reply supplied limited positive detail about reliable substitutes for religious inquiry that would preserve every allegedly hiddenness-dependent benefit in a nonhidden world.
