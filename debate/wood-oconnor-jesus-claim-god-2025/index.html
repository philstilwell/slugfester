Overall assessment

David Wood — Affirmative: 83/100

Published strengths

  • Built a cumulative case from scriptural frameworks, Jesus's titles, divine functions, early devotion, and christological chronology rather than relying on one isolated expression.
  • Connected Danielic cloud imagery, heavenly enthronement, universal judgment, resurrection, and equal honor directly to the motion's personal-claim question.
  • Answered dependence language with a coherent incarnation-and-exaltation model that accepted the objection's textual force.
  • Used Philippians 2's sequence and very early invocation of Jesus to place sustained pressure on merely late-development explanations.

Published weaknesses

  • The case repeatedly moved from divine association or unusually exalted agency to shared divine identity without fully establishing the differentiating bridge.
  • Follower devotion and early Pauline christology were sometimes asked to carry more weight concerning Jesus's personal historical self-understanding than they directly support.
  • Several linguistic and historical claims, especially the doubled Kyrios formulation and settled two-powers framework, were asserted with insufficient comparative demonstration.

Alex O'Connor — Critical reader: 90/100

Published strengths

  • Consistently separated Jesus's historical self-claim from Gospel framing, Pauline christology, follower devotion, and hostile interpretation.
  • Developed a coherent delegated-agency alternative spanning divine name-bearing, received authority, transferable prerogatives, and flexible homage vocabulary.
  • Closely read John 5's grant language and Psalm 82's comparison class, directly contacting central affirmative inferences.
  • Qualified speculative alternatives candidly and granted early high christology without conceding that it established the motion.

Published weaknesses

  • The promised-fulfillment interpretation of John 8:58 remained an interesting possibility but did not adequately explain the conspicuous present-tense formulation.
  • Appeals to misunderstanding and lexical flexibility sometimes established underdetermination more clearly than a positive non-divine interpretation of the complete context.
  • The challenge to two-powers attestation weakened claims about a settled controversy but could not remove earlier scriptural conceptual antecedents.

Two powers, divine name-bearing, and Jewish agency

6:57–110:09

David Wood — section score 81/100

Old Testament passages presenting two distinguishable heavenly figures acting and speaking as Yahweh provide a scriptural framework in which Jesus can identify himself as one divine power.

Strongest feature: The argument supplies a historically pertinent scriptural framework, preserves Jewish monotheism, and identifies recurring distinguishable heavenly figures whose speech and activity make the affirmative interpretation genuinely intelligible. Principal limitation: It compresses the consequential transition from two divine figures to two persons participating in one divine identity, without sufficiently differentiating ontological participation from exceptionally authorized representation. Live burden: The affirmative must compare its identity-sharing account directly with the strongest agency interpretation and explain which textual characteristics make the latter inadequate across the cited passages. Locked score: The strong and competent band appropriately recognizes substantial framework-building relevance and concrete scriptural support while retaining material reservations about terminological stability, comparative warrant, and representational charity.

Alex O'Connor — section score 91/100

Jewish representatives could bear Yahweh's name and exercise his authority without being Yahweh, so Exodus-evoking language does not by itself establish identity.

Strongest feature: The reply concedes the possible Exodus resonance while identifying a historically relevant representative-agency category, thereby defeating any straightforward necessity inference from divine name-bearing to personal identity with Yahweh. Principal limitation: The selected presentation asserts the Jewish tradition without documenting representative examples or demonstrating that their authority and naming are sufficiently comparable to Jesus's cumulative portrayal. Live burden: The critical side must specify the relevant agents, texts, and differentiating criteria, then show that Jesus falls within representative name-bearing rather than uniquely identity-sharing participation. Locked score: The very strong band reflects outstanding responsiveness, careful calibration, stable scope, and substantial conceptual force while preserving a meaningful evidentiary reservation concerning attestation and precise comparability.

Jesus's sayings, titles, and scriptural allusions

13:33–77:16

Principal limitation: It moves too quickly from exalted heavenly status and cloud imagery to participation in Yahweh's identity, underdeveloping Daniel's distinction between the recipient and the Ancient of Days. Live burden: The affirmative must establish why received dominion, right-hand placement, and messianic representation cannot adequately explain the titles without the stronger identity-sharing conclusion. Principal limitation: It risks treating a pragmatic defense against accusation as an exhaustive explanation of the passage, although an a-fortiori strategy could coexist with stronger Johannine implications. Divine prerogatives, dependence, and delegated authority

17:59–101:24

David Wood — section score 86/100

Jesus presents universal judgment, resurrection authority, and honor equal to the Father's as functions and treatment supporting his personal claim to divine status.

Strongest feature: The argument brings together universal judgment, life-giving activity, resurrection, and expressly equal honor, producing a concentrated textual case that bears immediately upon Jesus's claimed divine status. Principal limitation: It acknowledges but insufficiently interprets the passage's granted and entrusted language, leaving the exclusivity of these prerogatives and the inference from function to identity materially underqualified. Live burden: The affirmative must show why the Father's commissioning language describes incarnational administration rather than an exalted agent receiving powers that remain ultimately derivative. Locked score: The very strong band properly rewards direct motion contact, formidable cumulative evidence, and clear argumentative structure while registering the unresolved significance of delegation and the limited treatment of its strongest alternative.

Alex O'Connor — section score 92/100

Jesus receives and delegates glory, judgment, and forgiveness, showing that these prerogatives can represent transmitted authority rather than uniquely identifying their possessor as God.

Strongest feature: The reply uses textual grants to Jesus and subsequent delegation to disciples to challenge the asserted exclusivity of glory, judgment, and forgiveness with direct, structurally relevant counterexamples. Principal limitation: Transferability alone does not eliminate differences between universal and limited jurisdiction, intrinsic and derivative possession, or uniquely comprehensive and locally commissioned exercise of the same function. Live burden: The critical side must compare scope and mode carefully enough to show that Jesus's prerogatives provide no distinctive identity evidence beyond what delegated agents receive. Locked score: The very strong band recognizes excellent diagnostic contact, stable limitation, and powerful textual warrant while retaining an important qualification that differentiated possession could preserve cumulative evidential force for the affirmative.

Worship, early devotion, and hostile reactions

Strongest feature: The cumulative catalogue of prayer, hymns, baptismal practice, liturgy, doxology, and worship provides concrete evidence that elevated devotion to Jesus was extensive rather than isolated or merely honorific. Principal limitation: The argument compresses both the breadth of the earliest community and the cultic significance of each practice, then crosses too readily from follower devotion to Jesus's historical self-understanding. Live burden: The affirmative must connect these early practices to remembered claims or conduct of Jesus, while distinguishing divine worship from exalted devotion compatible with representative status. Locked score: The strong and competent band recognizes persuasive evidence for early high christology but preserves substantial reservations about scope, historical transmission, semantic differentiation, and relevance to the personal-claim motion.

Alex O'Connor — section score 88/100

Principal limitation: Misunderstanding does not make an immediate stoning response evidentially irrelevant, because even mistaken hearers can reveal how provocative language sounded within the narrated cultural setting. Live burden: The critical side must distinguish which reactions arise from narrative misunderstanding, which reflect plausible contemporary construal, and what independent evidence adjudicates between those possibilities. Locked score: The very strong band recognizes precise underdetermination, strong narrative relevance, and appropriately limited force while preserving a consequential reservation about whether the response weakens or merely qualifies the affirmative's contextual corroboration.

Live burden: The critical side must show that secrecy and misunderstanding favor christological development over deliberate scriptural allusion, rather than merely qualifying the clarity and publicity of disclosure.
