Overall assessment

Trent Horn — Cumulative Christian evidential case: 74/100

Published strengths

  • Horn constructs a genuinely cumulative route rather than resting everything on one proof, moving from contingency and immaterial causation through morality to historical revelation.
  • He treats sincere nonbelief charitably, explicitly allowing blameless cognitive error and salvation for sincere seekers instead of attributing disagreement to vice.
  • His developing-world defense supplies a positive greater-good account centered on compassion, courage, forgiveness, freedom, and eventual perfection.
  • He gives Christianity meaningful evidential vulnerability by identifying historical and philosophical defeaters that could rationally lower confidence.

Published weaknesses

  • The decisive bridge from generic theism to Christianity remains underdeveloped; resurrection and revelation are invoked without enough historical evidence or comparison with alternatives.
  • Appealing to unknown divine reasons makes compatibility possible but supplies weak comparative warrant and risks shielding theism from the suffering evidence under dispute.
  • The reply that eliminating evil would mean eliminating humanity answers a stronger proposal than O'Connor offered and diverts into atheistic moral grounding despite an internal critique.

Alex O'Connor — Skeptical evidential objections: 84/100

Published strengths

  • O'Connor consistently frames suffering as comparative evidence, conceding logical compatibility while arguing that atheism predicts the observed pattern more naturally.
  • His hiddenness replies isolate the key asymmetry: ordinary sincere error is unsurprising under atheism but demands explanation if a loving God can correct it noncoercively.
  • He precisely identifies the problem of evil as an internal critique and later calibrates it to the loving God Christianity specifically requires.
  • His distinction between probability-raising evidence and sufficient overall warrant permits serious concessions to resurrection evidence without abandoning cumulative assessment.

Published weaknesses

  • The hiddenness case relies heavily on the disputed premise that perfect love requires recognizable belief during the relevant lifetime, with limited treatment of developmental or postmortem possibilities.
  • The fairness reply invokes possible damnation for honest nonbelief even after Horn explicitly states that sincere, nonculpable seekers may be saved.
  • His resurrection assessment assigns an intuitive evidential increase without enough historical analysis to establish either its size or why it remains insufficient.
