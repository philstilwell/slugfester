Overall assessment

Adam Lloyd Johnson — God grounds objective morality: 71/100

Published strengths

  • Johnson consistently forced a comparison between rival ultimate stopping points and demanded positive support for Wielenberg’s foundational moral truths.
  • His lucky-coincidence objection clearly exposed the epistemic gap between independently necessary moral facts and beliefs shaped through unguided evolutionary history.
  • He distinguished created loving purpose from divine judgment, directly answering the concern that punishment alone generates moral obligation.

Published weaknesses

  • The evolutionary explanation of motherly love addressed the origin of feelings but did not show that the loving relation lacks objective value.
  • His resemblance account repeatedly explained why obedience is good while leaving Wielenberg’s specific question about obligatory mustness unanswered.

Erik Wielenberg — Objective morality needs no divine foundation: 82/100

Published strengths

  • Wielenberg’s axiom-parity challenge precisely targeted whether divine grounding actually escapes the foundational stopping point attributed to non-theistic realism.
  • He separated value from obligation with unusual clarity and exposed the missing inference from good resemblance to binding requirement.
  • His responses directly defined non-natural properties, made Platonism optional, and offered a concrete mechanism for limited evolutionary convergence.

Published weaknesses

  • His abductive defense of basic moral axioms remained thin and depended heavily on the disputed claim that every objective theory contains them.
  • The evolutionary reply preserved a useful probability qualification about recognizing others’ pain and rights, but overstated that generalization inevitably identifies relevant similarity on the way toward moral truth.

Ultimate foundations and stopping points

33:30–1:02:32

Adam Lloyd Johnson — section score 71/100

Johnson makes God, rather than propositions about God, the ultimate reality and identifies God’s triune loving character as what humans recognize and call good.

Strongest feature: Johnson offers a clear ontological distinction between God as the concrete ultimate reality and propositions that merely describe God, thereby giving his grounding account a definite terminus rather than leaving it at an unexplained sentence or abstract rule. Principal limitation: Listing divine kindness, respect, care, and unselfishness does not explain why those traits are morally good; saying humans call them good risks relabeling the target property instead of grounding it, so Wielenberg’s specific challenge remains only partly answered. Live burden: Johnson must supply a non-circular bridge from God’s character to its normative value and show why a concrete bearer explains goodness better than Wielenberg’s fundamental moral truths. Locked score: 68 recognizes a relevant and intelligible reply with good clarity, while its decisive value-grounding inference receives limited warrant.

Erik Wielenberg — section score 80/100

Wielenberg argues that calling Trinitarian love good may itself be a foundational moral axiom, so divine grounding does not automatically improve on non-theistic moral foundations.

Strongest feature: Wielenberg isolates the precise comparison the motion requires by distinguishing the loving relation from the further proposition that this relation is good, then arguing that an unexplained goodness claim would leave theism and non-theistic realism with structurally similar foundations. Principal limitation: The parity result depends on showing that Johnson cannot ground goodness in God’s concrete character, and this opening challenge poses that question rather than independently demonstrating that every divine-character account must contain an ungrounded moral axiom. Live burden: Johnson must explain why the ultimate being’s loving traits possess moral value without merely redescribing them as kind, caring, respectful, and unselfish, while Wielenberg retains responsibility for defending the claimed symmetry. Locked score: 84 reflects a highly precise, charitable, motion-level challenge whose central premise remains disputed rather than established.

Non-natural properties and intrinsic value

38:53–48:19

Adam Lloyd Johnson — section score 64/100

Johnson asks Wielenberg to define non-natural moral properties and address the concern that their departure from physicalism makes the account controversial among atheists.

Strongest feature: Johnson asks two diagnostic questions that force Wielenberg to specify what kind of reality moral properties occupy and to acknowledge that atheism alone does not entail acceptance of his non-natural ontology. Principal limitation: Resistance from physicalist atheists is sociological pressure rather than evidence that non-natural moral properties are false, and the challenge supplies no argument establishing either physicalism or the breadth and force of that constituency objection. Live burden: Wielenberg must explain the positive content of non-naturalness and why irreducible moral properties are preferable to physical reduction, while Johnson would need a truth-relevant objection rather than mere discomfort to turn clarification into a comparative advantage. Locked score: 76 rewards the clear and responsive request for ontological detail but reflects its modest evidential support and partly peripheral popularity concern.

Erik Wielenberg — section score 82/100

Wielenberg defines moral properties as real yet nonphysical, reasoning that wrongness differs categorically from measurable features and therefore belongs to a fundamental non-natural domain.

Strongest feature: Wielenberg directly defines non-naturalism, carefully separates science from scientism, and explains through the contrast between wrongness and properties such as mass or volume why objective morality may be real without being physically measurable. Principal limitation: The argument moves largely from moral appearance and the asserted implausibility of physical reduction to a separate fundamental category, without testing sophisticated reductionist alternatives or showing that failure of present empirical access establishes irreducibility. Live burden: Wielenberg must strengthen the inference from apparently nonphysical moral features to genuinely fundamental properties, whereas Johnson cannot defeat the account merely by noting that physicalist atheists reject it. Locked score: 81 reflects exceptional responsiveness, precision, and relevance to the motion, tempered by an intuitive evidential base and an underdeveloped elimination of competing ontologies.

Goodness, obligation, and accountability

52:26–1:05:57

Adam Lloyd Johnson — section score 70/100

Johnson accepts that goodness and duty differ, illustrates supererogatory goodness with an orphanage, then links good obedience to loving God and resembling Trinitarian love.

Strongest feature: Johnson explicitly accepts Wielenberg’s value-duty distinction and illustrates it effectively with a morally good orphanage project that is not obligatory, showing that he understands the precise conceptual problem his theory must solve. Principal limitation: His proposed endpoint again establishes that obedience is good because it resembles and expresses divine love, but it never identifies what makes that good act required, effectively restating the premise Wielenberg had already shown insufficient for mustness. Live burden: Johnson must add a principle connecting created persons, divine relationship, or authority to binding duty without assuming the obligation under explanation; Wielenberg need only sustain the exposed gap. Locked score: 60 gives credit for clarity, concession, and relevant engagement, while the unanswered central question substantially lowers coherence and responsiveness.

Erik Wielenberg — section score 88/100

Wielenberg distinguishes moral value from obligatory mustness and argues that showing obedience resembles divine goodness does not explain why obedience is required.

Strongest feature: Wielenberg identifies the missing inference in Johnson’s account: resemblance to a good exemplar may explain why obedience is valuable, but goodness alone does not entail the mustness that constitutes obligation. Principal limitation: Because the move is a diagnostic challenge, it does not itself provide Wielenberg’s rival theory of obligation or prove that no personal relationship could generate duties, although neither task is required for the objection to succeed. Live burden: Johnson must identify what converts loving resemblance into a binding requirement, while Wielenberg’s narrower burden is to maintain the conceptual distinction rather than establish an entire non-theistic moral system within this question. Locked score: 90 reflects exceptional coherence, precision, charity, and direct contact with the obligation burden, with only the inherent incompleteness of a targeted challenge.

Evolution and access to moral truth

1:07:13–1:13:25

Adam Lloyd Johnson — section score 81/100

Johnson argues that unguided evolutionary contingency could have produced very different moral beliefs, leaving their alignment with independent necessary moral truths in need of explanation.

Strongest feature: Johnson identifies a genuine epistemic challenge by separating independently existing moral truths from beliefs shaped by unguided evolution and asking what explains enough correspondence for humans to possess reliable moral knowledge. Principal limitation: The objection characterizes evolution as haphazard and invokes divergent possible paths only generally, without considering directional selection, cultural correction, or reasoning mechanisms that might reduce the alleged coincidence, so its evidential force remains suggestive rather than measured. Live burden: Wielenberg must explain truth-tracking rather than merely the usefulness of moral beliefs, while Johnson must show that theism actually predicts reliable access rather than simply announcing divine guidance as an available explanation. Locked score: 81 reflects a coherent, central, and clearly targeted challenge with strong burden contact, tempered by limited development and somewhat overstated evolutionary randomness.

Erik Wielenberg — section score 77/100

Wielenberg concedes imperfect alignment, fixes necessary truths on one side, and explains partial convergence through selected self-concern plus similarity-based generalization to others.

Strongest feature: Wielenberg acknowledges imperfect moral agreement and theism’s explanatory advantage, then supplies a two-stage mechanism in which survival favors recognizing one’s own pain and rights while similarity reasoning extends status to others. Principal limitation: The necessity of moral truths removes variation on the truth side but does not make beliefs track them, and although he says recognizing others’ pain and rights is probable and that the process heads toward truth, his claim that generalization inevitably identifies relevant similarity remains too confident. Live burden: Wielenberg must show that selection and reasoning yield truth-sensitive reliability beyond self-protection, while Johnson must not treat every imperfect or natural process as incapable of knowledge. Locked score: 77 rewards exceptional responsiveness, relevant mechanism-building, and concessions, with deductions for limited warrant and the overconfident inevitability claim.
