Overall assessment

Trent Horn — Resurrection case: 78/100

Published strengths

  • Connected Jewish resurrection expectations, Pauline transformed-body language, embodied encounters, and the empty tomb into a coherent physical-continuity interpretation.
  • Distinguished sincerity from truth and repeatedly treated danger or martyrdom as supporting commitment rather than independently proving resurrection.
  • Offered concrete differences between Mormon and resurrection traditions, including prompting, incentives, artifacts, historical background, and Paul's prior hostility.
  • Challenged natural explanations to account jointly for missing-body and appearance evidence instead of addressing each datum in isolation.

Published weaknesses

  • The group-appearance defense relied on broad confidence in Luke without supplying identifiable, independent corroboration for the five hundred.
  • The rival-miracle response did not adequately address continued Mormon attestation after estrangement or consistently match each proposed disanalogy to a particular claim.
  • The martyrdom case left person-specific links among firsthand bodily knowledge, danger, and testimony-specific death substantially underdeveloped.
  • The conjunction argument assumed a probability penalty without sufficiently examining dependence among natural mechanisms or equivalent commitments within the resurrection hypothesis.

Alex O'Connor — Historical skepticism: 86/100

Published strengths

  • Separated mixed Pauline and Gospel descriptions from an unambiguous claim of ordinary physical embodiment while acknowledging evidence in both directions.
  • Converted the five-hundred report into specific verification questions about identity, accessibility, independence, beliefs, and gathering circumstances.
  • Applied a detailed rival-testimony consistency test and directly explained why institutional defection can increase witness independence.
  • Distinguished sincere suffering from firsthand bodily knowledge and from martyrdom specifically caused by resurrection testimony.
  • Presented reburial and theft as calibrated historical possibilities rather than claiming that either alternative was established.

Published weaknesses

  • The comparison with better-documented Mormon reports sometimes moved too quickly from greater surviving detail to stronger overall evidential standing.
  • The Gospel-development claim remained historically compressed and did not fully establish why later physical details represent elaboration rather than preserved tradition.
  • The Pilate objection treated general ruthlessness as stronger evidence against pragmatic burial authorization than the presented warrant supported.
  • The natural tomb alternatives were not fully integrated with the appearance and proclamation evidence into a single developed historical account.

Empty Tomb, Burial History, and Natural Alternatives

Principal limitation: His inference treats an apologetic explanation presupposing emptiness as historical confirmation, although invention could generate guards, women, and restrained presentation without independently establishing the narrated event. Live burden: The opposing case must demonstrate why the reported emptiness substantially discriminates resurrection from temporary Sabbath accommodation, subsequent proper burial, or theft, whereas O’Connor must estimate these alternatives’ probabilities against the particular burial details.
