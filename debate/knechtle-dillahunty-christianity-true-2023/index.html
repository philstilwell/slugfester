Overall assessment

Cliffe Knechtle — Christianity is true: 73/100

Published strengths

  • Established that historical, relational, philosophical, and scientific questions can require different evidential methods rather than one universal laboratory standard.
  • Presented a concrete cumulative resurrection case involving death, burial, appearances, transformed witnesses, and later conversions instead of relying only on abstract possibility.
  • Used the compatible-perspectives analogy effectively to show why differing nonexclusive details need not constitute formal contradiction.
  • Offered coherent Christian frameworks for human dignity, moral objectivity, creation ideals, accommodation, and responsibility for human wrongdoing.

Published weaknesses

  • Repeatedly advanced from relevant Christian teachings or historical indicators to factual and supernatural conclusions without sufficiently discriminating those conclusions from rival explanations.
  • The Gospel-reliability case blurred background accuracy and internal consistency with broader historical truth, especially concerning the central supernatural claims.
  • The skepticism reply treated failure to provide a complete alternative as suggestive of cynicism, leaving the opponent's burden-allocation argument substantially unanswered.
  • The accommodation and free-will replies did not adequately explain why texts attributed to divine authority regulate concrete inequalities and slaveholding practices.

Matt Dillahunty — Skeptical atheism: 90/100

Published strengths

  • Consistently required methods that discriminate Christianity from mutually incompatible religions and realistic fiction rather than merely accommodating the desired conclusion.
  • Cleanly separated textual preservation from correspondence truth while granting the strongest plausible manuscript-reconstruction premise for purposes of argument.
  • Directly tested all four proposed Gospel-reliability indicators and showed why each can coexist with a narrative that is not factually true.
  • Maintained appropriate burden allocation by explaining why identifying a defective affirmative method does not require supplying a replacement worldview.
  • Addressed resurrection arguments with conditional grants, calibrated uncertainty, and nonconspiratorial alternatives rather than assuming deliberate deception.
  • Connected moral objections to specific domains, including unequal gender rules and Exodus regulations concerning slaveholding, release, and treatment.

Published weaknesses

  • The empirical-resurrection objection did not fully analyze how historical testimony can support singular physical events without repeatable laboratory confirmation.
  • The secular account established that nonbelievers can have reasons for valuing people but did not fully develop an objective foundation for equal human worth.
  • The embellishment alternative remained plausible but comparatively underdeveloped, lacking detailed source chronology and a demonstrated transmission mechanism.

Resurrection Evidence and Alternative Explanations

10:35–111:24

Cliffe Knechtle — section score 72/100

Principal limitation: The source status, independence, reliability, and historical security of the listed items are not defended proportionately, while serious natural and transmission alternatives receive no comparative treatment. Live burden: The affirmative must establish each premise at an appropriate confidence level, avoid counting dependent reports repeatedly, and show through explicit comparison why resurrection explains the secured facts better than grief experiences, legendary development, mistaken identification, or uncertain burial history. Live burden: The skeptical case must compare source quality, transmission, dependence, alternative explanations, and relevant prior expectations without treating laboratory repeatability as a prerequisite for every historical conclusion.
