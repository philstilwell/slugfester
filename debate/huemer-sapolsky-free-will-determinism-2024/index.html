Overall assessment

Michael Huemer — Libertarian free will exists and matters: 83/100

Published strengths

  • Precisely defined libertarian freedom as sometimes having genuine alternatives plus control over which alternative is realized.
  • Exposed the central gap between extensive causal influence and exhaustive determination, especially when abnormal cases are generalized to ordinary choice.
  • Defended appearances as defeasible sources of justification and separated scientific explanation from deterministic necessitation.
  • Used the innocent-framing comparison to reveal a serious omission in unconstrained deterrence-based punishment.

Published weaknesses

  • The self-defeat argument moved too quickly from avoidability in action to epistemic warrant, leaving its use of reasons insufficiently stable.
  • The appeal to deliberation, advice, reward, and punishment grouped practices that deterministic and forward-looking theories can explain differently.

Robert Sapolsky — Causal determination leaves no free will: 80/100

Published strengths

  • Presented a concrete multiscale causal account spanning immediate cues, physiology, development, inheritance, culture, and history.
  • Explained effectively how conditioning, learning, and changed inputs can produce behavioral change under determinism.
  • Separated practical dependence on blame from the moral justification of retribution and unequal entitlement.
  • Supported humane responsibility revision with historical examples of causal reinterpretation reducing punitive treatment.

Published weaknesses

  • The seamless-arc conclusion repeatedly treated pervasive causal influence as sufficient for exhaustive determination without supplying the decisive bridge.
  • The categorical dismissal of every libertarian and compatibilist conception lacked view-specific analysis and adequate comparative warrant.

Causal explanation, influence, and genuinely available alternatives

3:55–34:32

Michael Huemer — section score 88/100

Free will sometimes involves multiple genuinely available possibilities and an agent’s control over which possibility is ultimately realized.

Strongest feature: This opening defines the affirmative thesis with exceptional precision by combining genuinely available alternatives, agent control, and an explicitly limited claim that such freedom occurs only sometimes. Principal limitation: As a definition and motion-framing proposition, it supplies little independent evidence that the described alternatives or controlling relation are instantiated in actual human decision-making. Live burden: Huemer must connect the lucid conceptual formulation to a defensible epistemic basis and explain how control differs from both antecedent determination and an unexplained or merely random selection. Locked score: The placement appropriately rewards exact burden contact, stable terminology, careful existential scope, and considerable logical clarity, while withholding fuller recognition because articulation of the target phenomenon is not yet a demonstration of its reality.

Robert Sapolsky — section score 80/100

Choices reflect causal influences operating across immediate cues, physiology, childhood development, genes, culture, and deeper historical conditions.

Strongest feature: This constructive systematically connects immediate perception, physiological condition, childhood adversity, inherited disposition, cultural formation, and historical circumstance into a coherent multiscale account with substantial illustrative specificity. Principal limitation: Although these examples persuasively establish extensive causal influence, their cumulative presentation leaves implicit the indispensable transition from widespread contribution to exhaustive determination of every behaviorally available selection. Live burden: The causal route must demonstrate that these interacting antecedents completely settle which action occurs, rather than merely constraining probabilities, preferences, capacities, attention, and situational responsiveness. Locked score: The placement appropriately reflects excellent central relevance, coherent organization, and considerable evidential substance, while preserving a meaningful deduction for the still-compressed inference from pervasive causation to the exclusion of agent-controlled alternatives.

Intuition, scientific evidence, and the burden of proof

25:02–59:53

Michael Huemer — section score 84/100

Intellectual appearances are indispensable to justification, and arguments challenging free will also depend upon logical and intuitive appearances.

Strongest feature: This reply exposes the self-applicative pressure on sweeping intuition skepticism by observing that reasoning against free will necessarily relies upon logical, evidential, and intellectual appearances somewhere in justification. Principal limitation: Establishing that appearances are epistemically indispensable does not show that every appearance, or specifically the appearance of libertarian control, deserves equal presumptive reliability. Live burden: Huemer must articulate criteria distinguishing trustworthy agency appearances from historically unreliable intuitions while explaining why Sapolsky’s proposed mechanistic evidence does not defeat their initial justificatory force. Locked score: The placement recognizes strong diagnostic responsiveness, central epistemic relevance, coherent self-application, and a substantial challenge to generalized skepticism, while reflecting partial terminological instability and the missing comparative account of reliability across importantly different appearance-types.

Robert Sapolsky — section score 77/100

Felt freedom is unreliable evidence because historically compelling intuitive explanations have repeatedly been displaced by better mechanistic accounts.

Strongest feature: This reply directly challenges the evidential authority of experienced freedom by using historically vivid cases in which confident intuitive explanations yielded to increasingly detailed mechanistic understanding. Principal limitation: The analogy does not establish that introspective appearances of agency belong to the same epistemic category as speculative folk explanations constructed from limited external observations. Live burden: Sapolsky must provide a principled reliability argument specific to agency appearances, showing why their felt immediacy receives substantially reduced weight without implying indiscriminate skepticism toward perception or reasoning. Locked score: The placement appropriately recognizes clear contact with Huemer’s epistemic case, effective historical illustration, and substantial cautionary force, while preserving deductions for broad generalization and the failure to directly disprove genuinely available alternatives.

Control, deliberation, change, and reason-responsive agency

19:33–82:10

Michael Huemer — section score 75/100

Arguments for determinism appear self-defeating if rational belief requires reasons and reasons require actions or beliefs that remain avoidable.

Strongest feature: This constructive poses a consequential self-application challenge by asking whether determinism can be rationally argued for if reasons require possibilities that an agent can accept or avoid. Principal limitation: The inference shifts insufficiently between normative reasons for action and epistemic reasons supporting belief, then assumes without demonstration that determined belief formation cannot remain evidentially responsive. Live burden: Huemer must justify the avoidability requirement for epistemic warrant and explain why a causally produced belief cannot be rational when its generating process reliably tracks relevant evidence. Locked score: The placement captures the argument’s significant epistemic relevance and recognizable structure while reflecting major deductions for equivocal reason-language, an unsupported pivotal premise, and an overextended conclusion that determinism eliminates rational justification altogether.

Robert Sapolsky — section score 88/100

Determinism permits change because conditioning, learning, and altered circumstances mechanistically transform organisms and their later behavioral responses.

Strongest feature: This reply gives a concrete mechanistic account of behavioral change, explaining how conditioning, learning, altered inputs, and revised circumstances can produce later responses without presupposing self-originating transformation. Principal limitation: Its successful demonstration that causation permits change does not independently establish the stronger contention that every such change excludes meaningful agent control or genuinely open alternatives. Live burden: Sapolsky must distinguish causal participation from causal exhaustiveness and explain why deliberative learning cannot simultaneously be mechanistically realized and attributable to an agent exercising the disputed kind of control. Locked score: The placement reflects outstanding responsiveness, conceptual precision, strong practical relevance, and effective counterexample to claims that determinism makes advice or change pointless, while retaining a bounded reservation about the unproved universal exclusion of agency.

Responsibility, moral desert, punishment, and humane practice

18:18–48:34

Michael Huemer — section score 82/100

Deliberation, advice, evaluation, reward, and retributive punishment appear unintelligible if people never possess genuinely available alternatives.

Strongest feature: This constructive gathers several familiar normative and deliberative practices into an intuitively powerful challenge, directly linking alternative possibilities with advice, evaluation, reward, punishment, and practical reflection. Principal limitation: It treats functionally different practices as though each required libertarian alternatives, overlooking deterministic accounts of deliberation, learning, deterrence, communication, and prospective behavioral regulation. Live burden: Huemer must separate practices that merely influence future conduct from genuinely desert-sensitive practices and show why the latter cannot be justified without the particular control specified by his thesis. Locked score: The placement reflects strong central relevance, broad practical reach, and understandable intuitive force, while incorporating substantial reservations about underqualification, grouped phenomena, and insufficient engagement with forward-looking or compatibilist interpretations.

Robert Sapolsky — section score 82/100

Difficulty imagining society without free-will-based praise and blame does not justify retribution or unequal entitlement to benefits and suffering.

Strongest feature: This reply cleanly separates the practical difficulty of abandoning familiar praise and blame from the moral justification of retribution, punishment, and unequal entitlement to favorable treatment. Principal limitation: The assertion that rejection of desert better aligns morality with reality remains normatively compressed, particularly because causal determination alone does not specify a complete theory of permissible treatment. Live burden: Sapolsky must explain why differential suffering is undeserved while preserving principled distinctions among protection, deterrence, restitution, moral communication, and treatment of responsible versus innocent persons. Locked score: The placement reflects excellent responsiveness, stable conceptual distinctions, explicit acknowledgment of practical uncertainty, and direct contact with desert, while retaining a meaningful deduction because the central moral conclusion receives substantially less warrant than the practical critique.

Concepts of freedom, explanatory stopping points, and indeterminacy

69:55–76:00

Michael Huemer — section score 82/100

Explanation need not entail determination: scientific theories can be indeterministic, while an agent’s reasons can intelligibly explain behavior without necessitating it.

Strongest feature: This reply directly separates explanation from determination through two complementary countermodels, namely indeterministic scientific theory and agent-level explanation that cites reasons without claiming necessitation. Principal limitation: Conceptual compatibility between explanation and indeterminacy does not establish that actual human choices are undetermined or that any residual indeterminacy constitutes controlled selection rather than randomness. Live burden: Huemer must develop the positive relation between an agent, competing reasons, and the final selection so that nondetermining explanation supplies authorship rather than merely an explanatory stopping point. Locked score: The placement appropriately recognizes excellent diagnostic force, stable terminology, strong calibration, charitable scope, and decisive contact with an assumed equivalence, while retaining a bounded reservation because the counterexamples establish possibility without demonstrating the actuality of libertarian agency.

Robert Sapolsky — section score 68/100

Prior causes remain operative throughout decision-making, while quantum indeterminacy cannot transform an undetermined event into an agent-controlled choice.

Strongest feature: This reply directly addresses both components of the conceptual dispute by denying an unexplained causal stopping point and distinguishing quantum indeterminacy from purposive, agent-controlled selection. Principal limitation: The universal assertion that prior causes always exhaustively settle choice receives only compressed support, while rejecting randomness does not independently establish comprehensive antecedent determination. Live burden: Sapolsky must show why higher-level reasons and agent processes cannot exert genuine control within the causal sequence and why every proposed nondetermining explanation collapses into either prior determination or chance. Locked score: The placement recognizes strong responsiveness, clear differentiation of causation from quantum randomness, and direct subsidiary-burden contact, while preserving substantial deductions for categorical scope, incomplete warrant, and insufficient engagement with Huemer’s reasons-based model of authorship.
