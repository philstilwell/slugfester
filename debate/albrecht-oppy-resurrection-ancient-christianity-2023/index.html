Overall assessment

William Albrecht — Catholic resurrection case: 76/100

Published strengths

  • Established an early Pauline foundation while carefully limiting the claim to evidence of Paul's bodily-resurrection belief.
  • Directly engaged the visionary reading through First Corinthians 15 and a transformed-body interpretation.
  • Used concrete doctrinal and conciliar commonalities to support convergence among ancient apostolic churches.
  • Regularly acknowledged incomplete uniformity, linguistic difference, and contested reform intentions.

Published weaknesses

  • The Gospel and patristic convergence reply did not answer the decisive unknown-source, copying, selection, and independence objections.
  • The reform-versus-departure argument lacked a principled criterion for when doctrinal change creates a distinct religious system.
  • Several continuity claims moved from selected doctrinal compatibility to broad historical or institutional conclusions without a fully traced lineage.

Graham Oppy — Historical skepticism: 83/100

Published strengths

  • Precisely distinguished early resurrection belief from evidence for a bodily encounter.
  • Separated Paul's personal experience from secondhand witness reports and carefully calibrated interpretive influence as a possibility.
  • Developed distinct objections from compositional delay, unknown sources, copying uncertainty, and selective preservation.
  • Pressed early Christian plurality and regional divergence against a simple linear continuity narrative.
  • Answered the Protestant-departure taxonomy with shared inheritance and an explicit individuation challenge.

Published weaknesses

  • Several preservation arguments remained plausible hypotheses without named documentary examples or comparative survival evidence.
  • The preconciliar plurality argument risked conflating later formal definition with the prior nonexistence of an identifiable doctrinal trajectory.
  • The transmission objections were sometimes directed at Pauline claims they did not specifically contact.

Pauline Evidence for Bodily Resurrection and Appearances

4:25–12:02

William Albrecht — section score 82/100

Paul's earliest undisputed letters, despite limited narrative detail, indicate that he believed Christ was bodily resurrected and appeared to him.

Strongest feature: The chronologically early Pauline foundation directly advances the indispensable bodily-resurrection route, carefully distinguishes undisputed letters from contested authorship, acknowledges limited descriptive detail, and proportionately claims evidence for Paul's belief rather than pretending to supply an exhaustive resurrection narrative. Principal limitation: The excerpt identifies neither a particular passage nor the linguistic reasoning required to move from resurrection vocabulary and appearance testimony to a specifically tangible encounter, while the opposing visionary interpretation remains substantially unevaluated. Live burden: The case still must demonstrate that Paul's terminology and reported experience discriminate bodily manifestation from sincere visionary experience instead of merely establishing an early resurrection conviction. Locked score: The strong band reflects excellent central relevance, recoverable qualifications, and coherent chronology alongside thinner textual substantiation and limited engagement with the strongest alternative interpretation.

Graham Oppy — section score 88/100

Paul's early letters remain compatible with a vision and do not clearly describe a bodily meeting with someone restored from death.

Strongest feature: The reply challenges the affirmative's decisive inference, separating early resurrection belief from evidence that Paul's experience involved a tangible encounter between bodies, while presenting visionary compatibility as underdetermination rather than disproof. Principal limitation: Its textual assessment remains compressed because it examines no particular Pauline passage, leaves appearance language unexplained, and only briefly addresses the transformed-embodiment reading of First Corinthians 15. Live burden: The skeptical case must demonstrate that a visionary alternative fits the relevant wording and context comparably well, without neglecting evidence that spiritual embodiment could remain bodily. Locked score: The very strong band reflects exceptionally direct responsiveness, stable terminology, charitable targeting, and disciplined scope, tempered by a materially underdeveloped passage-by-passage evidential foundation.

Hostile Witnesses and Documentary Preservation

38:10–41:39

William Albrecht — section score 73/100

A hostile source can identify recognizable Christian beliefs without proving those beliefs were geographically universal or numerically dominant.

Strongest feature: The reply narrows the claim by arguing that early bishops, priests, and deacons, persisting amid conflict through Nicaea, can identify a structured community without requiring Christianity to have been prevalent. Principal limitation: Institutional persistence neither answers selective preservation nor establishes independence from Christian informants, and it cannot uniquely identify Catholic continuity rather than one community among several competing Christian lineages. Live burden: The affirmative must define the Catholic markers, show how outside testimony was acquired, and explain why continuity of offices supports exclusive apostolic and resurrection claims. Locked score: The mixed band rewards clear scope control, institutional evidence, and acknowledgment of disorder, but reflects overconfident identification and failure to answer representativeness, transmission, and plural-continuity objections.

Graham Oppy — section score 83/100

Untampered hostile writings may remain unrepresentative if Christian preservation favored pagan texts whose contents proved useful to later Christians.

Strongest feature: The reply distinguishes textual authenticity from representative survival, arguing that an untampered hostile document may still be nonrepresentative if Christian copyists preferentially preserved testimony useful to Christian claims. Principal limitation: The excerpt supplies neither manuscripts, comparative survival patterns, preservation history, nor evidence that useful hostile writings endured more often than unfavorable or irrelevant pagan material. Live burden: The skeptic must show that this selection mechanism is historically probable enough to weaken the independence of witnesses without converting every archival absence into evidence of Christian filtering. Locked score: The strong band recognizes conceptual sophistication, direct engagement with representativeness, and carefully qualified possibility, while withholding greater credit because the mechanism, scope, and documentary basis remain materially underdeveloped.

Early Orthodoxy, Diversity, and Ecclesial Structure

25:49–49:23

William Albrecht — section score 79/100

Roman and Syriac churches use different formulations yet share substantial Trinitarian, Christological, Marian, and incarnational commitments.

Strongest feature: The response tests claimed regional divergence against doctrine, adducing Trinitarian theology, joint Christological declarations, and shared convictions about Christ and Mary while recognizing that linguistic formulations need not represent incompatible beliefs. Principal limitation: Modern or later agreements cannot establish continuous identity from Christianity’s early centuries, and the excerpt leaves chronology, institutional transmission, disagreements, representativeness among Syriac communions, and the historical relevance of retrospective convergence underspecified. Live burden: The affirmative must connect doctrinal compatibility to traceable historical continuity without minimizing conciliar, political, or institutional separation. Locked score: The strong/competent band reflects concrete comparative evidence, charitable attention to linguistic difference, and responsive engagement, tempered by compressed chronology, incomplete institutional analysis, and historical generalizations broader than the examples substantiate.

Graham Oppy — section score 82/100

Numerous competing early Christian groups make preconciliar orthodoxy uncertain, with councils later establishing formal doctrinal boundaries.

Strongest feature: The reply directly challenges determinate continuity by foregrounding competing early communities and distinguishing the unsettled preconciliar landscape from later councils that formally articulated and enforced recognizable doctrinal boundaries. Principal limitation: Claiming nothing counted as orthodox before councils conflates formal recognition with substantive prior identity, while offering no representative groups, texts, prevalence evidence, institutional comparisons, or criteria for measuring the alleged plurality. Live burden: The skeptical route must show that early diversity defeats a developing proto-orthodox trajectory, rather than merely showing that trajectory faced competitors and lacked later precision. Locked score: The strong band reflects direct responsiveness and central relevance, constrained by terminological slippage, evidential generality, and an overbroad inference from conciliar definition to earlier indeterminacy.

Apostolic Continuity, Reform, and Later Christian Divisions

William Albrecht — section score 79/100

Catholic, Orthodox, and Syriac churches share central doctrine and conciliar inheritance despite incomplete uniformity and major institutional divisions.

Principal limitation: Seven shared councils does not straightforwardly characterize every Syriac communion, while papal authority, conciliar reception, and historical transmission into later churches remain underdeveloped. Live burden: The affirmative must establish that substantive doctrinal commonality and institutional ancestry together constitute durable apostolic continuity without illegitimately converting qualified family resemblance into singular institutional identity. Locked score: The very strong band recognizes responsiveness, qualification, charity, and concrete doctrinal support, while retaining reservations about conciliar variation and the bridge from shared inheritance to specifically Catholic continuity.

Graham Oppy — section score 81/100

Jewish Christian groups and divergent Syrian traditions complicate a single institutional and doctrinal path from earliest Christianity to Catholicism.

Strongest feature: The argument expands the continuity inquiry beyond Roman evidence by invoking classification as a Jewish sect and Syrian communities, thereby pressing the affirmative to explain regional variation before claiming a single institutional trajectory. Principal limitation: These examples lack chronology, representativeness, doctrinal characterization, institutional relationships, and reason why divergence signifies independent origin or discontinuity rather than development, schism, or plural expression within an apostolic inheritance. Live burden: The skeptic must establish that the communities generate incompatible lineage claims or substantive discontinuity, rather than merely confirming linguistically unsurprising diversity. Locked score: The strong/competent band recognizes direct relevance and comparative pressure on linear continuity, while preserving reservations about historical substantiation and criteria separating disqualifying divergence from plural development.
