Overall assessment

William Lane Craig — Theistic reasonableness: 84/100

Published strengths

  • Separated the strict logical problem from the probabilistic problem and identified the necessity burden governing the former.
  • Developed a coherent epistemic-limits challenge focused on remote consequences, providential history, and finite observation.
  • Connected Christian purposes and eternal fulfillment to altered expectations about suffering without calling suffering intrinsically good.
  • Insisted correctly that the motion concerns the complete evidential balance, not evil considered alone.
  • Answered the better-world challenge by distinguishing significant freedom from causally guaranteed conduct.

Published weaknesses

  • The positive evidence invoked by the total-evidence case was referenced rather than presented and comparatively weighed in the debate.
  • Providential explanations of disasters remained speculative about particular victims, actual effects, and the asserted maximizing relationship-building outcome.
  • The evolutionary contingency reply lacked the additional reliability premise needed to turn causal genealogy into a moral debunking argument.

A. C. Grayling — Secular objection: 86/100

Published strengths

  • Distinguished a conceivable better free world from the stronger question of divine feasibility and repeatedly pressed the missing bridge.
  • Defended ordinary fallible moral knowledge through experienced likelihoods rather than demanding exhaustive causal foresight.
  • Converted tsunamis and childhood cancer into a calibrated adverse inference about combined divine power, goodness, and concern.
  • Challenged unknown reasons and future compensation as insufficient sources of positive warrant.
  • Offered sentience, needs, suffering, and humane response as a concrete secular basis for moral deliberation.

Published weaknesses

  • The power-permission dilemma treated claims about different evils and different modes of prevention as inconsistent without establishing their shared scope.
  • The pluralism objection remained conditional on an insufficiently specified exclusivist salvation doctrine and answered only part of the theistic-purpose case.
  • The human-needs account did not fully supply the normative bridge from descriptive facts about suffering to stance-independent objective obligation.

Logical Compatibility, Freedom, and Better Possible Worlds

5:43–92:38

William Lane Craig — section score 88/100

The strict logical problem requires hidden premises connecting omnipotence and perfect goodness to the absence of evil, yet possible significant freedom prevents treating those premises as necessary truths.

Strongest feature: The modal structure is exceptionally clear, separating a strict contradiction from the evidential question and identifying precisely why possible significant freedom prevents the required hidden premises from counting as necessary entailments. Principal limitation: The defense establishes compatibility only unless a richer account of omnipotence and perfect goodness independently entails those premises, so its success does not determine whether evil nevertheless makes theism improbable. Live burden: The opposing case must demonstrate necessity rather than intuitive plausibility, while this position must preserve the distinction between defeating contradiction and answering the actual world's evidential severity. Locked score: The very strong band reflects excellent coherence, precise qualification, central burden contact, and charitable representation, with only the acknowledged boundary between logical compatibility and broader probability limiting the move's reach.

A. C. Grayling — section score 89/100

Bare compatibility does not show that a substantially better free world was infeasible; the theist must support the stronger modal claim that comparable good required this suffering.

Strongest feature: Grayling precisely isolates the modal burden, explaining that mere logical compatibility between freedom and suffering cannot demonstrate that an omnipotent creator lacked access to a better free world. Principal limitation: His interrogative formulation does not independently establish feasible actualization, since counterfactuals of freedom or valuable goods might constrain which improved worlds are realizable. Live burden: The theistic defense must substantiate its stronger necessity claim, while Grayling still owes engagement with sophisticated feasibility accounts rather than relying exclusively on the intuitive describability of freedom without terrible natural evil. Locked score: This exceptionally relevant, logically disciplined, charitable reply decisively contacts the hidden premise and appropriately limits its conclusion, although its evidential warrant remains less complete than its forceful burden placement, supporting the locked very-strong score of 88.

Evidential Evil, Unknown Reasons, and Human Moral Knowledge

12:30–55:55

William Lane Craig — section score 84/100

Finite observers cannot confidently infer that God probably lacks morally sufficient reasons for suffering because providential history, remote consequences, and causally relevant facts exceed their survey.

Strongest feature: The argument precisely targets the disputed induction rather than denying suffering, emphasizing that finite observers cannot comprehensively inspect remote consequences, providential interactions, or every morally relevant feature needed for confident gratuitousness judgments. Principal limitation: Human incompleteness alone does not establish unreliability, and describing the difficulty as insuperable outruns the displayed support unless ordinary comparative moral inference is independently shown to fail here. Live burden: The defense must explain why unseen complexity materially weakens this particular probability estimate, while the objection must justify extending familiar local judgments to comprehensive divine-purpose assessments. Locked score: The strong upper-band placement recognizes direct central relevance, careful epistemic scope, coherent skeptical pressure, and substantial responsiveness, while retaining a meaningful deduction for overstated force and the unresolved bridge from limited knowledge to defeated comparative reliability.

A. C. Grayling — section score 89/100

Unknown morally sufficient reasons cannot independently justify confidence in divine care when applied to tsunami drowning, painful cancer, and other severe harms whose alleged benefits remain unspecified.

Strongest feature: Grayling distinguishes the existence of morally sufficient reasons from having grounds to trust that such reasons explain tsunamis, childhood cancer, and gratuitous suffering. Principal limitation: His examples undermine claims that suffering benefits each victim, but do not fully exclude system-wide goods, benefits to others, prevention of worse outcomes, remote consequences, or independently grounded confidence in providence. Live burden: The defender must supply more than human ignorance to make divine care reasonable, while Grayling must address whether antecedent evidence for God could legitimately alter the evidential significance of otherwise inscrutable harms. Locked score: This coherent, centrally relevant, and charitable reply decisively contacts epistemic restraint and recompense, yet its incomplete treatment of broader unknown-reason strategies keeps evidential warrant below its strongest dimensions and supports the locked very-strong score of 88.

Divine Purposes, Salvation, and Eternal Recompense

15:38–91:50

William Lane Craig — section score 81/100

Conditional on Christian theism, earthly happiness is not life's chief purpose; suffering may foster knowledge of God, and eternal fulfillment may outweigh temporal hardship.

Strongest feature: The move supplies an explicitly conditional Christian background model in which knowledge of God, freely received salvation, and eternal fulfillment alter expectations without claiming that suffering is intrinsically valuable or logically required. Principal limitation: A general non-hedonic purpose does not explain the extraordinary scale, distribution, and apparent indiscriminateness of childhood cancer, disasters, or suffering that destroys rather than develops agency. Live burden: The position must connect its doctrinal purposes to actual severe harms and clarify who receives the proposed benefit, while critics must distinguish reduced expectedness from complete justification. Locked score: The strong upper-band result reflects disciplined qualification, a coherent probability-adjusting structure, central contact with the theistic-context burden, and identifiable doctrinal support, tempered by limited evidence connecting those purposes to particular victims and magnitudes.

A. C. Grayling — section score 83/100

Tsunami victims belonged to competing faiths, creating a pluralism problem if an exclusivist Christian salvation account denies many sufferers the relationship or recompense invoked to explain suffering.

Strongest feature: Grayling exposes tension between diverse-faith victims and exclusivist Christian salvation, contacting the salvific component of Craig’s divine-purpose and eternal-recompense response rather than reiterating suffering’s severity. Principal limitation: The reply leaves the operative doctrine unspecified and exclusion’s reach unestablished, while postponing development, so it cannot defeat inclusivist possibilities or answer the distinct claims about divine purpose and postmortem compensation. Live burden: Grayling must show that Craig’s explanation requires profession-dependent exclusivism, then demonstrate why alternative Christian accounts cannot extend salvation or recompense across earthly faith boundaries without becoming ad hoc or doctrinally incoherent. Locked score: At 78, this is a strong, relevant, appropriately tentative partial answer with meaningful doctrinal pressure, but its unstable scope, implicit qualifications, limited warrant, and incomplete contact with the broader target cap the result.

Divine Attributes, the Actual World, and Total Evidence

18:04–96:04

William Lane Craig — section score 87/100

Even conceding that evil counts adversely, the reasonableness of theism depends on all relevant evidence rather than on suffering considered as an isolated evidential datum.

Strongest feature: The argument frames rational assessment as cumulative, concedes evil’s adverse evidential relevance, and directly connects the total-evidence standard to whether theistic belief remains reasonable. Principal limitation: Favorable evidence is merely referenced through unspecified published arguments, so the presentation neither supplies their premises nor comparatively weighs their probability against suffering, leaving confidence in a positive theistic balance materially under-supported. Live burden: Craig must demonstrate, rather than presuppose, that identifiable cosmological, teleological, moral, or historical considerations retain sufficient independent weight after the scale, distribution, and apparent gratuity of evil receive equally explicit probabilistic treatment. Locked score: At 85, this is a very strong, coherent, exceptionally relevant framing move whose qualification and charitable concession merit substantial credit, while the absent within-debate evidential comparison prevents a higher evaluation.

A. C. Grayling — section score 82/100

At first blush, tsunamis and childhood cancers indicate that an intelligent supernatural agent concerned with humanity would be either malevolent or materially limited in power.

Strongest feature: By grounding an explicitly preliminary inference in tsunamis and childhood cancers, the reply directly confronts providential and recompense defenses with concrete, morally consequential evidence about divine character and power. Principal limitation: The malevolence-or-limited-power disjunction remains preliminary rather than comparatively established, because perfectly good omnipotence could remain compatible with suffering if sufficiently specific, morally adequate reasons were independently supported. Live burden: Grayling must demonstrate that proposed freedom, soul-making, epistemic limitation, or eventual compensation explanations are improbable or insufficient relative to the observed severity and distribution of apparently gratuitous suffering. Locked score: The very strong 87 appropriately recognizes exceptional relevance, explicit qualification, stable scope, and decisive contact with both targets, while retaining deductions because the adverse inference does not yet exclude competing explanations.

Objective Morality and the Foundation of Judgments About Evil

74:42–83:08

William Lane Craig — section score 81/100

If objective moral values exist and cannot exist without God, then recognition that some suffering is objectively evil supplies a conditional argument for God's existence.

Strongest feature: The explicitly conditional syllogism ingeniously transforms acknowledged objective wickedness from superficially countervailing evidence into affirmative theistic support, while preserving conceptual distinctions between genuine immorality and subjective disapproval. Principal limitation: Its indispensable metaphysical premise—that objective moral values cannot exist without God—receives considerably less argumentative substantiation than the deductive conclusion, leaving sophisticated secular-realist accounts substantially unanswered. Live burden: The reasoning must independently demonstrate divine necessity for moral objectivity, not merely establish widespread recognition of actual evil, and must explain why non-theistic normative foundations cannot provide comparably objective authority. Locked score: At 83, this remains a strong, logically coherent, precisely qualified, and highly responsive subsidiary reframe, with its evidential warrant and main-burden contribution constrained by the compressed foundation premise.

A. C. Grayling — section score 83/100

Objective facts about sentience, relationships, security, interests, and vulnerability to suffering can generate moral demands without requiring a divine source of obligation.

Strongest feature: The reply supplies concrete, publicly accessible features of the human condition, including sentience, vulnerability, relationships, comfort, and security, as a substantive alternative to divine moral grounding. Principal limitation: Descriptive facts about needs and suffering do not yet explain why agents objectively ought to respond in prescribed ways, leaving the normative bridge stated more than defended. Live burden: The secular account must articulate how interests generate stance-independent reasons or obligations, while the theistic argument must show why that bridge requires God rather than merely questioning its completeness. Locked score: The strong upper-band result reflects direct contact with the divine-foundation premise, stable and concrete grounding facts, proportionate presentation, and practical evidential force, balanced by the unresolved metaethical transition from what sentient beings need to what moral agents objectively must do.
