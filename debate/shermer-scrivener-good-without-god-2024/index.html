Overall assessment

Michael Shermer — Morality without God: 79/100

Published strengths

  • Shermer consistently framed morality as corrigible reasoning about suffering, flourishing, reciprocity, and the interests of affected people.
  • His interchangeable-perspectives test directly constrained special pleading and supplied a practical secular route toward impartial judgment.
  • He acknowledged uncertainty, Christian contributions, and historical setbacks, making his progress narrative more credible than a triumphalist account.
  • His distinction between possessing rights and receiving legal enforcement clarified why injustice can precede formal recognition.

Published weaknesses

  • He often moved from shared preferences or convergent psychology to objective obligation without supplying the needed normative bridge.
  • Several historical replies challenged Christian priority through broad chronology or context requests without enough specific supporting analysis.
  • His appeal to democratic resolution explained collective decision-making but did not protect moral truth or minorities from mistaken majorities.

Glen Scrivener — Christian foundations of morality: 82/100

Published strengths

  • Scrivener’s strongest move conceded secular morality and prosocial conduct before isolating the harder problem of concern for outsiders and enemies.
  • He repeatedly pressed the gap between useful cooperation and equal human worth, keeping the debate focused on universal moral scope.
  • His image-of-God and incarnation arguments offered an internally coherent theological basis for shared dignity and universal human kinship.
  • He distinguished moral obligations from enforceable rights, exposing a real institutional step that abstract ethical agreement alone does not explain.

Published weaknesses

  • He claimed broad Christian historical priority and uniqueness more confidently than the exchange’s comparative evidence could support.
  • His theological foundations depended on contested premises whose truth and public authority were usually assumed rather than independently defended.
  • The godless counterfactual preserved Jesus’ ethic but did not clearly explain why its moral authority would collapse without Christianity.

Moral reality and secular reason

2:10–44:22

Michael Shermer — section score 83/100

Shermer combines mind-independent moral truth with probabilistic inquiry, arguing that fallible human access does not reduce morality to cultural or personal preference.

Strongest feature: Shermer clearly separates the existence of objective moral truths from human certainty about them, allowing inquiry to improve without pretending that any speaker possesses infallible access. Principal limitation: His analogy with probabilistic investigation explains how beliefs might be revised, but the opening largely asserts rather than demonstrates that moral properties belong to mind-independent reality. Live burden: He still needs to identify public reasons or evidence that distinguish genuine moral discovery from durable preference, while critics need not prove relativism merely because his first statement is incomplete. Locked score: 81 reflects a coherent, candidly qualified realism whose responsiveness and calibration are appreciably stronger than its initial evidential warrant or its still-undeveloped bridge from general reality to specifically moral facts.

Glen Scrivener — section score 79/100

Scrivener contends that objective obligations join minds, mathematics, beauty, and morals in a reality richer than matter in motion, making moral realism theological.

Strongest feature: Scrivener notices that Shermer’s language of objective moral discovery resembles realism rather than mere preference, and he accurately isolates the debate’s metaphysical pressure point. Principal limitation: Calling that language theological does not show that moral facts require God; resemblance in vocabulary is not an argument for divine ontology. Live burden: Scrivener must explain why mind-independent value needs a personal source, while Shermer must identify what makes moral reasons objectively authoritative beyond human endorsement. Locked score: 74 recognizes a relevant conceptual challenge that exposes unresolved grounding, but the reply remains primarily diagnostic, risks defining theology too expansively, and offers neither a developed argument from moral realism to God nor a response to secular realist alternatives that accept objective value without treating it as legislation from a divine mind.

Genealogy of rights and moral reform

8:42–32:26

Michael Shermer — section score 79/100

Shermer answers Christian credit for abolition by citing Christian defenses of slavery and arguing that religious communities largely joined reform after broader moral change.

Strongest feature: Shermer introduces important internal counterevidence: nineteenth-century Christians defended slavery from scripture even as Wilberforce, Quakers, and other Christians opposed it, defeating any simple story of unified religious leadership. Principal limitation: The existence of pro-slavery Christians weakens exclusive credit but does not establish that abolitionist Christians merely followed secular change, and his claims about proportions and timing receive little support in the exchange. Live burden: Shermer must offer a fuller causal comparison of religious and secular reform, while Scrivener must acknowledge contrary Christian practice without abandoning the narrower claim of theological influence. Locked score: 79 rewards a direct, historically relevant reply that usefully complicates Christian genealogy, while withholding stronger credit because its “late to the game” narrative remains under-documented and somewhat sweeping.

Glen Scrivener — section score 79/100

Scrivener cites just-war reasoning, opposition to ancient exploitation, and abolition as Christian contributions from which modern rights, dignity, equality, and humanism developed.

Strongest feature: Scrivener supplies concrete historical candidates—Augustine, resistance to ancient sexual exploitation, and Christian abolitionism—rather than leaving Christian influence as an abstract cultural assertion. Principal limitation: Those examples support significant contribution, but they do not establish that the listed modern values are exclusively Christian, foundationally theological, or causally unavailable through rival traditions. Live burden: He needs comparative history that weighs Christian opposition, internal reform, and non-Christian sources, while Shermer must not treat religious inconsistency as proof that Christian ideas made no contribution. Locked score: 74 reflects a relevant, intelligible genealogy with real examples and a clear conclusion, yet the evidential comparison is thin and the move repeatedly slides from participation in reform toward ownership of the entire moral vocabulary.

Rights authority and Enlightenment foundations

15:56–24:20

Michael Shermer — section score 74/100

Shermer attributes universal rights to eighteenth-century secular reasoning and later moral-circle expansion, treating bodily autonomy as the philosophical starting point condemning slavery and genocide.

Strongest feature: Shermer connects universal rights, expanding inclusion, and bodily autonomy into a recognizable secular route from anti-tribal reasoning to prohibitions on slavery and genocide. Principal limitation: He openly begins the moral chain with autonomy instead of justifying it, while sweeping claims that comparable rights were unprecedented and purely secular disregard the earlier Christian evidence already raised. Live burden: Shermer must explain why birth confers equal autonomy and separate philosophical grounding from historical priority; rebutting Scrivener’s genealogy cannot substitute for that normative work. Locked score: 70 reflects the move’s central relevance and recoverable logic, sharply reduced by weak historical warrant, conflation of origin with justification, incomplete responsiveness to equal scope, and insufficient charity toward rival universalist traditions.

Glen Scrivener — section score 80/100

Scrivener argues that equal universal rights avoid arbitrary human self-assertion only when an authoritative creator confers unconditional value across identity, ability, wealth, and status.

Strongest feature: Scrivener carefully distinguishes rights as nonarbitrary, unconditional, universal, and equal, then presses the exact authority question left open by Shermer’s decision to start with bodily autonomy. Principal limitation: The argument assumes that rational recognition among humans is merely a power play unless an external creator validates it, without defending divine authority or engaging Shermer’s realist account of discoverable reasons. Live burden: Scrivener must show why creator valuation is authoritative rather than another unexplained assertion, while Shermer must still identify a nonarbitrary truth-maker for equal rights. Locked score: 80 reflects an exceptionally relevant and responsive positive ground stated with considerable clarity, discounted because comparative historical claims are unsupported and the dismissal of nontheological justification understates the strongest secular alternative.

Equal human worth and rights

11:14–57:48

Michael Shermer — section score 78/100

Shermer makes reversibility and generalizability tests for moral rules, using slavery and equal marriage rights to extend reasons consistently across differently situated people.

Strongest feature: Shermer articulates a powerful anti-special-pleading test: a proposed rule must survive reversal of positions, giving secular reasoning a transparent mechanism for exposing unequal treatment. Principal limitation: Reversibility works only after deciding whose perspective counts and why each participant has comparable standing, so it cannot by itself supply the universal membership premise Scrivener later targets. Live burden: Shermer must ground inclusion of weak people and outsiders rather than merely generalize among accepted members, while Scrivener must show why theological membership improves on reasoned equal consideration. Locked score: 82 reflects strong coherence, clarity, relevance, and practical illustrations, reduced by lightly supported historical claims about religious change and by the unresolved distinction between a fair procedure and the prior basis for equal status.

Glen Scrivener — section score 87/100

Scrivener distinguishes reciprocal procedure from moral membership, asking why every human—including economically weak people—must count equally before perspectives can be exchanged.

Strongest feature: Scrivener isolates the debate’s central missing premise with unusual precision: role reversal can regulate treatment only after a theory has established that every human perspective enters the calculation equally. Principal limitation: The weakest-ten-percent scenario is an intentionally stark diagnostic rather than evidence that exclusion would actually increase flourishing, and the challenge itself does not yet provide Scrivener’s positive ground. Live burden: Shermer must explain universal membership without circularly assuming equal rights, while Scrivener must later show that creation or incarnation establishes equality rather than simply redescribing his conviction. Locked score: 87 marks an exceptionally responsive, charitable, and logically focused objection that accurately grants Shermer’s egalitarian intention, uses historical exclusion effectively, and exposes a decisive scope problem without attributing the hypothetical exclusionary view to Shermer.

Scripture, Jesus, and shared humanity

26:38–1:03:21

Michael Shermer — section score 77/100

Shermer observes that canon lawyers appear to reason from cases, then asks for biblical texts explicitly supporting equal treatment or universal rights.

Strongest feature: Shermer distinguishes Christian institutional participation from revelation as the source of a rights conclusion, using Scrivener’s own account of canon lawyers reasoning from duties to claims. Principal limitation: His question does not show their reasoning was independent of scriptural premises, nor does demanding an explicit rights formula rule out a cumulative biblical trajectory. Live burden: Shermer must establish that case reasoning supplies decisive content rather than developing revealed commitments, while Scrivener must provide passages and a defensible inference from them to equal treatment and universal rights. Locked score: 86 reflects a precise, charitable, highly responsive challenge targeting the canon-law account’s key inference without denying Christian contribution, tempered because it offers limited positive grounding for Shermer’s view and imposes a textual standard potentially too narrow for historical doctrinal development.

Glen Scrivener — section score 83/100

Scrivener answers with Genesis image-bearing for male and female and unity in Christ across sex, status, and ethnicity as a trajectory toward rights.

Strongest feature: Scrivener promptly supplies two relevant scriptural anchors and connects creation-wide royal status with a Christ-centered family that crosses male and female, slave and free, Jew and gentile boundaries. Principal limitation: Shared image-bearing and ecclesial unity form a plausible trajectory, but neither passage by itself states universal civil rights, and membership in Christ is not identical to equal legal standing for everyone. Live burden: He must explain the transition from theological status to enforceable rights and universal outsiders, while Shermer must engage the full creation-and-Christ sequence rather than only one verse. Locked score: 83 reflects a highly responsive, clear, and calibrated answer that acknowledges nonideal biblical institutions, with deductions for compressed context and for treating a theological trajectory as more complete than a derived rights system.

Evolution, cooperation, and outsiders

44:46–53:46

Michael Shermer — section score 80/100

Shermer argues that reciprocity and evolved cheater detection favor sincere caring emotions, so an evolutionary origin need not reduce altruism to conscious strategic pretense.

Strongest feature: Shermer distinguishes ultimate evolutionary advantage from an agent’s immediate motive, explaining how selection for trustworthy cooperation can produce genuine affection rather than perpetual conscious calculation. Principal limitation: The cheater-detection mechanism and population claims are lightly supported, and sincere concern for friends and family does not yet establish universal obligation, outsider care, or normative selflessness. Live burden: He must connect evolved motivation to moral authority and wider scope, while critics should not dismiss felt altruism merely because its history benefited reproduction. Locked score: 79 reflects a responsive, intelligible mechanism with useful conceptual distinctions and candid uncertainty about inner states, moderated because “more selfish to be selfless” invites confusion and because the proposed bedrock explains prosocial psychology more successfully than objective or universally inclusive morality.

Glen Scrivener — section score 87/100

Scrivener grants objective secular morality and evolved prosocial conduct, then argues that naturalism still struggles to secure equal concern for enemies, outsiders, and the weak.

Strongest feature: Scrivener explicitly grants that atheists can recognize objective values and behave prosocially, then sharply relocates the dispute to equal concern for enemies, outsiders, and powerless people. Principal limitation: The restricted default range of kin selection or reciprocity does not prove that secular reflection cannot widen concern, or that Christianity uniquely caused universal inclusion. Live burden: He must connect doctrine to a distinctive, truth-tracking expansion of standing, while Shermer must explain how impartial reasoning overcomes evolved parochialism rather than simply endorsing inclusive preferences. Locked score: 87 reflects a productive concession, direct engagement with naturalistic cooperation, and a demanding scope test that targets the exchange’s deepest issue, reduced because Christianity’s comparative causal success and exclusive ability to sustain outsider protection are asserted more confidently than this move’s historical evidence permits.

Moral uncertainty and the future

1:05:42–1:19:46

Michael Shermer — section score 79/100

Shermer grounds hope in fallible inquiry: societies can preserve reforms, revise mistakes, credit Christian contributions, and gradually converge through evidence and open criticism.

Strongest feature: Shermer’s fallibilism avoids relativism and complacency: he permits error, credits Christian contributions, and ties progress to criticism that exposes cruelty and special pleading while preserving successful reforms. Principal limitation: Persistence and convergence can track power, technology, or coordinated preference rather than truth, so later consensus does not automatically constitute moral improvement. Live burden: He must identify standards distinguishing learning from durable fashion, while Scrivener must explain why revelation corrects error more reliably despite Christian disagreement and failure. Locked score: 83 reflects an intellectually modest, historically flexible model of improvement with credible practical mechanisms, reduced because revisability supports responsible inquiry more clearly than it establishes the truth conditions, direction, or universal authority of the values toward which societies supposedly converge.

Glen Scrivener — section score 73/100

Scrivener says that even under a godless counterfactual he would retain Jesus’ enemy-love ethic, treating it as a radical achievement that exceeds ordinary reciprocal morality.

Strongest feature: Scrivener answers concretely by preserving Jesus’ enemy-love ethic even if its supernatural basis vanished, highlighting the demanding outsider concern central to his argument. Principal limitation: Retaining the ethic because it remains admirable partly concedes that its practical force survives without divine truth, while praise for uniqueness does not exclude comparable ideals elsewhere. Live burden: He must explain what grounding, authority, or motivation disappears under the counterfactual, while Shermer should show how secular reasons support costly enemy concern beyond familiar reciprocity. Locked score: 74 rewards a candid commitment and relevant example that advances the discussion past easy cooperation, reduced because the answer sidesteps the changed foundation and rests on sweeping historical comparisons without demonstrating Christianity’s exclusivity or causal responsibility for the ethic’s wider adoption.
