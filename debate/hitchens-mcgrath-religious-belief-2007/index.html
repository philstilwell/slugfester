Overall assessment

Alister McGrath — Christian belief as credible and constructive: 80/100

Published strengths

  • Clearly separated scientific method from total worldview adjudication while avoiding the claim that science itself proves Christianity.
  • Reframed atonement through voluntary, restorative, and transformative models that directly addressed the punitive substitution objection.
  • Used motive symmetry effectively: desire was treated as truth-neutral whether attached to belief or unbelief.
  • Identified enemy-love and Jesus's nonviolence as internal Christian resources against retaliation and dehumanization.

Published weaknesses

  • Several replies established conceptual room or internal norms without supplying enough affirmative evidence, institutional uptake, or direct contact with the strongest natural-history and coercion objections.
  • The progressive-revelation proposal lacked sufficiently explicit criteria for consistently adjudicating difficult texts and did not answer the assigned redemption and coercive-authority components.

Christopher Hitchens — Religion as unsupported and coercive: 80/100

Published strengths

  • Distinguished minimal cosmological possibility from detailed revelation with the debate's strongest diagnostic reply.
  • Connected delayed, localized revelation to humanity's long history of suffering and demanded an explanatory account of timing and scope.
  • Supplied solidarity, reciprocity, and evolved cooperation as concrete secular resources for moral practice.
  • Separated religion's possible welfare benefits from both theological truth and its general moral character.

Published weaknesses

  • Some categorical claims about religion, monotheism, sexuality, and fascism generalized beyond the evidence and underrepresented doctrinal or historical variation.
  • The secular-morality case explained cooperative capacities more fully than it justified categorical obligations when reciprocity is absent or sacrifice is personally costly.

Public Evidence, Science, and Religious Explanation

16:54–97:31

Alister McGrath — section score 78/100

Scientific methods do not adjudicate God's existence, so nature remains available for atheist, agnostic, or Christian interpretation rather than methodologically compelling atheism.

Strongest feature: The argument carefully distinguishes scientific method from comprehensive worldview adjudication, correctly preserving multiple interpretive possibilities and resisting the unsupported assumption that scientific practice itself necessarily entails atheism. Principal limitation: Methodological underdetermination establishes conceptual permission, not evidential parity, and the reply leaves untouched the specific chronology, suffering, and delayed-revelation considerations offered as comparative evidence against Christian interpretation. Live burden: The case must provide affirmative public warrant for Christianity and show that it survives comparative scrutiny, rather than treating science's jurisdictional limits as sufficient support for a particular theological conclusion. Locked score: The strong/competent band recognizes excellent precision, coherent methodological reasoning, and central relevance, while reflecting restricted evidence, compressed asymmetries among interpretations, and materially incomplete responsiveness to the natural-history objection.

Christopher Hitchens — section score 88/100

A late, localized Christian intervention sits uneasily with humanity's long suffering natural history and raises difficult questions about the designer's competence, concern, and timing.

Strongest feature: The objection places Christianity's localized redemptive narrative against humanity's immense prior natural history, producing a concrete explanatory challenge about prolonged suffering, delayed intervention, geographical restriction, and the slow dissemination of supposedly universal salvation. Principal limitation: Its stark alternatives of ineptitude, cruelty, or indifference implicitly assume that Christianity requires the particular intervention model presented, leaving gradual revelation, providential natural history, autonomy, and nonliteral theological accounts insufficiently considered. Live burden: The challenge must show that those alternatives cannot coherently explain the timing, localization, and accumulated suffering without surrendering claims of competent and benevolent providence. Locked score: The upper strong/competent band reflects unusually powerful relevance, coherent chronology, and substantial evidential warrant, tempered by compressed doctrinal representation and somewhat overstated claims about what every Christian necessarily believes.

Divine and Secular Grounds of Moral Obligation

39:27–83:08

Alister McGrath — section score 80/100

Evolved tendencies and culturally manipulated values may support moral practice without fully explaining obligation against interested power, motivating inquiry into a transcendent moral foundation.

Strongest feature: The move fairly distinguishes Hitchens's evident moral competence from the deeper justificatory question, directly asking how secular values resist manipulation by interested power and acquire authority beyond evolved disposition or cultural preference. Principal limitation: A transcendent basis remains insufficiently defined, and identifying difficulties for secular justification does not itself explain how transcendence supplies obligation, resolves disagreement, or avoids analogous interpretive and epistemic problems. Live burden: The argument must articulate the mechanism connecting transcendence to binding reasons and show why solidarity, reciprocity, or secular moral realism cannot accomplish comparable justificatory work. Locked score: The strong/competent band reflects precise burden contact, charitable framing, coherent philosophical relevance, and appropriately interrogative calibration, moderated by an incomplete positive account and reliance on a challenge whose decisive comparative premise remains to be demonstrated.

Christopher Hitchens — section score 79/100

No uniquely religious moral act appears unavailable to unbelievers, whereas claimed divine commands can furnish distinctively religious motivations for wicked conduct.

Strongest feature: The challenge sharply tests religion's practical indispensability by contrasting ordinary moral capacities shared with unbelievers against conduct allegedly motivated by distinctive divine commands, creating an accessible asymmetry for examination. Principal limitation: Behavioral nonuniqueness does not answer whether morality requires a transcendent ontological foundation, and repeated failure to receive an example cannot establish the universal absence of uniquely religious moral action. Live burden: The argument must distinguish motivation, availability, justification, and grounding, then demonstrate why shared conduct undermines the proposed source of obligation rather than merely showing that unbelievers can behave morally. Locked score: The mixed band reflects strong rhetorical relevance and a useful practical challenge, offset by incomplete contact with the decisive metaethical burden, ambiguous criteria for uniqueness, and evidential overreach from anecdotal unsuccessful questioning.

Religious Harm, Extremism, and Constructive Effects

33:28–90:33

Alister McGrath — section score 79/100

Jesus's nonviolent example gives Christianity an internal standard for criticizing violent adherents and can motivate forgiveness that interrupts retaliation.

Strongest feature: The move identifies a specific internal Christian norm and explains its critical operation, using Jesus's nonviolence and practiced forgiveness to show how religious commitment can interrupt retaliation rather than simply accompany violence. Principal limitation: A normative capacity and one constructive example do not establish characteristic institutional effects, and the response does not contact the opposing mechanisms of divine surveillance, compulsory obedience, thought judgment, or threatened punishment. Live burden: The case must demonstrate reliable uptake, governance, and corrective effectiveness across Christian communities while explaining how the nonviolent norm constrains coercive doctrines and institutions in practice. Locked score: The strong/competent band reflects clear formulation, matched calibration, direct relevance to constructive effects, and credible illustrative support, moderated by limited institutional evidence and incomplete responsiveness to the targeted coercive-authority challenge.

Christopher Hitchens — section score 72/100

Religious health or welfare benefits may be genuine, but they establish neither theological truth nor the generally benign moral character of religion.

Strongest feature: The response cleanly separates psychological or medical utility from theological truth and general moral character, conceding possible benefits while preventing an invalid evidential transition from feeling better to believing truly. Principal limitation: The distinction answers an effects-based inference but does not dispute the existence, internal authority, or actual operation of the specific nonviolent norm and forgiveness example offered by McGrath. Live burden: The reply must evaluate whether constructive effects are representative, causally religious, and sufficiently weighty for the moral half of the motion, even if they cannot authenticate supernatural claims. Locked score: The strong/competent band reflects exceptional conceptual clarity, proportionate calibration, charitable concession, and important relevance, moderated by limited direct engagement with the opponent's concrete examples and the narrower normative claim those examples were introduced to support.

Scripture, Salvation, Sacrifice, and Coercive Authority

10:00–86:45

Alister McGrath — section score 81/100

Christ's death admits multiple biblical, transformative models centered on freely offered purification and restoration rather than imposed punishment or transferred responsibility.

Strongest feature: The response directly reframes atonement away from a single punitive transaction, distinguishing later terminology from multiple biblical models and emphasizing voluntary suffering, purification, restoration, and transformation without denying the seriousness of wrongdoing. Principal limitation: Multiplicity and voluntariness do not yet explain why Christ's suffering is redemptively necessary, how it changes another person's condition, or whether punitive substitution remains embedded within influential Christian doctrine. Live burden: The account must specify a coherent causal and moral model connecting voluntary sacrifice to restoration while preserving each wrongdoer's responsibility and avoiding the transfer of deserved punishment. Locked score: The very-strong band reflects highly effective reframing, excellent precision, strong charity, and direct contact with the central objection, tempered only by the unresolved mechanism of redemptive efficacy and incomplete comparative doctrinal substantiation.

Christopher Hitchens — section score 80/100

Vicarious redemption is morally objectionable if one person's punishment supposedly forgives another while leaving the wrongdoer's individual responsibility unexplained.

Strongest feature: The objection clearly connects nontransferable personal responsibility with the moral difficulty of punishing an innocent representative, directly challenging a central Christian account of redemption through an intelligible and consequential ethical principle. Principal limitation: Its categorical condemnation implicitly treats punitive substitution as the controlling model, without examining restorative, participatory, or voluntarily representative interpretations that might preserve responsibility while understanding Christ's suffering differently. Live burden: The criticism must demonstrate that these alternative theological models still entail objectionable punishment or cannot explain redemptive necessity without transferring guilt from the accountable wrongdoer. Locked score: The strong/competent band reflects excellent relevance and coherent moral pressure, moderated by incomplete doctrinal delimitation, limited comparative warrant, and materially reduced charity toward the strongest available restorative interpretation.

Consolation, Human Dignity, and Worldview Motivation

13:54–62:59

Alister McGrath — section score 83/100

McGrath describes his conversion from scientific atheism to Christianity as arising from intellectual examination and explanatory coherence, not psychological need or wish fulfillment.

Strongest feature: The autobiographical account directly supplies relevant first-person evidence about McGrath's own motivation, carefully distinguishing perceived intellectual coherence from psychological need and limiting the claim to an individual conversion rather than believers generally. Principal limitation: Personal motivational testimony cannot independently establish theological truth, and the answer contacts the consolation allegation while leaving the separate challenge concerning innate moral agency and exclusive divine command substantially unanswered. Live burden: The reply must connect intellectual conviction to publicly assessable reasons and address whether religious accounts preserve ordinary human moral discrimination without treating introspection as decisive evidence. Locked score: The strong/competent band reflects precise scope, proportionate calibration, and meaningful contact with wish-fulfillment criticism, moderated by autobiographical evidential limits and only partial responsiveness to the compound opposing move.

Christopher Hitchens — section score 75/100

Religious consolation should not displace truthfulness, and human moral discrimination need not depend exclusively on commands from a celestial authority.

Strongest feature: The move grounds secular moral capacity in recognizable human integrity, directly resisting the degrading proposition that people possess no meaningful discrimination between right and wrong without immediate supernatural instruction. Principal limitation: It combines an underdeveloped criticism of consolation with an overstated inference from innate moral discrimination to the rejection of divine grounding, overlooking theistic accounts in which conscience is natural yet ultimately grounded in God. Live burden: The argument must distinguish psychological moral competence from metaethical justification and establish why acknowledging an innate conscience excludes, rather than merely coexists with, a transcendent explanation of obligation. Locked score: The strong/competent band reflects substantial relevance and intelligible constructive force, moderated by modest evidential development, compressed distinctions between agency and foundation, and insufficient engagement with the strongest conscience-based theistic alternative.
