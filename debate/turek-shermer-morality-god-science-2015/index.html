Overall assessment

Frank Turek — Theistic moral grounding: 76/100

Published strengths

  • The descriptive-prescriptive objection directly exposed the missing bridge between evolutionary history and binding duty, becoming the pro side's most consistently developed central argument.
  • The ontology-epistemology distinction clarified that disagreement about moral knowledge does not automatically refute a proposed metaphysical ground of moral truth.
  • The divine-nature response supplied a coherent third option to the elementary Euthyphro dilemma and avoided reducing morality to arbitrary commands.
  • The pro side repeatedly contacted central burdens, especially the requirements for normative authority, comparative standards, and an explanation of objective obligation.

Published weaknesses

  • The materialism critique treated physical causation, reaction, irrationality, and unreliability as equivalent without addressing compatibilist or nonreductive accounts.
  • The difficult-cases reply caricatured evolutionary naturalism as making human beings valueless, ignoring Shermer's explicit appeals to sentience, rights, autonomy, and flourishing.
  • The slavery response preserved a relevant distinction but generalized biblical practices too broadly and omitted coercive or inheritable forms that complicated its defense.

Michael Shermer — Evolutionary moral realism: 79/100

Published strengths

  • The sentient-flourishing framework supplied a positive naturalistic basis rather than relying exclusively on objections to divine grounding.
  • The genocide answer translated sentient interests and rights into a direct, coherent, and proportionately forceful condemnation of a difficult concrete case.
  • The rival-gods challenge precisely demanded discriminating evidence while leaving open the possibility that an adequate cumulative theistic case could answer it.
  • The autonomy argument constrained aggregate welfare through bodily integrity and individual liberty, substantially improving the framework beyond simple maximization.
  • The evolved-capacities argument connected moral psychology to observable developmental and cross-species patterns with strong relevance to the evolutionary route.

Published weaknesses

  • The account of moral progress made sweeping claims about Christian resistance while omitting the historically mixed participation of religious actors in reform movements.
  • The appeal to science and Enlightenment reason did not answer the specific challenge that material causation allegedly undermines rational warrant or agency.
  • The flourishing framework never fully defended the normative bridge from facts about sentient interests to objective and binding moral obligations.

The Ground of Objective Standards and Duties

12:19–51:51

Frank Turek — section score 77/100

Objective duties require a moral standard, an authoritative personal source, and a purpose for human life that transcend individual and collective opinion.

Strongest feature: The three-part framework directly identifies the explanatory tasks that a robust account of objective morality must address, connecting comparison, obligation, and human purpose to the debate's central grounding question with unusually clear structural relevance. Principal limitation: Its necessity claims are supported mainly through analogy and reiteration, while impersonal moral realism, constitutivism, and other alternatives are not eliminated or even sufficiently differentiated from ordinary social convention. Live burden: The argument must demonstrate why objective standards require personal authority and transcendent purpose, rather than merely showing that a divine account conveniently supplies all three elements together. Locked score: The strong but qualified band reflects excellent burden contact and recoverable organization alongside materially overstated warrant, missing qualification, and limited engagement with competing explanations.

Michael Shermer — section score 84/100

Individual sentient beings' survival and flourishing provide an objective moral basis, while evolved empathy, reciprocal altruism, and cooperation supply relevant moral capacities.

Strongest feature: The proposal gives the naturalistic side a substantive positive foundation rather than merely criticizing theism, connecting sentient interests, flourishing, empathy, and cooperation directly to the motion-level burden with clear practical intelligibility. Principal limitation: It compresses the crucial transition from facts about what sentient beings need or characteristically value to objective obligations, and it does not specify how survival, flourishing, and conflicting individual interests are ordered. Live burden: The framework must defend a normative principle granting equal, nonarbitrary significance to sentient interests and provide rules for resolving collisions without treating evolutionary prevalence as moral authority. Locked score: The strong band recognizes excellent motion contact, coherent construction, and plausible evidential support for moral capacities, while preserving the unresolved is-ought bridge, implicit qualifications, and incomplete conflict-resolution architecture.

Evolution, Flourishing, and the Is–Ought Bridge

16:24–111:52

Frank Turek — section score 80/100

Evolution can describe inherited behavior and survival outcomes, but descriptive processes lack personal authority to prescribe binding duties about how anyone ought to behave.

Strongest feature: The move sharply isolates the descriptive-prescriptive divide and asks how evolutionary facts could acquire genuinely binding force, directly contacting the indispensable normative bridge rather than disputing the empirical existence of evolved cooperation. Principal limitation: It additionally assumes that prescription must originate in personal authority, although the is-ought objection alone does not exclude rational, contractual, constitutive, or realist principles that supplement evolutionary description. Live burden: A successful answer must explain why every objective normative bridge requires an authoritative person, while the opposing account must identify and defend the independent value premise connecting sentient facts to duties. Locked score: The upper strong band reflects precise formulation, excellent relevance, and fair recognition that evolution might inform morality, moderated by the unproved transition from insufficient description to necessary personal legislation.

Michael Shermer — section score 81/100

Adults, infants, primates, and capuchin monkeys display moral emotions and sensitivity to fairness, supporting an evolved and partly innate basis for moral capacities.

Strongest feature: Shermer assembles a developmentally and cross-species comparative pattern connecting adults, infants, primates, and capuchins, thereby giving the evolved-capacities thesis coherent, highly relevant observational support. Principal limitation: Those observations establish evolutionary plausibility more readily than categorical innateness or genetic determination, because the presentation supplies neither representative documentation nor controls separating inheritance, learning, environment, and convergent behavior. Live burden: He must connect fairness-sensitive behavior to specifically moral cognition and provide discriminating evidence that its developmental and phylogenetic distribution reflects inherited adaptations rather than broader social-learning capacities. Locked score: At 81, the constructive succeeds through strong coherence, excellent burden contact, and appropriately positive evidence, while materially overstated certainty, broad scope, and absent qualification prevent the observational pattern from independently establishing its strongest biological conclusions.

Divine Authority, Revelation, and Moral Knowledge

30:23–115:36

Frank Turek — section score 82/100

Questions about knowing and interpreting moral rules concern epistemology, whereas the prior ontological question asks what makes objective moral laws true or authoritative.

Strongest feature: The reply makes a conceptually important distinction between what grounds objective morality and how persons identify or interpret moral truths, preventing epistemic disagreement from automatically functioning as a metaphysical refutation. Principal limitation: Calling grounding the key question understates the independent explanatory importance of reliable access, and the analogy with ordinary communication offers only limited support for resolving profound scriptural and interreligious disagreement. Live burden: The account must retain the distinction while showing that its proposed divine ground yields an adequately reliable, noncircular method of moral knowledge compared with competing approaches. Locked score: The upper strong band reflects excellent coherence, explicit terminology, justified reframing, and strong responsiveness to both challenges, moderated by the subsidiary bridge's narrower relevance, slightly overstated prioritization, and incomplete defense of interpretive reliability.

Michael Shermer — section score 77/100

Changing religious judgments, conflicting scriptural norms, and apparently contingent commands undermine the reliability of divine-command or scriptural methods for resolving moral questions.

Strongest feature: Shermer’s historically anchored comparison identifies a consequential epistemological vulnerability: professedly authoritative religious interpretation demonstrably changes, directly challenging confidence that scripture reliably settles contested moral questions. Principal limitation: The examples and confident prediction are insufficiently representative, conflate mutable human interpretation with divine ontology, and therefore materially overstate what observed changes establish about God’s nature, commands, or an external standard. Live burden: He must substantiate systemic scriptural conflict and explain why interpretive variability discredits the proposed divine method itself, rather than merely documenting fallible believers’ inconsistent access to an otherwise stable morality. Locked score: At 70, the reply earns substantial credit for central relevance and meaningful contact with authoritative-rule reliability, while evidential thinness, missing qualification, and the unanswered grounding distinction keep it mixed.

Reason, Agency, and the Nature of Persons

26:30–125:41

Frank Turek — section score 64/100

If persons are only molecular machines, Turek argues, free will and trustworthy reasoning collapse into reaction, undermining morality, science, and materialism itself.

Strongest feature: The argument raises an important self-referential challenge concerning whether a worldview can explain reliable reasoning and responsible agency while treating cognition as fully realized through causally structured physical processes. Principal limitation: It equates material causation with nonrational reaction and then infers global unreliability, overlooking compatibilist freedom, nonreductive physicalism, and accounts in which causal mechanisms implement rather than negate reasoning. Live burden: The pro side must show an incompatibility between physical realization and norm-responsive cognition, not merely restate that molecular systems are caused, while explaining how the proposed alternative secures reliability. Locked score: The mixed band preserves the subsidiary issue's significance and recognizable argumentative structure but reflects categorical overstatement, weak comparative support, unstable terminology, and insufficient charity toward developed materialist theories of agency.

Michael Shermer — section score 68/100

Science, reason, and Enlightenment humanism provide corrective methods for harmful moral beliefs that inherited religious superstition and ancient texts have sometimes sustained.

Strongest feature: Shermer identifies an intelligible, historically consequential corrective method—Enlightenment humanism, scientific inquiry, and public reasoning—that can expose harmful inherited practices and improve practical moral deliberation. Principal limitation: his sweeping opposition between rational progress and religious superstition overgeneralizes from the illustration, supplies little historical substantiation, and compresses sophisticated, reformist religious reasoning into an undifferentiated ancient-book caricature. Live burden: this relevant appeal to successful rational practice still does not answer whether material and evolutionary causation can underwrite trustworthy reasoning, or whether scientific methods can furnish authoritative objective standards rather than merely effective problem-solving procedures. Locked score: the weak-band result reflects coherent practical relevance and a recoverable proposal, offset by materially overstated warrant, missing qualifications, limited charity, and noncontact with both decisive philosophical challenges.

Moral Progress, Rights, and Difficult Applications

55:17–108:38

Frank Turek — section score 65/100

Biblical slavery differed from race-based chattel slavery, condemned slave trading, affirmed equal human dignity, and included principles that Turek associates with liberation.

Strongest feature: The reply directly contests the opponent's universalized historical portrayal and usefully distinguishes modern race-based chattel slavery from multiple ancient practices while invoking anti-trading and equal-dignity principles within the biblical tradition. Principal limitation: It overcorrects by portraying biblical slavery principally as voluntary debt service or captive management, omitting coercive, inheritable, and unequal arrangements that complicate any categorical moral defense. Live burden: The response must examine representative texts, institutional variations, and implementation history while explaining how its liberation principles govern passages that regulate rather than prohibit coercive servitude. Locked score: The mixed band recognizes direct engagement with both historical and textual components and one valid distinction, but reflects unsupported breadth, materially unstable scope, missing counterexamples, weak evidential documentation, and an insufficiently charitable treatment of the strongest scriptural objection.

Michael Shermer — section score 76/100

Moral progress often advances when oppressed people and allies demand equal rights and expanded autonomy despite resistance from established religious communities.

Strongest feature: Shermer offers a plausible, directly relevant mechanism of moral progress by emphasizing oppressed people’s agency, allied mobilization, demands for equal rights, and the social expansion of autonomy. Principal limitation: his categorical attribution of resistance to Christians is historically indiscriminate, radically exceeds the selective examples supplied, and disregards religious participants and arguments operating on multiple sides of abolition, suffrage, and comparable reform movements. Live burden: the account partially challenges the claim that progress requires transcendent authority, but it leaves unanswered whether God’s nature supplies the operative external standard by which reform itself becomes genuinely moral progress. Locked score: the weak-band result recognizes meaningful burden contact and a coherent alternative mechanism while substantially discounting unsupported universalization, materially unstable scope, absent qualification, poor representational charity, and thin historical warrant.
