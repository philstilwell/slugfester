Overall assessment

Cliffe Knechtle & Stuart Knechtle — Biblical moral defense: 69/100

Published strengths

  • Cliffe consistently distinguished regulation from moral idealization and tied that distinction to a recognizable whole-canon interpretive method.
  • The defense offered a positive account of equal dignity through creation, neighbor-love, Exodus, redemption, and church fellowship.
  • Both defenders engaged concrete passages and historical cases instead of relying exclusively on an abstract claim about biblical goodness.

Published weaknesses

  • Cliffe’s metaethical attack on naturalism overreached by treating evolution as if it straightforwardly entailed eugenics and unequal human worth.
  • The Numbers 31 protection reading asserted innocence, purpose, and noncoercion without adequately addressing the text’s classification of the girls as plunder.

Matt Dillahunty & Joshua Bowen — Biblical slavery critique: 85/100

Published strengths

  • Bowen anchored the critique in detailed legal incidents involving purchase, inheritance, permanent status, corporal punishment, and unequal remedies.
  • Dillahunty kept autonomy and ownership central, preventing debt, warfare, or historical difference from quietly redefining the disputed institution.
  • The critics answered canonical-trajectory claims with specific New Testament passages, lexical context, comparative law, and a direct hermeneutical consistency challenge.

Published weaknesses

  • Dillahunty’s sweeping rhetoric occasionally outran the textual distinctions, especially when broad charges displaced narrower analysis of individual legal provisions.
  • Bowen’s Luke 17 argument pressed the trajectory effectively but risked treating a parable’s social presupposition as stronger moral endorsement than it independently establishes.

Interpretation and canonical trajectory

3:03–54:25

Cliffe Knechtle & Stuart Knechtle — section score 73/100

Knechtle argues that Matthew 19 treats divorce as accommodation to human hardness, supporting his distinction between regulating slavery and morally endorsing it.

Strongest feature: Knechtle separates regulation from endorsement through Jesus's Matthew 19 explanation that Mosaic divorce permission accommodated human hardness without making divorce God's ideal, providing a concrete interpretive model for biblical permissions. Principal limitation: Matthew 19 interprets divorce, not slavery, and the excerpt provides no slavery-specific text establishing ownership rules as reluctant accommodation, leaving the analogical transfer asserted rather than demonstrated. Live burden: Knechtle must show slavery regulations constrain an evil arising from sinful hearts without authorizing it, while explaining why instructions to masters and enslaved people do not constitute moral acceptance. Locked score: The fixed 78 rewards a lucid, responsive distinction addressing whether biblical treatment entails approval, while the missing textual bridge justifies the lower evidence score and leaves the defense incomplete.

Matt Dillahunty & Joshua Bowen — section score 83/100

Dillahunty argues that Philemon is a personal exception, while repeated New Testament obedience commands and silence about abolition defeat the claimed anti-slavery trajectory.

Strongest feature: Dillahunty directly answers the abolitionist trajectory by distinguishing Paul’s personal plea for Onesimus from institutional condemnation and citing four epistles that command slave obedience, even to cruel masters. Principal limitation: Saying Philemon has nothing relevant to slavery is too categorical, since receiving Onesimus as a brother may exert relational pressure without expressly declaring slaveholding immoral. Live burden: He must show why that fraternal language and its context fail to undermine slave status, while defending his reading of the obedience passages beyond their bare presence and several ancillary moral claims. Locked score: The fixed 87 reflects a highly responsive, textually specific rebuttal, while contextual support and charitable calibration remain weaker than its direct engagement with the affirmative’s strongest claimed canonical trajectory.

Legal status, ownership, and lexical context

6:02–77:17

Cliffe Knechtle & Stuart Knechtle — section score 68/100

Knechtle argues that runaway protections, capital punishment for kidnapping, and debt or wartime contexts constrain biblical servitude, making its regulation morally significant.

Strongest feature: Knechtle grounds the regulation case in provisions protecting runaways, punishing kidnappers, and addressing debt, then connects wartime captivity to divine judgment rather than presenting biblical servitude as unrestricted ownership. Principal limitation: His sweeping claim that most Old Testament slavery involved culpable war prisoners lacks representative textual support, while the prison analogy compresses imprisonment, debt service, and captivity despite their consequential differences. Live burden: To establish defensibility, he must explain why these restrictions authorize the broader institution and justify enslavement specifically, especially when collective guilt is asserted without demonstrating individual wrongdoing or answering coercive permanent status. Locked score: The fixed 71 recognizes direct engagement through relevant legal texts, yet appropriately withholds stronger credit because the central wartime justification remains under-evidenced, overgeneralized, and insufficiently calibrated to contested interpretations.

Matt Dillahunty & Joshua Bowen — section score 90/100

Joshua Bowen defines slavery as temporary or permanent ownership of a person or labor, emphasizing the master’s control and benefit from the worker’s actions.

Strongest feature: Bowen synthesizes three definitions into an operational criterion—deprivation of freedom, ownership rights over person or labor, and a master’s control and benefit—while allowing temporary and permanent forms and acknowledging that legal protections can coexist with ownership. Principal limitation: the quoted definitions support a coherent taxonomy, but their authority is not independently established, and the formula insufficiently discriminates mixed cases where property, family, and contract rules overlap. Live burden: later textual analysis must show that biblical arrangements satisfy this ownership-and-control criterion, especially against claims that ancient service was contractual or meaningfully constrained by protections for servants. Locked score: 87 reflects a precise motion-level test that answers the terminological objection, with evidence-warrant at 84 because synthesis alone neither verifies the cited authorities nor resolves every mixed-status case.

Moral foundation and autonomy

8:32–38:40

Cliffe Knechtle & Stuart Knechtle — section score 68/100

Knechtle argues that divine image, neighbor-love, Exodus, Christ’s universal sacrifice, church equality, and Philemon jointly reveal slavery as a violation of biblical human dignity.

Strongest feature: Knechtle builds a cumulative biblical case: God’s image establishes shared worth; neighbor-love condemns dehumanization; Exodus depicts deliverance; Christ dies for all; Galatians rejects slave-free rank in church; and Philemon receives Onesimus as a brother. Principal limitation: These themes support equal dignity without showing that every biblical regulation of slavery is morally defensible, and dismissing dissenters as unread in Philemon exceeds what the letter unambiguously proves. Live burden: Knechtle must explain how this anti-slavery canonical trajectory governs permissive legal texts and why receiving Onesimus fraternally entails rejecting the institution, not merely improving conduct within it. Locked score: The fixed 78 rewards direct moral grounding, responsiveness, and textual range while deducting for compressed inferences, unresolved contrary particulars, and overstatement about Philemon.

Matt Dillahunty & Joshua Bowen — section score 84/100

Bowen grants every proposed mitigation, then argues that temporary ownership of people remains immoral and that invoking divine endorsement merely substitutes faith for moral evidence.

Strongest feature: Bowen grants debt servitude, eventual release, runaway refuge, and protection from beating, then isolates temporary ownership as the objection surviving those concessions, directly answering mitigation defenses with unusual charity. Principal limitation: His premise that ownership is immoral rests chiefly on modern condemnation and intuition, without developing the autonomy-based warrant needed to show why even temporary ownership is impermissible independently of prevailing social judgment. Live burden: Bowen must justify that prohibition and explain why divine endorsement cannot supply objective grounding, instead of dismissing appeal to God as faith-based post hoc rationalization because no further evidence appears here. Locked score: The fixed 85 fits a centrally relevant, highly responsive argument whose lucid charitable hypothetical identifies the residual dispute, while evidential warrant remains weaker than coherence, precision, and burden contact.

Ancient comparison and hard-heart accommodation

49:00–59:10

Cliffe Knechtle & Stuart Knechtle — section score 71/100

Knechtle invokes Jesus’ treatment of divorce to argue that biblical legal permission may accommodate human hardness without making the permitted practice morally ideal.

Strongest feature: Knechtle uses Jesus’ treatment of divorce to distinguish legal permission from moral endorsement, challenging the inference that biblical regulation declares slavery good. Principal limitation: Matthew 19 establishes accommodation for divorce, but the reply transfers that rationale to slavery without textual support and leaves unanswered the objection that ownership, coercion, and lost autonomy remain wrong without idealization. Live burden: Knechtle must establish that slavery laws share divorce’s hard-heart logic and explain how this account answers ownership’s moral consequences, rather than merely showing that permission and approval can conceptually diverge. Locked score: 71 credits a coherent, clear, relevant interpretive reply, while recognizing that its evidential bridge is asserted, its response does not reach the autonomy objection, and its framing treats the disputed analogy as more decisive than the exchange warrants.

Matt Dillahunty & Joshua Bowen — section score 83/100

Bowen compares biblical debt-slave law with Hammurabi’s shorter release term, arguing that broad legal parity undercuts claims that Israelite slavery was exceptionally progressive.

Strongest feature: Bowen directly answers exceptional-progress claims by contrasting Hammurabi’s three-year debt-slave release with Israel’s six years, while acknowledging Deuteronomy’s more humane provision for Israelites. Principal limitation: His appeal to an international legal tradition and scholarly consensus remains compressed, and the rapid comparison does not fully specify which systems and provisions establish broad parity. Live burden: Because comparative parity only weakens a subsidiary contextual defense, the argument still needs the stated examples to carry their limited role without pretending that parity independently settles slavery’s moral defensibility. Locked score: The fixed score of 83 rewards a coherent, responsive comparison, concrete legal examples, and a carefully bounded concession, while retaining deductions for summarized scholarship, compressed comparison classes, and limited motion-level reach.

New Testament synthesis and contested cases

78:12–140:38

Cliffe Knechtle & Stuart Knechtle — section score 68/100

Knechtle argues that creation, Exodus, Christian equality, and neighbor-love expose slavery as sinful, making abolition the canon’s culminating moral implication rather than a divinely authorized institution.

Strongest feature: Knechtle organizes creation, fall, Exodus, neighbor-love, Christian equality, Philemon, and Revelation into a canonical case that treats slavery as sinful corruption and makes abolition the trajectory’s conclusion. Principal limitation: The synthesis does not demonstrate how Philemon or Acts yields institutional abolition, distinguish spiritual equality from legal emancipation, or resolve the ownership provisions and slave-obedience passages pressed by his opponents. Live burden: His conclusion therefore depends on showing why those theological themes govern the disputed particulars, rather than merely coexisting with texts that regulate ownership and command obedience within compromised institutions. Locked score: The fixed 73 recognizes a central and textually wide-ranging reply, while retaining deductions because its decisive bridge from dignity and redemptive love to legal abolition remains asserted more strongly than demonstrated.

Matt Dillahunty & Joshua Bowen — section score 83/100

Dillahunty argues that explicit biblical permissions for ownership, inheritance, beating, and unequal release condone slavery, outweighing appeals to general equality or love.

Strongest feature: Dillahunty grounds condonation in explicit permissions to own, inherit, beat, and retain people, directly contrasting those concrete rules with appeals to creation, love, and spiritual equality. Principal limitation: His closing ranges into other biblical wrongs and speculative religious protections, while calling opposing principles flowery, detours that reduce precision and charity without strengthening the central slavery inference. Live burden: He must show why these permissions cannot be harm-reducing accommodations within a fallen society and why legal toleration defeats the asserted canon-wide trajectory toward equal dignity, rather than revealing only a morally compromised historical stage. Locked score: The fixed 86 rewards direct engagement, concrete textual warrant, and forceful replies to the non-ideal defense, while deducting for rhetorical expansion, uneven calibration, and the unresolved regulation-versus-endorsement gap.
