## Adopted burdens and qualifications

Craig defines the Christian God as God decisively revealed in Jesus, without undertaking to defend every church council or biblical author. He offers cosmological, fine-tuning, moral and resurrection arguments and personal experience as a way of knowing rather than an inferential proof. His final audience answer explicitly says the first three arguments concern monotheism, while resurrection supplies the Christian identification. Assess those connecting steps without treating a generic creator as already establishing the full motion.

Pyle explicitly declines to prove nonexistence and calls himself an atheist-leaning agnostic. His opening separates absence of evidence from evidence of absence by expected detectability. He critiques the offered reasons; he does not adopt a universal atheist disproof burden. His positive claims about naturalism’s track record, rival explanations, moral theories and testimony nevertheless require the support he actually offers. Craig’s repeated claim that no atheist case has been made is itself an assessable move, not a rule assigning Pyle a contrary burden.

Pyle says he rejects neither every causal inference nor the possibility of explaining a cause without explaining that cause’s origin. His virus and Newton examples explicitly concede those points. He asks what warrants identifying the cause and whether the hypothesis yields independent testable content. He later distinguishes merely having a cause from being a personal transcendent cause. His Kant clarification rejects deciding cosmology by a priori methods, rather than endorsing Kant’s antithesis. The closing question about timeless persons and the audience answer describing an empirical track-record objection are relevant qualifications. Do not substitute an unqualified rejection of all reasoning for this position.

Pyle calls loop quantum cosmology speculative and says he does not know whether it is true. Its role is a potential naturalistic and testable alternative, not an established fact. His fine-tuning objection distinguishes local sensitivity of constants from the probability across the full possibility space. Craig replies with the fly analogy, independent constraints, and the distinction between a deductive philosophical argument and a scientific prediction. Preserve what each actually argues; do not import outside cosmology or assume that naming a scholar provides missing premises.

On morality Pyle separates truth-aptness, truth and truth-makers and cites three secular ethical approaches, then attacks arbitrary divine commands. Craig explicitly rejects voluntarism and grounds commands in a necessarily loving divine nature. Do not describe his reply as arbitrary commands. His audience replies invoke moral experience and distinguish human status from an objective standard. Do not credit Pyle with Laura’s separate follow-up objection or penalize Pyle for not answering a question addressed only to Craig.

On resurrection Pyle accepts Jesus and likely crucifixion, suspends judgment on the empty tomb, and distinguishes an empty tomb from a resurrection. His fourfold alternatives challenge and later ghost-story analogy are not a claimed historical reconstruction of what happened. He later offers a tentative myth-development interpretation involving scripture and the Emmaus narrative, with explicit limited expertise. Craig appeals to historical criteria, several evidential facts jointly, Jewish expectations and the religious context; his case is not just an empty-tomb inference. His admission of unfamiliarity with the Ganesh reports qualifies his comparison. Do not confuse Pyle’s historical possibility with a proven explanation or misstate his track-record case as assigning miracles zero probability. His closing explicitly rejects zero probability, and his audience answer supplies hypothetical evidence that would convince him.

On experience Craig grants in audience answers that other religions can involve genuine experiences, while retaining personal entitlement absent a defeater. Pyle asks for a criterion to distinguish genuine experiences rather than merely denying that experiences occur. The later agnosticism/fideism exchange refines the use of those terms and does not mean either speaker equates full Christian belief with withholding all belief. Preserve chronology and actual opportunities to reply. A newly introduced closing objection is not automatically an earlier unanswered burden.
