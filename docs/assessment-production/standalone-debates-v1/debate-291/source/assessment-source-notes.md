## Burden fidelity

Motion is exactly “Does God exist?” Rauser defines a necessarily existent nonphysical agent who is omniscient, omnipotent and perfectly good. He argues theistic belief CAN be rational without inferential evidence; he does not say it is always properly basic or immune to counterevidence. He distinguishes this personal warrant from God’s legitimacy as an explanatory hypothesis and from a positive inference explaining moral intuition. His third argument concerns access to moral truth (moral epistemology), not the existence or source of moral values (moral ontology). Undeveloped arguments merely listed near the end of his opening cannot receive credit as an established cumulative case.

Schieber expressly defends metaphysical naturalism, not mere absence of belief. His case compares simplicity and evidential fit with classical theism. He explicitly says the observations do not conclusively disprove God. Preserve nonresistant unbelief and a conscious reciprocal relationship; hostility in conjunction with cosmic scale, not size alone; and apparently/seemingly pointless suffering while allowing some justified suffering. Unknown-reasons symmetry is an objection to an unsupported asymmetry of explanation, not proof that God has no reasons.

## Replies and qualifications

Rauser accepts that some unbelievers are nonresistant. His infant and adult anonymous-Christian examples claim meaningful relationship without correct propositional belief; his later aid-worker example concerns justice and mercy as implicit relationship. Schieber grants the theological tradition but asks why loving God would withhold the additional benefits of conscious reciprocal relationship. Schieber calls the proposed relation thin/trivial, explicitly not the aid work morally trivial. Rauser’s temporary-distance illustration is a possibility, not evidence that all unbelievers resist.

Rauser’s architect analogy invokes grandeur and intimacy and challenges an anthropocentric premise; Schieber replies that grandeur alone does not address hostility and clarifies that maximal hospitality is not required. Rauser’s master/apprentice and hospital analogies appeal to expected limits of finite knowledge; preserve that reason, then assess whether it supports the relevant probability claim. Schieber’s unknown-reasons answer must be read alongside those actual replies.

Schieber’s fairy analogy targets a permissive justification method; he explicitly denies simply equating God with fairies. Universal sanction means nearly all thinkers under normal conditions, not strict unanimity. His criteria are offered as plausible and tentative. Rauser challenges methodological starting points with particularism and psychopathy; Schieber replies about normal functioning and concedes another criterion might be better.

Schieber concedes God is a legitimate explanatory hypothesis, not that God exists. In the exchange he distinguishes conditional rationality and updating from granting proper basicality. On moral intuition he allows prima facie justification without a known mechanism, acknowledges ignorance of the mechanism and that the specific datum might support theism, without conceding the whole comparative case. Rauser answers moral disagreement in his closing with basic cross-cultural consensus plus self-justification; retain this reply rather than infer silence. Rauser’s forgetting to restate an argument is not itself an inferential defect.
