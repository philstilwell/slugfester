## Executive summary

The archive supports a narrower and more defensible version of the slogan hypothesis: **the religious side more often combines weak public support, overstated confidence, and changing or missing limits**. In the 146 debates that retain the necessary detailed findings, 398 of 1,393 theist moves meet all three conditions, compared with 111 of 1,407 non-theist moves. That is **28.6% versus 7.9%**, a 3.62-fold relative rate.

When each debate receives equal weight, the average difference is **20.65 percentage points**, with a 95% range from repeated draws of debates of 16.28 to 25.01. The pattern is not produced by John Lennox alone: removing his eligible debates leaves a 21.05-point difference. His contributions nevertheless provide unusually clear examples for close reading, including affirmations of creator-given worth, postmortem compensation, and miracle claims protected by a diagnosis of the critic's prior assumptions.

The main limit belongs beside the headline. **The three-part test does not directly count unfalsifiable slogans.** Some flagged moves are poorly supported arguments that could be improved or tested. Some unflagged statements may still be protected from correction. We have counted warning signs; we have not independently checked how often a warning identifies a genuine slogan.

The archive now contains 253 assessments, but the original test cannot honestly be expanded to all of them: newer scoring records do not retain the same finding fields. A separate, explicitly different score-based check covers all 187 comparable religious-versus-skeptical debates. It finds a 23.62-percentage-point difference in the same direction. The later-process subset alone shows a 21.39-point difference. That strengthens the broader concern about weak support and overclaim without pretending the two tests are interchangeable.

Phil Stilwell's hypothesis also proposes a cause: that religion is often sustained through emotional affirmation rather than rigorous public argument. The observed pattern is consistent with that explanation. **It does not establish how religious communities enforce belief, why individuals believe, or what emotional effect a particular statement had on its audience.** Those are additional questions requiring different evidence.

The conclusion is clear but limited: these warning signs are much more common on the recorded theist side, and the examples show how claims can be protected from criticism. The response should be the same for both sides: give reasons for the claim, explain what could count against those reasons, and be willing to reconsider.

## 1. Why slogans are a reasoning problem

A slogan is not weak merely because it is brief. “Correlation is not causation” is concise and useful, although applying it correctly requires explanation. Nor is a claim weak merely because it moves an audience. A compelling account of suffering can be emotionally powerful and well supported. The problem begins when the persuasive force of a statement substitutes for the work needed to establish it.

An affirmation can make a conclusion feel settled without showing why an unconvinced listener should accept it. It may unite a group, reassure a person, express a commitment, or frame an experience. Those functions can be legitimate. They become problematic when the statement is offered as public evidence for the very worldview that gives the affirmation its authority.

The hypothesis predicts that this happens more often on the theist side. The proposed explanation is that trust, personal religious experiences, inherited authority, and comfort can sustain faith without giving an outsider enough reason to share it. A speaker may then bring those same standards into debate. The question is not whether religious speech contains emotion. It is whether the speaker has supplied a reason to move from “I believe this” to “you should believe this too.”

The same issue arises outside religion. “Science will eventually explain everything,” “markets always know best,” or “everyone who disagrees is protecting privilege” can be used as correction-resistant affirmations. These illustrative formulations are not quotations from the archive. They show why the test must target a reasoning pattern rather than a favored set of beliefs.

## 2. Four concepts that should not be collapsed

The earlier paper's theme becomes clearer when four judgments are separated. A claim can be rhetorical without being false, not backed up without being unfalsifiable, or non-empirical without being irrational. Conflating those categories would turn a critique of sloppy reasoning into an instance of it.

| Concept | What it means here |
| --- | --- |
| Rhetorically forceful | The presentation encourages confidence, identification, or emotional commitment. This can accompany good or poor reasoning. |
| Weakly substantiated | The contribution does not supply enough support for the strength of its conclusion. Additional evidence or a better argument might repair it. |
| Slogan-like | A compact or repeatable affirmation does work that its supporting reasoning has not earned. The description concerns its use, not simply its length. |
| Insulated from correction | No clear result is allowed to count against the claim, or adverse results are routinely redescribed as confirmation. |

Literal empirical falsifiability asks whether an observation could conflict with a claim. It is important in scientific testing, but it is not a universal test of meaningful thought. Mathematical arguments are assessed through proof; moral claims through reasons, implications, and consistency; metaphysical proposals through consistent reasoning, explanatory comparison, and their relationship to experience. They need not all operate like laboratory hypotheses.

The broader standard relevant to this paper is **answerability to reasons**. Does the speaker identify a burden that could remain unmet? Would a failed starting claim, a stronger alternative, a counterexample, or an unexpected observation lower confidence? A claim need not be refutable by a single experiment to be exposed to rational correction.

> **The right question is not simply “Can you falsify this?”** Ask: “What would count against it, and why?” The answer may involve an observation, a logical objection, a historical source, or a competing explanation. What matters is whether the proposed support can genuinely succeed or fail.

## 3. The original three-part test

The earlier assessment process records detailed judgments about support, the strength asserted, and the stability of limits or conditions. The test uses those existing fields. A move is flagged only when it satisfies all three conditions below.

- Its support score is below 70.
- Its asserted force is judged materially or radically overstated relative to the support supplied.
- Its terms or scope are unstable, or an important limit or condition is missing or materially misleading.

The combination matters. Weak support alone does not make a slogan. A compressed statement alone does not make one either. The test aims to identify the more concerning pattern in which a contribution carries limited support but presents a stronger or less qualified conclusion than that support earns.

No new reader has independently coded all of these passages for this edition. The calculation applies a transparent rule to recorded assessment judgments. That makes it reproducible, but it also means the result inherits the assessments' interpretations. A model that systematically overreads confidence on one side could inflate the measured difference.

The eligible set remains **146 debates and 2,800 moves**. The full archive's growth to 253 does not create missing fields in later records. The unchanged total being counted is an important result of the data audit, not a failure to update the statistics. The original quantities were recalculated; their unchanged values reflect an unchanged eligible measurement set.

## 4. The measured difference is substantial

Weak support appears in 57.2% of theist moves and 19.7% of non-theist moves. Material overclaim appears in 36.3% and 10.0%, respectively. Compressed wording or unclear limits appear in 53.4% and 29.9%. The combined pattern is narrower: **398 theist moves and 111 non-theist moves**, or 28.6% and 7.9% of their respective totals.

![The bars show combined move rates in the 146-debate eligible set. The final row requires all three conditions together. The separate components overlap and must not be added to produce a total.](p3-components)

There are two useful ways to describe the combined difference. Subtracting the combined percentages gives about **20.7 percentage points**. Dividing them gives a **3.62-fold relative rate**. The first tells us the absolute difference in rate; the second tells us the multiplier relative to the smaller baseline. Reporting only the multiplier can make a result difficult to evaluate without knowing the baseline.

The primary paired analysis avoids letting longer assessments dominate. Within each debate, we calculate each side's flagged share and subtract the non-theist share from the theist share. Averaging these 146 differences gives **20.65 percentage points**. The direction favors a higher theist rate in 103 debates, ties in 26, and reverses in 17. Thus the combined result is not just the product of one or two unusually large records.

The 95% range from repeated draws, 16.28 to 25.01 points, describes sensitivity to reselecting observed debates. It does not estimate how many flags are genuine slogans, and it cannot eliminate systematic judging error. The distinction between a precisely reproducible measurement and a valid interpretation of that measurement remains essential.

## 5. Does the pattern survive narrower checks?

The narrower checks ask whether the pattern is confined to opening claims, peripheral statements, a particular role orientation, or Lennox's contributions. They preserve the same three-part rule while changing which recorded moves enter the comparison. The changing totals being counted are part of the result, not a technical detail to conceal.

![The same three-part rule is applied to narrower selections. The theist-CON row has only 15 debates and an interval crossing zero; it is not conclusive evidence that role orientation is irrelevant.](p3-checks)

The paired difference is 27.20 percentage points for constructive moves across 78 eligible debates, 17.47 for replies across 145, and 22.09 for high-importance moves across 146. Each comparison requires both sides to have the relevant kind of move. These are related but not identical populations, so their differences should not be treated as a causal effect of making a reply rather than an opening claim.

Removing Lennox leaves 140 debates and a 21.05-point gap. His examples are therefore illustrative, not the numerical foundation of the finding. In his six eligible debates, **15 of 56 theist-side moves** meet the rule. Those counts concern particular assessed contributions. They are not a judgment about every statement in his work or his personal intentions.

The theist-CON check is weaker. In 15 eligible debates where the religious side occupies CON, the average difference is 11.00 points, but the interval runs from about −4.65 to 25.47. The data are compatible with a positive pattern but are too limited and variable to establish it securely in that subgroup. This is a meaningful restraint on the conclusion: the argument should not claim to have ruled out burden or role effects completely.

## 6. What the new assessments can—and cannot—add

Later records contain final scoring area scores but not the earlier rule's separate judgments of asserted force and limit or condition. Reconstructing those missing judgments from the final scores would falsely suggest that the same measurement had been performed. Instead, this edition introduces a separate descriptive check.

The new check flags a move when support is below 70, clear wording below 80, and care and fairness below 80. This is a **score-based combination**, not a translation of the original rule. The last scoring area combines confidence with fair representation, so a low score can reflect uncharitable treatment even when the confidence component is not the main problem.

![The score-based combination shows a higher theist rate in both assessment groups. It supports continuity of the broader low-support pattern, but it does not convert the newer records into a literal slogan census.](p3-proxy)

Across the 187 relevant debates, the average within-debate flagged rates are 36.7% for theist moves and 13.1% for non-theist moves: a **23.62-percentage-point paired gap**. The earlier-process gap is 24.25 points, and the later-process gap is 21.39. The later interval is approximately 12.00 to 30.66 points across 41 debates.

The two tests overlap conceptually and rely on the same underlying scoring framework. They are not independent experiments whose evidence can simply be multiplied. Their value is more modest: the broader pattern of low support coupled with other presentation weaknesses is not confined to the oldest records. The precise rate of correction-resistant slogans remains unmeasured.

## 7. Lennox example one: infinite worth through creation

In debate 170, John Lennox grounds human meaning and worth in divine intention and image-bearing. A short source phrase captures the move: **“infinite value because it bears the image of its creator.”** The assessment gives the move an support score of 61. See [Lennox–Singer, 93:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HoTILnpd3q8&t=5600s).

The affirmation performs a clear emotional and moral function. It tells a person that their existence is wanted and their worth is not reducible to productivity, ability, or social approval. Recognizing that appeal is not a refutation of the claim. Nor should a reader infer that the statement is insincere or manipulative merely because it reassures.

The reasoning difficulty is that the explanation assumes a disputed creator and leaves “infinite value” insufficiently specified. What establishes divine intention? How does creation confer the relevant moral status? Why would a secular account of equal standing or finite purpose fail? Without those steps, the statement offers a compelling description of life *within Christian belief* more readily than a public reason to adopt that belief.

This is an example of **unfalsifiable slogan proffering in its protective use**: every person's existence can be described as compatible with being wanted by God, while no contrary condition is specified in the move. That does not show that human worth is false or that moral value must be empirically measurable. The issue is whether creator-given worth is being offered as an established explanation without identifying what could undermine it.

A stronger version would separate the moral commitment from its proposed grounding. It could defend equal worth through explicit moral reasoning, then explain why divine intention adds a necessary or superior foundation. Alternatively, it could present the statement honestly as a faith-based affirmation rather than as evidence that should settle a public debate.

## 8. Lennox example two: compensation after death

In the same debate, Lennox appeals to divine solidarity and postmortem restoration. The source passage concludes that **“God can compensate.”** The assessed contribution receives an support score of 64. See [Lennox–Singer, 83:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HoTILnpd3q8&t=4980s).

The humane aspiration is clear: a victim's suffering need not be the final fact about that person's life. Christianity's account of Christ entering suffering and of an eventual life without pain offers a response to abandonment and despair. A careful critic should acknowledge that this is more than a random change of subject.

Yet three claims need separation. Compensation may improve a victim's eventual condition. It may outweigh an earlier harm. It may or may not justify permitting the harm in the first place. Showing one does not automatically establish the others. In ordinary moral reasoning, a later benefit does not invariably authorize an earlier avoidable injury.

The correction problem arises if any amount of suffering can be absorbed by an unspecified future compensation. More suffering can always be met with more unseen restoration. Without independent support for that restoration and an account of permissible divine action, the proposal risks becoming an answer that no observed pattern can weaken.

This is not an empirical disproof of an afterlife. It is a diagnosis of why the particular reply can become insulated from the evidence it is supposed to address. A stronger defense would explain what the afterlife claim rests on, distinguish compensation from justification, and identify whether some pattern of suffering would still count against the defended conception of God.

## 9. Lennox example three: miracles and the critic's assumptions

In debate 169, Lennox describes miracles as exceptional divine acts within an ordinarily regular universe. He says **“science has nothing to say about singularities”** and connects categorical rejection of the supernatural to prior exclusion. The recorded support score is 54. See [Lennox–Krauss, 24:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yotpTOKLZ00&t=1474s).

There is a legitimate point inside this move. A critic who rules out supernatural action by definition has not independently shown that a particular miracle report is false. Likewise, an exception to an otherwise regular pattern is not simply the same claim as a world without any regularities. Those distinctions deserve engagement.

The problem is the stronger protective use. A miracle claim needs a way to distinguish divine action from error, legend, coincidence, or an unknown natural cause. Identifying a possible prior bias in the critic does not supply that discrimination. And the uniqueness of an event does not prevent reasoning from traces and evidence about it; much historical inquiry concerns singular events.

If nonacceptance is routinely explained as prior naturalism, the critic's objection can be converted from a challenge to the evidence into evidence of the critic's closed worldview. That is the slogan-like escape route. It is not established merely by pointing out that philosophical assumptions exist. The fair test is whether the speaker also answers the actual evidence-based reasons for nonacceptance.

## 10. Other examples: private confirmation and protected motives

Ray Comfort provides two distinct cases in debate 142. At [95:13](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cRCbvfyiu0s&t=5713s), private manifestation of the risen Christ, promised after repentance and trust, is offered as support for resurrection. The support rating is 39. A participatory test is not automatically worthless: some experiences require a person to undertake a practice. But the test needs intelligible success and failure conditions.

What counts as manifestation? How is it distinguished from expectation or a conflicting religious experience? What follows if a sincere seeker undertakes the practice and does not receive confirmation? If failure can always be assigned to insufficient sincerity, the procedure protects the claim from precisely the result that ought to test it.

At [25:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cRCbvfyiu0s&t=1540s), Comfort explains rejection of God through avoidance of moral accountability, comparing it with criminals' attitude toward law enforcement. The support score is 30. The assessment's concern is not that avoidance of responsibility never occurs. It is the general attribution of motive without an independent way to distinguish it from sincere disagreement.

The danger is a self-sealing interpretation: agreement confirms the religious diagnosis, and disagreement confirms it too by revealing resistance. A psychological claim could in principle be investigated. In the reasoning use at issue, however, the speaker has not identified what evidence would show that the diagnosis is wrong about a particular critic.

These examples show why “unfalsifiable” should describe the **way the claim is protected in the exchange**, not function as a casual insult meaning “I do not believe it.” The relevant question is whether the explanation permits the challenge to make any rational difference.

## 11. The same standard catches secular overclaim

Peter Atkins's categorical treatment of scientific practice and religious belief in debate 85 supplies a non-theist counterexample. The assessment records an support score of 40 for a move treating religion as superstition or wishful thinking and asserting intellectual incompatibility. The broader [Atkins–Fox scorecard](https://slugfester.com/debate/atkins-fox-god-science-future-humanity-2024/) gives the theist side a 15-point advantage.

A claim of incompatibility must specify what is incompatible with what. Particular doctrines may conflict with particular findings; that does not establish that every religious interpretation conflicts with scientific reasoning in the same way. If religious scientists are dismissed as insufficiently thoughtful whenever they appear as counterexamples, the generalization is being protected rather than properly tested.

Another recorded non-theist move invokes an unrestricted space of possible worlds to make a godless multiverse overwhelmingly probable. The problem is not that multiverse proposals are automatically irrational. It is that a probability claim requires a justified way of assigning weight to the possibilities. Merely saying there are enormously many possibilities does not supply that weighting.

An easy mathematical illustration helps. There are infinitely many positive integers and infinitely many even positive integers. Counting each set as “infinite” does not establish that a fair lottery over all integers exists, or tell us how to draw from it. A claim about the probability of whole universes requires more care, not less. This is a methodological criticism that applies equally to unsupported fine-tuning probabilities on the religious side.

## 12. Not every theist argument fits the pattern

The archive also supplies strong theist-side counterexamples. In debate 137, William Lane Craig argues for the authenticity of a passage in First Corinthians using manuscripts, early citation, vocabulary, context, and the effect of removing the passage. Its support score is 88. Those considerations can be examined and challenged. Whether the larger resurrection case succeeds is separate from the quality of that bounded historical move. See [Craig–Price](https://slugfester.com/debate/price-craig-resurrection-historical-analogy-1999/).

In debate 139, Matthew Adelstein explains that hypotheses should be compared using both their initial plausibility and how strongly they lead us to expect the evidence. The move receives an support score of 87. In debate 144, Plantinga carefully distinguishes establishing compatibility from establishing that divine guidance actually occurred; that move receives an overall score of 92.

These cases matter for the hypothesis. If the proposed mechanism were simply “religion makes rational argument impossible,” well-supported religious-side moves would be inconvenient exceptions. A more precise hypothesis predicts variation: religious arguments should improve when they supply independent support, expose assumptions, compare alternatives, and limit their conclusions. The counterexamples fit that more useful formulation.

The newer Sechler–Clifton debate provides another kind of counterweight. The assessment credits Sechler for revising or narrowing inherited positions about salvation and hell rather than defending every traditional claim unchanged. His side still scores lower overall. Concession and flexibility can be strengths without guaranteeing victory; a lower score should not erase evidence that a speaker is responsive to reasons. See [debate 253](https://slugfester.com/debate/clifton-sechler-divine-hiddenness-hell-2022/).

## 13. The causal hypothesis remains open

The proposed explanation is that emotional affirmation helps sustain religious belief and that this habit can carry into argument. The close readings show how an affirmation can offer reassurance, belonging, or protection from uncertainty. The statistical analysis shows that a related low-support pattern is more common on the religious side. These are compatible observations, but there is a gap between them.

The archive has no systematic measures of audience emotion, institutional pressure, childhood instruction, identity threat, private belief formation, or sincerity. It therefore cannot establish that theism is *largely enforced* through emotional devices. The debate transcripts are not a representative sample of religious life. Even an unmistakably reassuring statement might have been offered sincerely as a reason rather than deliberately as an emotional substitute.

Other explanations remain plausible: more ambitious religious claims, asymmetrical burdens, conventions of public apologetics, unequal speaker skill, or a judging framework more comfortable with skeptical caution. The measured combination itself partly overlaps with the scoring areas used to assign scores, so its association with low scores is not independent evidence for a psychological cause.

A stronger follow-up would randomly select passages, hide speaker and side identities where feasible, and have separate teams code emotional appeals, correction conditions, and adequacy of support. The teams should not see each other's judgments or the public scores. Their disagreements would be published. Only then could the analysis ask whether emotional framing predicts missing support beyond topic, role, and conclusion strength.

## 14. Conclusion: an affirmation is not its own evidence

### Argument one: the measured warning signs are more common on one side

**1.** A move that combines weak support, overstated confidence, and missing or changing limits gives a listener reason to question whether the conclusion has been earned.

**2.** All three signs occur in **398 of 1,393 theist moves (28.6%)**, compared with **111 of 1,407 non-theist moves (7.9%)**. Removing John Lennox's debates does not remove the difference: the average within-debate gap remains **21.05 percentage points**.

**3.** A different check using the available scores in all 187 relevant debates finds a difference in the same direction. It supports the broader concern, although it is not the same measurement.

**Therefore:** this archive provides clear evidence that the recorded theist-side arguments more often combine these three weaknesses. It does not establish that every flagged move is an unfalsifiable slogan.

### Argument two: a claim protected from every objection has not thereby gained support

**1.** If every possible objection can be dismissed without changing a claim, surviving those dismissals gives us little reason to believe the claim is true.

**2.** The close readings show ways this can happen: future compensation absorbs suffering without a stated limit; the critic's supposed prejudice explains rejection of a miracle; a failure to receive confirmation is blamed on the seeker. Lennox's examples make several of these patterns concrete.

**3.** The examples explain possible uses of the statements. They do not measure every speaker's motives, every community's methods of sustaining belief, or the frequency of literally unfalsifiable claims across all transcripts.

**Therefore:** the proposed link between emotional affirmation and weak public support is worth testing, but its social and psychological cause is not proved here. A direct, independently checked count of slogan use would be needed to establish the stronger claim.

**The test for either side is the same: what would count against this reason, and would you actually reconsider if it occurred?** A statement can help people feel certain without giving an unconvinced listener enough reason to agree. The paper's own hypothesis must remain open to that same test.

## Methods and sources

The source revision is **76d006b37**, frozen September 4, 2026. The archive has 253 assessments; the broad position comparison has 187 debates and 4,086 moves. The original detailed-findings rule applies to 146 earlier-process debates, comprising 1,393 theist and 1,407 non-theist moves. The separate final-score combination applies to all 187 comparisons, including 41 later-process debates. The two eligible sets must not be combined as though the measurements were identical.

Combined percentages divide flagged moves by all eligible moves on the same side actually defended. Paired percentage-point differences first subtract within each debate and then average across debates. Intervals use 20,000 complete-debate replacement resamples, seed 20260904. They reflect variation in observed debate composition, not measurement validity, transcript accuracy, all repeated-speaker dependence, or the uncertainty of the causal hypothesis.

The Lennox, Comfort, Atkins, Craig, Adelstein, and Plantinga examples are traceable to locked source excerpts and public scorecards. Short quotations above are quotations; the surrounding case descriptions are paraphrases and interpretations. Timestamp links point to the relevant recorded contribution, although caption timing and the boundaries of an exchange may be approximate. The examples were selected to explain mechanisms and counterexamples, not to estimate their rate.
