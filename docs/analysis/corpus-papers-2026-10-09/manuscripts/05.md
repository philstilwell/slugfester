Among {{p5.untagged_locked.n}} comparable losses with no named-fallacy tag, {{p5.untagged_locked.five_or_six}} trail in five or six scoring areas. {{p5.winner_with_fallacy}} higher-scoring sides carry at least one fallacy tag. ## 1. Count the right thing

This study uses all {{counts.published}} public assessments for annotation counts. It excludes {{p5.ties}} tied results when identifying a lower-scoring side, leaving {{p5.decisive}} decisive assessments. “Loss” is shorthand for the lower published score; it is not a declaration that a worldview is false.

A named-fallacy tag is an accepted public annotation on an assessed move. It is not every weakness described in a critique, every bias tag, or every sentence in a transcript. A move with several fallacy labels counts once when asking whether a side has any label, but contributes several instances to a label-frequency inventory.

The catalogue has {{counts.unique_video_urls}} unique video links rather than {{counts.published}} independent recordings. Assessments 13 and 125 share a source under different formats. They remain separate observations in this assessment-level annotation inventory; the comparable one-on-one subset contains only one of them. The full-archive rate should not be presented as the percentage of independent videos with an error-free losing side.

## 2. The pooled majority is a mixture, not a law

![The earlier and later groups give very different answers to the same label-count question.](p5-cohorts)

The current combined rate is {{derived.loss_no_fallacy_pct:1}}%, compared with 61.7% in the previous edition. The later-process rate is {{derived.later_no_fallacy_pct:1}}%, compared with 14.5% previously. The earlier group's {{derived.earlier_no_fallacy_pct:1}}% is unchanged. Thus the overall rate can fall even while the later group's own rate rises: the later group now occupies more of the archive.

For a simple illustration unrelated to the real counts, a catalogue with many records from a process that seldom tags fallacies will have a high untagged rate. Adding records from a process that tags them more often can lower the overall rate without any change to the old judgments. This is why the process-specific rows are essential, not optional caveats below a dramatic headline.

The records alone cannot tell us whether later arguments contain more qualifying errors, later reviewers recognize more of the same errors, acceptance rules differ, or several factors contribute. Describing the contrast as annotation drift is shorthand for a change in recorded practice, not proof of its cause.

## 3. No label can coexist with a broadly weak performance

Of the {{p5.untagged_locked.n}} comparable untagged losses, {{p5.untagged_locked.five_or_six}}—{{derived.untagged_five_six_pct:1}}%—trail in at least five of the six weighted scoring areas. Some arguments fall short through cumulative weaknesses: incomplete support, an unanswered objection, an unclear scope, or confidence stronger than the supplied reasons justify.

![The number of areas behind the opponent is a broader description than a count of named fallacies.](p5-dimensions)

These dimensions are not independent discoveries. One unsupported inference can depress several related ratings, and the dimensions themselves help define the overall score. The result nevertheless explains why an absence of a named label cannot be treated as a contradiction in a low score. The rubric rewards positive argumentative work as well as avoiding recognizable mistakes.

Imagine a speaker who stays on topic, never equivocates, and never explicitly appeals to an irrelevant authority, but supplies little evidence for the central claim and never answers a specific counterexample. That illustrative performance can reasonably score below a well-supported answer without a dramatic named fallacy. The appropriate scrutiny is whether the critique identifies the missing work accurately, not whether it can attach a familiar Latin label.

## 4. A labeled error does not erase everything else

There are {{p5.winner_with_fallacy}} decisive assessments in which the higher-scoring side has a fallacy tag. That does not excuse the tagged move. It means the final score incorporates the rest of the performance and the importance of the error in context.

Readers should ask what proposition the mistake affects, whether the speaker later repairs it, and whether the central conclusion depends on it. A local digression and a circular premise supporting the main case need not have equal consequences. The site's move importance and section weights attempt to capture differences of argumentative role, but those judgments remain open to challenge.

Conversely, a high total does not validate every argument uttered by the higher-scoring speaker. The most useful reading is local: identify the particular reason, inference, objection, and response. A scorecard is more informative when it directs attention back to those elements than when it becomes a substitute for them.

## 5. The inventory is narrow and inspectable

The snapshot records {{derived.public_label_instances}} named-fallacy label instances on {{p5.tagged_moves}} assessed moves. Six labels occur: Argument from ignorance, Appeal to authority, Equivocation, Begging the question, Special pleading, and Red herring. Their relative frequencies reflect what was assessed and accepted under the site's process, not the prevalence of every reasoning error in public discourse.

![Label instances are not unique moves, and their frequency is not the same as their impact on a conclusion.](p5-labels)

Multiple tags can share a move. The denominator for a rate per assessed move is also different from a rate per spoken word or minute. The catalogue selects and scores argumentative moves rather than transcribing every sentence into a separate score unit. Comparisons with the direct slogan study therefore require attention to both the event definition and the denominator.

## 6. What should readers and reviewers do?

**Reason 1:** untagged lower scores remain common but are strongly dependent on assessment process. **Reason 2:** many untagged losses trail on several broader dimensions. **Reason 3:** many higher-scoring sides have a tag, so presence alone does not determine the aggregate result.

**Therefore:** “Beyond the Fallacy Count” remains a better title than a universal claim that debates are usually lost without a fallacy. The stable lesson concerns the limits of the count, not a majority percentage that moves with the catalogue.

A useful correction report should identify a concrete passage, explain the proposed error or missing justification, and state how it affects the particular assessment. Reviewers should separately check the presence of the named error, its importance, and any downstream numerical effect. Counting more labels is not automatically stricter or fairer judging.

**Therefore:** read tags as pointers into an argument, not verdicts detached from it. The best future audit would apply consistent acceptance rules to a shared set of passages across both processes and publish disagreements. That would address the source of the large process contrast more directly than another pooled total.
