## Executive summary

**Yes: Slugfester rankings capture a strong, repeatable pattern of assessed performance within this catalogue.** Different subsets of a speaker's recorded debates reproduce much of the same broad ordering. The positive finding is substantial stability, not merely the usefulness of a leaderboard for navigation.

The strongest direct evidence comes from {{p7.split_half.n}} people with at least six comparable appearances. Across 3,000 random splits of each person's record into two halves, median ranking agreement is {{p7.split_half.median:2}}, with the middle 95% of results between {{p7.split_half.interval.0:2}} and {{p7.split_half.interval.1:2}}. Dividing records by assessment-number order gives agreement of {{p7.split_half.sequence_split_spearman:2}}. These are strong correlations: 1 means identical ordering, not 100% forecast accuracy.

The full ranked field contains {{p7.ranked_speakers}} people and {{p7.eligible_appearances}} appearances, up from 50 and 334 in September. Adjusting for average score differences between assessment processes leaves this field's ordering almost unchanged: agreement is {{p7.centered_rank_spearman:2}}. The broad pattern is therefore robust to that particular process-level difference.

**Broad stability and exact-rank precision are different questions.** Neighboring averages are typically only {{p7.median_adjacent_gap:2}} points apart, so their places can exchange even while the overall ordering remains strong. The evidence supports broad comparisons of assessed performance, especially for well-represented speakers; it does not establish every adjacent place, impartial judging, or performance on unseen debates. Those boundaries qualify the conclusion without reversing it.

## 1. Define the field before interpreting its order

The comparable one-on-one records contain {{p7.appearances}} appearances by {{p7.all_speakers}} people. Only {{p7.ranked_speakers}} meet the paper's three-appearance minimum. Team scores and other non-comparable formats are not credited as if each participant individually earned the team result.

The field is fixed to the October 9 source snapshot. This paper is not a live leaderboard: new assessments may subsequently change the website. Average scores use every eligible appearance in the research set, and displayed places sort by average, then appearance count and name for deterministic ties. The uncertainty calculations use average ranks for tied means, avoiding a claim that alphabetical order carries evidence.

![The minimum appearance rule excludes many people because evidence is sparse, not because their scores are low.](p7-samples)

A three-appearance threshold is a practical publication rule, not a guarantee of stability. Three similar scores might reflect a limited range of topics and opponents. A person with many appearances has a broader observed record, but those appearances are not randomized tests of equal difficulty.

## 2. Why a repeatable broad order can have uncertain places

Imagine, solely as a teaching example, two lists A–B–C–D–E and A–C–B–D–E. Most of the order agrees even though B and C exchange exact places. A measure called Spearman rank correlation compares the ordering of all entries: +1 means identical order, −1 reversed order, and values near zero little overall ordering agreement. The example has agreement +0.90, not “90% of people kept their places.”

For the actual archive, {{p7.split_half.n}} people have at least six eligible appearances. In each of 3,000 calculations, each person's scores are randomly divided into two nearly equal groups and averaged. The resulting rank correlations have a median of {{p7.split_half.median:2}} and a middle-95% range of {{p7.split_half.interval.0:2}}–{{p7.split_half.interval.1:2}}. Splitting by assessment-number order instead gives {{p7.split_half.sequence_split_spearman:2}}. Assessment order is not necessarily recording chronology.

The direct conclusion is strong repeatability across subsets of the assessed record. The middle-95% range describes variation across splits, not a confidence interval for a wider population. Agreement remains high across those splits and under the separate assessment-number split; the broad pattern is not dependent on one particular partition of the record.

This is evidence about stability, not a separate validation of the judges. Recurring opponents and topics can contribute to repeatability, as can a consistent judging bias. Testing genuinely new debates would address forecast performance. These distinct questions do not erase the observed consistency; they set its scope.

## 3. Two rank ranges with different assumptions

**Resampled ranks** are obtained by drawing, with replacement, from each person's recorded scores, retaining their observed appearance count, then ranking the resulting means. Repeat this 20,000 times. A person with only a few nearly identical scores can get a deceptively narrow range because the procedure has no other performances to draw.

**Model ranks** use a simple normal model that estimates within-person performance variation across the ranked group and variation between people's underlying means. Small-sample averages are pulled somewhat toward the overall mean; the model then generates 20,000 possible sets of underlying means and ranks them. This permits variation not present in a tiny personal sample, but it adds assumptions about common variation and the model's shape.

Both ranges keep the middle 95% of calculated positions and hold the field fixed. The model conditions on its fitted variance estimates rather than accounting for every uncertainty in those estimates. Neither range is a probability of winning the next debate, and neither is a guarantee that the “true rank” lies inside the printed interval.

![The leading 15 observed averages have overlapping uncertainty ranges. Calculations still include the full eligible field.](p7-ranges)

Typical widths are {{p7.median_empirical_rank_width:0}} and {{p7.median_model_rank_width:0}} places, compared with 12 and 19 in September. A larger, more crowded field can have wider rank ranges despite repeatable broad ordering. Width means upper endpoint minus lower endpoint: a range of 2–9 has width seven, even though it includes eight integer places. Half-places arise from tied ranks and interpolation; they are not a hidden extra level of measurement precision.

## 4. Read real rows rather than a single headline

Joseph Schmid has six eligible appearances and an average of 87.33, first in this fixed research field. His resampled rank range is 1–5; the model range is 1–11. First place is a correct description of the observed mean, not a well-established separation from every nearby speaker.

Matt Dillahunty has 32 appearances and an average of 85.62 when rounded to two decimals. His resampled range is 3–11 and model range 2–11. The larger record supports a more constrained estimate than a small sample at a similar location, but it does not make the result independent of his particular opponents and topics.

Susan Blackmore has three appearances with little observed score variation. Her resampled range is 5–11.5, whereas the model allows 1–24. The disagreement is instructive: drawing only from three closely spaced scores can understate unobserved variation; borrowing a wider group's variation can overstate it if that person is genuinely more consistent. Neither procedure resolves that question with the evidence available.

These examples explain the methods, not personal psychological qualities. Religious identities and biographies play no role in this ranking calculation. The source records, not a person's present worldview, determine which assessed performances enter.

## 5. Check dependence, process differences, and sample size

The independent resampling calculation redraws each person's appearances separately, breaking some dependence between opponents who shared a debate. A sensitivity check instead assigns a common positive random weight to each debate for both participants. All people remain in the fixed field. The typical rank-range width is {{p7.median_paired_rank_width:0}} places under this shared-weight check, compared with {{p7.median_empirical_rank_width:0}} under independent drawing. It preserves more pairing but still generates no genuinely new performances.

The earlier and later assessment processes have different score levels. Subtracting each process's mean before ranking produces order agreement of {{p7.centered_rank_spearman:2}} with the observed table: the broad ordering is almost unchanged. This is strong evidence that the ranking pattern is not simply an artifact of the average level difference between the two processes. It does not test every process-related effect or leave every individual place unchanged. Process-centering is an exploratory sensitivity exercise, not an authorized adjustment to public scores.

The model estimates a within-person standard deviation of about {{p7.repeatability.within_sd:2}} score points and single-appearance repeatability of {{p7.repeatability.single:2}}. Here repeatability is the estimated share of score variation attributable to persistent differences between people rather than variation across their appearances. Under the model, averaging three appearances raises repeatability to {{p7.repeatability.means.3:2}}, and ten to {{p7.repeatability.means.10:2}}. This supports using multi-debate averages rather than judging a speaker from one performance. These are conditional projections for averages, not observed improvement in people as they debate more often; recurring contexts or judging effects can contribute to the persistent differences.

## 7. A stable broad ordering, not a precise hierarchy

**Reason 1:** different halves of the assessed record reproduce a strongly similar ordering, with median agreement {{p7.split_half.median:2}} and a middle-95% split range of {{p7.split_half.interval.0:2}}–{{p7.split_half.interval.1:2}}. **Reason 2:** a separate assessment-number split gives {{p7.split_half.sequence_split_spearman:2}}, while removing the mean difference between assessment processes preserves almost the same full-field ordering ({{p7.centered_rank_spearman:2}}). **Reason 3:** the repeatability model indicates that multi-debate averages contain a stronger persistent signal than individual performances. These are complementary checks on the same archive, not independent replications.

**Therefore:** the rankings provide strong evidence of stable differences in assessed performance within this catalogue. They support broad comparisons, especially among people with substantial records. Calling exact neighboring places uncertain should not be mistaken for calling the overall ranking pattern weak or arbitrary.

**Therefore:** present the positive stability result first, and use sample sizes, score distributions, and rank ranges to show how finely readers can distinguish people. Do not turn a one-place difference into a claim of demonstrated superiority. The observed repeatability is not proof of impartial judging or a context-free hierarchy of reasoning ability, and the calculations are not future-win probabilities. A future study using genuinely new debates and matched opponents would test whether this established within-catalogue stability extends to prediction.
