## What this new study asks

A sentence can be memorable without being a bad argument. A speaker may spend ten
minutes giving reasons, then sum them up in a single line. Calling that line a
slogan tells us little about the strength of the case. The important question is
whether the line summarizes the reasons or takes their place.

This paper tests two questions. First, how often do the speakers use a compact
affirmation to carry a claim they have not supported? Second, how often does that
affirmation also shut out objections in advance? These are different questions.
The second is narrower and needs its own evidence.

The original hypothesis predicted more such uses on the theist side. It also
proposed that emotional reinforcement helps explain the difference. The recordings
can give evidence about the words used. They cannot, by themselves, show what
caused a speaker's beliefs or how an audience was affected.

## Three questions to ask of a striking line

### 1. Does it act like a slogan?

Look for a short, repeatable affirmation that presents a general conclusion as
settled. A joke, vivid comparison, confident tone, or repeated phrase is not enough
on its own. The line must be doing some of the work of establishing the claim.

### 2. What reason is it replacing?

Check the actual exchange. Has the speaker already given reasons for this claim?
Does the next sentence supply them? Is the line only a summary, a limited
comparison, or a statement of personal belief? A reason can be disputed without
being absent. A weak ordinary argument is not automatically an unsupported slogan.

Only a line that meets both tests is counted here as an **unsupported slogan**:
it has slogan form and does work that a missing reason would need to do.

### 3. Can an objection make a difference?

A further problem appears when the speaker sets up the claim so that contrary
evidence cannot count against it. Perhaps every possible outcome is called a
success. Perhaps anyone who disagrees is said, for that very reason, to be
morally defective. Perhaps a promised test is given an escape clause for every
failure. The study looks for this protective move in the words themselves.

This is what the paper means by **protected from criticism**. It does not mean
merely that the speaker has failed to name a decisive laboratory experiment.
Claims about morality, meaning, or philosophy can be assessed through reasons,
consistency, consequences, and comparison with alternatives. They are not bad
arguments simply because a single physical test would not settle them.

## What was reviewed—and what was not

The source set is the 187 relevant religious-versus-skeptical debates in the
September 4, 2026 archive of 253 assessments. Every retained transcript was supplied
in full to a fresh AI reading. The review did not rely on whether the older
assessment happened to include a particular field or warning label.

The two sides retain the position labels used in the earlier papers. Some of
these conversations range beyond God's existence into politics, culture, or
meaning. A detected slogan in such a passage is still part of that side's
contribution, but it is not necessarily a religious claim. The narrower topic
check helps show how much those broader conversations matter. These are
comparisons between recorded debate sides, not personality tests of believers
and nonbelievers.

The source files contain 3,496,979 caption words across about 335 hours. The saved
files match the original source fingerprints. A fingerprint is a check that the
file has not changed; it does not prove that the captions are accurate. These are
retained transcripts, not a new listening check of every recording.

The reviewer was GPT-6 Astra at the Light setting. Each debate received one fresh
reading under the same instructions, without old scores, critiques, slogan tags,
or a statement of which side was expected to have more slogans. The two main
speakers were labeled A and B, with their order varied. Their names and speech
remained recognizable, so this was not an identity-blind test.

Five earlier two-reader pilot debates were excluded when the study changed to
the simpler method. They do not give some debates extra votes. The final method
does not claim two independent reviews of every debate.

## Why count uses, words, and debates?

A speaker who talks twice as much has more chances to use a slogan. Raw counts
therefore answer only part of the question. The main comparison divides each
side's detected uses by the words attributed to that side, then expresses the
result per 10,000 words. This is a unit for comparison, not a claim that every
speaker said exactly 10,000 words.

For a simple illustration, four uses in 20,000 words gives two uses per 10,000
words. Three uses in 10,000 words gives three. The first speaker has the larger
raw count but the second has the higher rate. Both facts are worth knowing.

The study compares the two rates within each debate, then averages those
differences. Each debate gets equal weight. It also reports the result obtained
by combining all the words and uses, which gives longer contributions more
weight. A separate count asks how many debates contain at least one use on each
side. That shows whether a difference is spread widely or comes from a few
especially repetitive exchanges.

An immediate repetition of the same claim in one uninterrupted argument counts
once. A later renewed use after a reply may count again. An editor's replay of
an earlier clip does not count as a new act by the speaker. Hosts, audience
questions, advertisements, introductions, and unclear speaker passages are not
treated as one of the two sides' substantive speech. The speakers' substantive
answers to audience questions are included.

## What the ranges mean

The analysis draws 187 debates from the reviewed set, allowing some to be drawn
more than once and others not at all. It keeps the two sides of each debate
together and calculates the average difference again. It repeats this 20,000
times. The reported range contains the middle 95% of those new averages.

In everyday terms, this asks how much the result moves when the mix of debates
changes. It does not ask whether the AI reader made the right judgment. A narrow
range can accompany a consistently mistaken measurement. Repeated appearances
by the same people also limit how far these ranges can be generalized. They are
checks on the observed collection, not a guarantee about all religious debate.

## Why this replaces the old warning-rule result

The earlier paper counted a combination of weak support, overstated confidence,
and changing or missing limits in existing assessments. That was a warning rule,
not a direct count of slogans. It could not cover all 187 debates in the same way
because later assessment records did not contain the same detailed findings.

The new study starts from the transcripts and applies one rule to the whole
relevant collection. Its counts are uses of particular claims, not percentages
of previously scored argumentative moves. The old percentages and the new rates
therefore must not be placed side by side as though they measure a rise or fall
in the same thing.
